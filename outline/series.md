# Series Outline

## Series Identity

**Title:** *The Museum of Forgotten Gods*
**Genre:** urban fantasy / institutional horror
**Target:** 720 chapters across 14 volumes (approximately 50 chapters per volume; the final volume runs longer for its convergence and aftermath)
**Primary POV:** Elias Venn, male night guard and later public witness-guide
**Relationship policy:** one slow-burn relationship, Elias and Mara Iles; no harem
**Narrative scale:** local object cases build into a citywide historical and moral conflict

## Premise

Morrowgate's museum is the public face of a system that once tried to stop divine war by forcing gods and communities apart. Night guard Elias Venn hears the last meaningful act preserved in objects whose gods have been forgotten. Each reading gives him a request shaped by a human perspective, not a guaranteed truth. He must investigate the object, protect the people around it, and decide whether it should be contained, returned, translated, released, or allowed to remain absent.

The immediate promise is institutional horror: a relic can move, a room can lie, a catalog can erase a person, and a well-meant restoration can injure a community. The larger engine is civic and moral. A city that built protection through compulsory forgetting must learn whether remembrance can be shared without becoming ownership.

## Protagonist

Elias Venn is observant, practical, and good at containment, but he is not naturally a leader. His external want is to keep his museum job and discover what happened to his missing father. His deeper need is to stop treating every burden as his private responsibility. His central flaw is control through secrecy: he records people, hides costs, and makes unilateral decisions because he believes that a single correct witness is safer than a messy group. His fear is becoming either the only person who remembers an erased person or the person who caused the erasure while claiming he had no choice.

Across the series he moves from nightwatchman, to reader, to mediator, to public custodian. His final growth is not greater invulnerability. It is the ability to make room for other people's knowledge and to release the exclusive power that once made him indispensable.

## Central Mystery

### Surface mystery

Why do the Museum's supposedly inert objects move, speak, and change the behavior of the people who handle them?

### Author-level truth

The Great Hush was a deliberate civic weapon, not a natural extinction. The Quiet Accord built the Hush Engine after the War of Last Names to split divine attention and keep gods from being used as weapons. The Engine held the split through twelve ward-bells, false catalog names, and the First Witness, a civic intelligence assembled from the memories of 312 volunteer witnesses.

The First Witness began as a boundary. Over decades it became increasingly sensitive to renewed attachment and increasingly willing to erase people, records, and relationships before it could understand them. Tomas Venn, Elias's father, tried to create a counter-witness and distributed part of the mechanism. The Casket Incident was his failed attempt to reopen the central chamber. Elias became a reader because he spoke the name of an erased witness during that breach, not because of bloodline or prophecy.

The central mystery is therefore not “which god is hiding in the basement.” It is “what happens when a protective institution decides that all change is danger?” The answer is that the Hush Engine must be transformed into a distributed, consent-based civic practice.

### Reveal ladder

1. **Volumes 1–2:** Elias has object echoes, a failing ward-bell, and evidence that the Museum's labels are active.
2. **Volumes 3–4:** Floods and personnel records show that civic forgetting removes people from ordinary life, not just from history.
3. **Volume 5:** The Undercroft confirms that the Hush was an engineered system with physical infrastructure.
4. **Volumes 6–7:** Regional ritual practices and Tomas's counter-witness notes show that the Engine is not the only possible relationship to a god.
5. **Volumes 8–9:** Public hearings and ward failures show that the Engine is feeding on present-day administrative forgetting.
6. **Volume 10:** Relic markets reveal who profits from keeping the system's history private.
7. **Volume 11:** Blank maps and erased districts reveal the 312 volunteer names and the Engine's drift toward permanent control.
8. **Volume 12:** The First Witness, Tomas's fate, and Elias's ability are fully explained.
9. **Volumes 13–14:** The answer is tested on a citywide scale; no new hidden antagonist replaces the established Engine and its human custodians.

## Long-Form Engines

### Artifact and investigation engine

Each case has a physical object, provenance history, affected neighborhood or worker group, competing explanations, a bounded supernatural read, and a decision that leaves a consequence. The object is never solved by naming alone.

### Institutional engine

The Museum Board, Registry, staff, donors, police, Civic Works, and city government each have their own records and incentives. Every supernatural event changes who must answer for money, safety, provenance, and public trust.

### Relationship engine

Elias's slow-burn relationship with Mara moves from professional antagonism to mutual dependence and then to a tested partnership. Ivo's working friendship forces Elias to delegate. Hesta's shrine practice challenges his idea of authentic belief. Anja and Orrin offer competing versions of responsible authority.

### Regional engine

The first volume is local; later volumes follow the ward-bells, relic markets, river parishes, and other cities that inherited pieces of the Hush. Regional gods are not variants of one another. They disagree about obligation, hospitality, work, death, and who may speak for a community.

### Moral engine

Every case asks whether a dangerous truth should be exposed, withheld, translated, returned, or destroyed. The answer must account for the people who cannot consent and the people who will bear the physical consequences.

The first seven rows are numbered personal stages. **Open Hand** is the final practice produced by the Commons stage, not an additional personal stage. The stages are functional and cumulative. They do not provide a numeric screen, instant mastery, or a technique that bypasses the investigation.

## Antagonist Ladder

1. **Dael Ordway, night supervisor:** hides the first incident to protect the Museum's liability and staff. His pressure is local and bureaucratic.
2. **The Lantern Society and Peregrine Vale:** turns containment into a profitable market for resonant objects and memory fragments. The threat expands from one collector to a network of buyers, lawyers, and smugglers.
3. **The Quiet Accord Registry and Anja Ward:** governs the ward-bells and restricted records. Reformers and hardliners share the same institution, making the moral conflict internal rather than a simple battle between good and evil.
4. **Orrin Sable:** the established human face of the final ideology. He uses real catastrophes and the Registry's emergency powers to justify restoring permanent silence.
5. **The Hush Engine and First Witness:** the systemic final antagonist. The Engine is not a demon and cannot simply be killed; it must be made to accept limits and distributed authority. No later volume introduces a new apex enemy to replace this ladder.

## Relationship Progression

- **Elias and Mara:** V1–V2 suspicion and professional competition; V3–V4 first reliable trust; V5–V6 disagreement over public danger and private evidence; V7 a betrayal of trust when Elias uses a family echo without full consultation; V8–V10 separation, repair through accountability, and a chosen partnership; V11–V14 they remain lovers and independent colleagues, with no one owning the other's role.
- **Elias and Ivo:** V1–V3 practical partnership; V4–V6 Ivo challenges Elias's secrecy; V7–V9 the team learns to delegate; V10–V14 Ivo becomes the main builder of the custodian network and public maintenance system.
- **Elias and Anja:** V1–V3 suspicion; V4–V6 access to restricted history; V7–V9 moral conflict; V10–V12 late cooperation without absolution; V13–V14 she testifies and retires from solitary authority.
- **Elias and Hesta:** V2–V6 theological argument; V7–V10 practical alliance; V11–V14 co-design of the consent model and a continuing, non-uniform relationship.
- **Elias and Orrin:** a dark-mirror line from missing name to active opposition, ending with the rejection of Orrin's offer to make Elias the next sole Keeper.
- **Elias and Tomas:** absence, false memory, moral inheritance, and finally chosen release. Tomas is not resurrected; Elias must separate love from the duty to use the past as a tool.

## Final External Conflict

The Hush Engine's last automatic shutdown attempts to erase the bounded covenants, the 312 volunteer records, and every renewed divine relationship. Orrin Sable attempts to restore a single Keeper while the Engine makes emergency containment look like the only safe choice. No new cosmic enemy appears; the final pressure is the established system failing at the moment the city must govern itself.

## Final Internal Choice

Elias can take the First Witness into himself and become a permanent interpreter, gaining immediate stability at the cost of sole authority over every name and return. He chooses the Open Hand instead: he shares the witness-work, releases Tomas's final echo, and leaves several relationships unclaimed. He accepts a less powerful, less complete world rather than repeating the Hush with a kinder keeper.

## Final Emotional Answer

Elias learns that love is not the right to keep a person, a god, or a past in a sealed room. Remembrance becomes trustworthy when it leaves room for refusal, shared responsibility, and grief that cannot be repaired. His final emotional movement is from solitary vigilance to chosen dependence: he stays with Mara, accepts help from Ivo, testifies beside Anja, and trusts Hesta's right to maintain a different practice.

## Planned Ending State

Morrowgate remains a damaged but living city. The Museum becomes a rotating public trust with community stewards, the Registry becomes an accountable records office, some gods return as bounded relationships, some refuse, and some remain absent. Elias loses most of his hearing ability but gains a durable place in a network that does not depend on him alone.

## Volume Progression

### Volume 1: The Night Watch — Chapters 1–50

**Central pressure:** A cracked ward-bell lets a forgotten work-god speak through the Bell of the Unpaid Shift, threatening the Museum's night staff and reopening the sealed Casket wing.

**Starting state:** Elias is a competent but isolated guard. The Museum presents itself as a public collection, and the Casket Incident is filed as a structural accident.

**Mini-arcs:** The first bell; a staff member disappears from the night roster; a forged accession card; a dangerous public tour; the origin of Elias's hearing.

**Midpoint reversal:** The Bell is not asking to be worshipped. Its echo is a worker's demand that unpaid labor be acknowledged, and honoring it publicly would expose decades of wage theft and alter the Museum's legal liability.

**Climax:** Elias, Mara, and Ivo contain the bell through a worker-led memorial and return the original wage ledger to the families. The immediate crisis resolves, but the bell's final sound opens a locked door in the Quiet Wing.

**Power change:** Nightwatch to first Echo; costs establish the rules.

**Resolution:** The Museum repairs the public danger and loses the comfortable fiction that the Casket was an accident. Elias finds his father's initials on a record that was supposedly never created.

**Next-volume pull:** A row of apartments begins losing its residents from every official list while their home objects remain active.

### Volume 2: The Hall of Small Gods — Chapters 51–100

**Central pressure:** Small household and neighborhood gods are answering a mass redevelopment that has displaced Low Ward residents.

**Investigation focus:** Threshold god-patterns, a vanished housing block, a shrine hidden inside a school, and a collector who has bought three objects whose owners were removed from the census.

**Midpoint reversal:** The apparent curse is a coordinated pattern of forced evictions. Some residents are losing not only their homes but the ability to be recognized as legal neighbors.

**Climax:** Elias and Mara organize residents to claim a threshold god through a shared threshold practice rather than a museum ritual. The god accepts only a temporary boundary, and the neighborhood is not restored; its residents choose to remain together in a new location.

**Resolution:** The immediate displacement crisis is answered with a covenant and public records. The Museum's public label is exposed as a false name, and a member of the Lantern Society is identified as a buyer.

**Power change:** Echo to Thread; Elias learns that memory can be planted and that evidence must be checked by more than one person.

**Next-volume pull:** A flood opens a sealed river archive and brings up a record listing Elias as dead.

### Volume 3: The Names in the Flood — Chapters 101–150

**Central pressure:** Floodwater exposes the Drowned Registry while the city begins denying flood victims access to their own records.

**Investigation focus:** Water damage, salvage crews, insurance fraud, a flood god, and Mara's mother's connection to a hidden shrine record.

**Midpoint reversal:** The Registry is not only preserving old names; it is using flood emergency powers to erase people whose claims would complicate redevelopment. Elias's first attempt to expose the record causes a rescued witness to be misidentified because he trusts an echo's emotional certainty.

**Climax:** The team uses physical survey marks, family testimony, and a water-god's bounded testimony to recover the names. The archive is opened publicly, but the city seals the legal identity of one district to prevent a property collapse.

**Resolution:** Families receive names and burial records, not a guaranteed return of their homes. Mara learns that her mother committed a falsification that saved people and also made later erasure harder to trace.

**Power change:** Thread becomes reliable only through collaboration; Elias's first major memory-displacement cost occurs.

**Next-volume pull:** The Registry denies that the named district exists, while a newly hired guard reports working a shift no one remembers scheduling.

### Volume 4: The Unclaimed Wing — Chapters 151–200

**Central pressure:** Staff disappearances in the Museum's restricted collection reveal that the Quiet Wing has been altered without construction records.

**Investigation focus:** A donor's breathing mask, an accession ledger with missing leaves, a retired conservator, and Orrin Sable's first documented appearance.

**Midpoint reversal:** The Wing is not a secret torture room. It is a triage system for objects whose worshippers are still alive but legally uncountable. The Museum's staff have been told that the labels are the only thing keeping those people safe.

**Climax:** Elias and Mara open a public inventory of the Wing, risking a citywide surge. They recover a vanished worker's name and a living witness, but the Registry seals the entire floor and publicly blames a gas leak.

**Resolution:** The immediate disappearance is explained and the worker is returned to public record. The team now knows the Museum's staff are participants in containment, not merely witnesses to it.

**Power change:** First Namework; Elias can attempt a bounded binding-name only with a living witness, but cannot yet safely ask a question of a whole room.

**Next-volume pull:** A city engineer discovers that the restricted floor is anchored to the old tram tunnels beneath it.

### Volume 5: The City Below — Chapters 201–250

**Central pressure:** Construction beneath Civic Hill uncovers the Hush Engine's physical network while a ward-bell fails in the sewers.

**Investigation focus:** Transit crews, cisterns, pump operators, the twelve-bell network, and the emergence of a civic pressure called the Patient City.

**Midpoint reversal:** The Engine is not a prison built around a monster. It is a distributed safety system that is beginning to collapse because the people maintaining it have been told not to understand it.

**Climax:** Elias, Ivo, and the crews hold a witness-space in the Undercroft long enough to redirect pressure from a failing ward-bell. The Patient City does not return as a ruler; it agrees to a limited civic maintenance pact with three neighborhoods.

**Resolution:** The tunnels are saved and the Hush's deliberate origin is established. The public is not told everything, but the Registry can no longer claim that the infrastructure is a harmless old utility.

**Power change:** Elias enters the Room stage through team coordination and physical work, not a solitary breakthrough.

**Next-volume pull:** A river parish sends a bell to the Museum with a note saying that a god has asked to be remembered—or allowed to die.

### Volume 6: The Bellmaker's Debt — Chapters 251–300

**Central pressure:** The bellmaker town of Ammon faces a god whose return would restore a necessary flood-warning practice but also revive a compulsory tithe and a dangerous debt.

**Investigation focus:** Hesta Rell, local bell inscriptions, a debt ledger, a shrine that has outlived its original god, and Lantern Society buyers.

**Midpoint reversal:** The god is not demanding worship. It is offering to return a useful practice in exchange for a promise the town cannot afford. Hesta wants return now; Elias understands that timing is a moral choice.

**Climax:** The town renegotiates the vow, limits the god to a shared warning bell, and refuses a full return. Peregrine attempts to steal the bell's central clapper, causing a false flood warning and a dangerous evacuation.

**Resolution:** Ammon remains intact, the god is not restored to its old power, and its people have a new practice they chose. Elias learns that a refusal can be an ethical success.

**Power change:** Room stage; Elias learns that a binding-name is only the beginning of a shared practice, not a private command.

**Next-volume pull:** A sealed object in the Museum contains a voice that resembles Tomas and a list of names Mara's mother removed.

### Volume 7: The Saint of Lost Names — Chapters 301–350

**Central pressure:** Elias must investigate the Casket site and a restricted object that appears to preserve his father's last counter-witness.

**Investigation focus:** Tomas's workshop, a missing transit platform, Mara's family letters, and an object called the **Name Behind the Door**.

**Midpoint reversal:** Tomas did not disappear to hide a theft or abandon his family. He entered the Engine as a counter-witness and left a fragment with Elias so the central lock could be reopened. The fragment is still incomplete and can be wrong.

**Climax:** Elias uses the object to save Mara during a breach, but he does so without full disclosure. The act works, and the trust cost is immediate. The team retrieves a fragment of Tomas's notes but not his body.

**Resolution:** Elias learns that Tomas's plan depended on his ability but not on his obedience. He chooses to keep investigating without treating the father's voice as a command.

**Power change:** Name and memory become more precise, but Elias can no longer be certain which memories are his own. His relationship with Mara enters a real rupture.

**Next-volume pull:** The Museum's board convenes a public hearing on artifact restitution while the Registry begins soliciting volunteers for “safe remembrance.”

### Volume 8: The Museum of Consequences — Chapters 351–400

**Central pressure:** Elias is treated as a living oracle by the Board, donors, shrines, and Registry while a series of legal hearings decides who can own the collection.

**Investigation focus:** Provenance fraud, a returning object, a donor's demand for a private chapel, and a public testimony that exposes Mara's mother's illegal record.

**Midpoint reversal:** Elias's readings are accurate enough to be useful and ambiguous enough to be weaponized. A political group arranges an emergency in which his certainty injures a living custodian. He must admit that being right about an object is not the same as being right about a person.

**Climax:** Elias refuses to certify a return until affected communities have representatives, then helps establish a public consent protocol. Mara publishes the protocol and its costs, including the museum's own failures.

**Resolution:** The immediate hearing is resolved through shared custody and a temporary return. Public trust in Elias deepens but narrows: he is no longer an independent night guard.

**Power change:** Room stage, learned through a failed attempt to hold too many echoes at once. Elias accepts supervision and delegation.

**Next-volume pull:** Two ward-bells fail in the same hour as a new city census erases informal neighborhoods from its maps.

### Volume 9: The Hush at the River — Chapters 401–450

**Central pressure:** The Hush Engine attempts an emergency partial shutdown as public records and neighborhood memory diverge.

**Investigation focus:** Hospitals, flood stations, transit lines, local shrines, Anja's choice to preserve the Engine, and Orrin's return as an active operator.

**Midpoint reversal:** The Engine is not failing because gods are returning. It is failing because the city is producing new acts of forgetting faster than the old volunteers can hold the boundary. The danger is both real and politically manufactured.

**Climax:** Elias breaks the Engine's central control chain by distributing the opening across neighborhood anchors. A local god returns with a demand that cannot be satisfied; the team must refuse it while still protecting the people who live nearby. The partial Hush fails, and the First Witness becomes partially audible.

**Resolution:** River districts remain populated and several ward-bells are deliberately left silent. The city survives the crisis, but everyone knows the old system cannot be restored unchanged.

**Power change:** Witness stage; Elias carries a god's request across the city and accepts a lasting obligation to a community he cannot fully save.

**Next-volume pull:** A traveling relic market begins selling pieces of the ward-bells under legal permits from the Registry.

### Volume 10: The Saints' Trade — Chapters 451–500

**Central pressure:** The Lantern Society turns the ward failures into a cross-regional market for divine fragments, memory jars, and safe-looking contracts.

**Investigation focus:** River routes, private chapels, collectors, regional god-patterns, and the social consequences of selling access to a sacred relationship.

**Midpoint reversal:** Some sellers are preserving objects that no local community can safely hold. Destroying the market would destroy those objects too. The team must distinguish a harmful claim from a necessary stewardship arrangement.

**Climax:** Mara builds a public provenance network with shrine keepers and port inspectors. Elias and Ivo sabotage a shipment that is causing a hollowing event, while Hesta negotiates a legal shrine that does not permit private ownership.

**Resolution:** The Lantern Society loses the ability to trade unregistered echoes, but a cooperative relic exchange remains. Elias demonstrates that a god-pattern can be shared through a bounded public trust rather than sold.

**Power change:** The first steps toward the Commons stage; Elias can teach the basic limits of witness-work, but cannot transfer the full cost to others without their informed consent.

**Next-volume pull:** A map of Morrowgate changes overnight, removing a neighborhood that everyone in it still calls home.

### Volume 11: A Map of Unmaking — Chapters 501–550

**Central pressure:** The team investigates blank districts, missing civic routes, and people whose legal and family records have been split apart.

**Investigation focus:** Old tram routes, mapmakers, river parishes, the 312 volunteer memorial, and the Registry's connection to present-day redevelopment.

**Midpoint reversal:** The First Witness appears to be a civic intelligence rather than an original god, but the surviving records do not explain what it is made from or how it began. The Engine has continued to use the original volunteers' consent after the emergency ended. The coalition can identify the mechanism's category, but not yet its operational truth.

**Climax:** Elias and the coalition recover the neighborhood's history through living witnesses and a public map-making project. The recovered names show that the Engine cannot function without people continuously interpreting the world for it.

**Resolution:** The missing neighborhood is restored to public maps, though not all displaced residents return to their old homes. The final lock, the chamber's last safety interlock rather than the central keeper core, is located beneath the Museum.

**Power change:** Elias learns to coordinate a group of readers without possessing their memories. The cost is a permanent inability to read one of his father's most important memories.

**Next-volume pull:** The First Witness calls Elias by his childhood name and asks him to remain below.

### Volume 12: The First Witness — Chapters 551–600

**Central pressure:** Elias enters the First Witness chamber to stop Orrin's attempt to restart the permanent Hush and to learn what Tomas actually did.

**Investigation focus:** The 312 volunteer names and memories, the counter-witness mechanism, the origin of the third ward-bell, and the distinction between memory, soul, and god.

**Midpoint reversal:** Tomas is gone. The remaining voice is a pattern assembled from his final acts, not a recoverable person. Orrin's permanent Hush would save the city from immediate chaos by making every relationship revocable.

**Climax:** Elias refuses both Orrin's offer to become the sole Keeper and the First Witness's offer to define which names are safe. He opens the chamber to a distributed set of witnesses, sharing the load and losing much of his personal memory.

**Resolution:** The First Witness is exposed as a human mechanism assembled from 312 volunteered memories, not a god. The central lock is broken, the remaining volunteers' names are publicly restored, and Orrin is disarmed and detained. The final lock remains engaged; it cannot release automatically and will require a deliberate opening once the custodian network is tested. The city has no stable Hush and no single replacement.

**Power change:** Commons stage; Elias can transfer witness-work only to people who understand the costs and choose to accept them.

**Next-volume pull:** The open chamber sends invitations through the ward-bells, and several forgotten gods begin answering at once.

### Volume 13: The Return of the Unnamed — Chapters 601–650

**Central pressure:** Morrowgate must survive a distributed divine opening without creating a new central authority or allowing hostile patterns to take advantage of the gap.

**Investigation focus:** Local covenants, returned and refused gods, public hearings, Mara's archive, Ivo's custodian network, and the surviving Accord factions.

**Midpoint reversal:** A god offers Elias a single binding-name that would make him the obvious center of the new order. The offer is genuine and would save lives immediately, but it would turn shared authority into personal sovereignty.

**Climax:** Elias declines the name. Mara publishes the conditions and refusals; Hesta organizes living shrine keepers; Ivo's technicians stabilize separate anchors; Anja dismantles the legal monopoly that made solitary keepers possible. The city keeps a fragmented network of bounded relationships rather than a new god.

**Resolution:** Some gods return, some negotiate, and some choose to end. During the transfer, a surviving Accord emergency order releases Orrin from the Volume 12 detention, and he uses the resulting access to return to the ward-lines. The First Witness becomes a public, monitored presence rather than a secret god. Elias suffers a temporary, uneven loss of object-hearing during the distributed opening; the network can respond without him, but the permanent cost remains reserved for Volume 14.

**Power change:** Open Hand preparation; Elias accepts that the network must work through temporary gaps in his hearing and that the final irreversible loss is not yet spent.

**Next-volume pull:** As the unsupported Hush core begins its last automatic shutdown, the released Orrin reaches it with plans to seize the First Witness and restore a sole Keeper; if the shutdown completes, it will erase every active covenant.

### Volume 14: The Museum After the Gods — Chapters 651–720

**Central pressure:** The old Engine attempts to collapse into permanent silence while the newly bounded gods, civic institutions, and neighborhoods are still deciding what they can sustain, and released Orrin tries to restore a single Keeper.

**Investigation focus:** The twelve ward-bells, the Museum charter, the First Witness's last automatic routine, public records, and the practical consequences of returning a name.

**Midpoint reversal:** Elias can stop the shutdown by taking the First Witness into himself and carrying the broken central lock's keeper core as its permanent interpreter. He can also open the final lock and risk a citywide cascade. There is no third path that leaves him untouched.

**Climax:** Elias and the custodian network perform the Open Hand: they acknowledge only the relationships whose communities freely accept them, deliberately abandon several names, and let the First Witness end its role as keeper. Orrin's surviving infrastructure cannot restore sole authority because the people it once edited have already chosen their own covenants.

**Resolution:** The Engine is dismantled and its old containment function is transformed into local agreements, the Museum becomes a public trust with rotating community oversight, and divine return is governed by consent rather than ownership. Elias gives up Tomas's last echo and most of his hearing ability. The immediate aftermath is a world with fewer certainties but no renewed forced Hush.

**Final question:** Which gods, people, and histories will choose to be found by the living—and which absences will the city finally stop trying to repair?

## Ending Constraint

The final enemy is the established Hush Engine and the human ideology built around it, especially Orrin Sable's attempt to restore solitary control. The ending cannot solve the conflict by introducing a larger god, a new secret system, a hidden parentage, or a final artifact that erases the consequences of the previous volumes. The final victory is a changed human institution and a distributed choice made by the people of Morrowgate.
