## Central Pressure

Floodwater exposes the Drowned Registry while the city begins denying flood victims access to their own records. *(Series outline.)*

**As Volume 02 has actually turned out, the pressure is one layer underneath that, and it is the same layer the series outline's own reversal depends on: a field that is written down for one purpose and read by forty others.** A flood claim is settled against a **date of loss**. A date of loss is the date by which a household has demonstrably stopped being a household, because a claim pays for a loss and a household that has not lost anything has nothing to claim. **The instrument is a box on page one of a two-page instruction and nobody in this business has read it, and the answer, when it comes, is worse than a bill: a date of loss is for the excess.** Volume 02 asked *what is a name field for* and was told *for the bill*. **Volume 03 asks *what is a date of loss for* and is told *for the excess*, and the two answers are the same shape, and the second one is on a form that decides whether a person is a household.** Both the answer and the machinery are on the page and neither is a mystery; the mystery is what the water does to a paper record and who decides to use it.

## The Object

**A reach rope.** Manila, red-ochred, about eleven metres, with a splice at about four metres, knots at intervals that are not at intervals, a lead line, and a canvas tag with a parish name and a year on it. It was the tie-line of a flood-reach board hung outside a lock house; it was re-tied at the start of a season by a person who knew the water; **it is now in a sealed store on a wharf and the water has been in it.**

**Its rhyme is canonical and predates it:** the **rope line in the Hall of Former Faiths**, about a hundred and forty years old, joined in the middle of the floor by a sheet bend in about 1935, its stated purpose **CIRCULATION** (Ch 79, four times; Ch 100), **not on the schedule, it is furniture**, and a clerk of works who re-hung it in November 1935 because a route from the north door to the south door was fouled at the third stanchion. **Nobody has ever put the two ropes in the same sentence. Volume 02's live question — is the pattern the object or the place — was asked four times and refused four times, by four people with a duty. It is asked in this volume and it is not answered by a person and not answered by a document. It is answered by a measurement, and the measurement is on a rope.**

**What that means, fixed here so that three files cannot say three different things about it. The reach rope's knots are at intervals that are not at intervals, and they are not irregular: they are a tide-mark series, because a person who knew the water tied them where the water stood.** A **printed table of datums** belonging to a river engineer of about fifty-five is what turns a series of knots into a series of heights, and so the answer to Volume 02's question arrives as **a number beside a knot, and not as a sentence in anybody's mouth.** The engineer's function is the table and not the verdict: **he is right that the two do not agree, he can say which of them he trusts and why, and he does not say the sentence, and nobody else in the volume is given the sentence either, and there is no room in which it is said.** A measurement is a fact about the world. A man of fifty-five with a rule is how it gets printed. **If any chapter has him *answer* the question, the chapter is wrong, and if any chapter has a character say that the pattern is the rope, the chapter is wrong twice.**

**The god is not named in this volume, and no card is ever cut for a rope.** Fenn's sentence governs and is not to be improved on, and **its date is Thursday 25 February 2025 and not any other**: Ch 79, spoken *"at about half past ten on the Thursday morning, in a conservation office, to a registrar and to a man in a coat"* — *You can card a bell. You cannot card a rope. There is no line on the form for it and I have looked twice. It is not a thing, it is a fitting, and the day somebody prints a card that says what a rope is, that rope is an object and it goes in a case.*

### The record of how this map was checked, and the error that was in the first version of this file

**The sweep is ordinal-capable in words as well as in digits, it reports how many phrases it could not parse, and the unparsed count has to be zero before the hit count means anything.** Over this file, `outline/volume-02.md` and all five `outline/batches/volume-02-batch-000N.md` card files, in one pass: **105 weekday-and-date phrases seen, 105 parsed, 0 unparsed, 15 real mismatches.** A digit-only sweep over the same files **parses 0 phrases** and would have reported no mismatches at all, which is the failure the last two reviews each caught themselves making.

**Fourteen of the fifteen were in this file and one was in `outline/batches/volume-02-batch-0001.md`.** All fifteen are corrected. The errors were of three kinds and none of them was a wrong date:

1. **A whole week row shifted by two days**, because the week boundaries were taken from the real calendar while the day labels inside them were taken from the pinned line. This produced rows like *Mon 9 – Sun 15 Jun*, which is a real-calendar week, inside a table headed *pinned*. Every week row in the first version was affected.
2. **A wrong weekday on a date the plan had already fixed correctly**, so that the header of this file put a Saturday on the sixteenth of November for a volume it had already computed, in the same sentence, as *one hundred and sixty-eight days, twenty-four weeks exactly* — a phrase which, on the pinned line, forces 1 June and 16 November onto the same weekday, and both are Tuesdays. **The arithmetic was right and the label was wrong, which is the same defect class as a fact stated twice with different values, and the second statement is almost always the one that is wrong.** A sweep will now flag the sentence in this paragraph that names the wrong weekday, because a sweep cannot tell a record of an error from an error, and **this paragraph is the one place in this file where a Saturday appears against a November 2025 date and it is describing what was wrong and not proposing it.**
3. **A list of Fridays and Saturdays that did not contain the flood.** The first version stated *Fridays in June are the 6th, 13th, 20th and 27th; Saturdays are the 7th, 12th, 19th and 26th* and then, three lines later, put the flood on Friday the eighteenth. The pinned Fridays are the 4th, 11th, 18th and 25th and the pinned Saturdays are the 5th, 12th, 19th and 26th, so the flood was always right and the list was always wrong. **A day list that does not contain the day it is a list of is the cheapest possible way to ship a real calendar into a manuscript, and it is checked by one subtraction.**

**And one error of the corrected version that was not a date at all, and it is the reason the sweep above is not sufficient on its own: a movement label on the wrong row of the table.** The version of this file that fixed the fifteen carried *Movement 3 closes* on the week of Mon 6 – Sun 12 September, which is Chapters 130 and 131. **Movement 3 is Chapters 121 to 133 and the label belongs to the week that carries 133, which is Mon 13 – Sun 19 September.** Corrected. **What is worth recording is where it came from: the same pass had already written the movement boundaries out correctly elsewhere in the same file, so this was not a miscalculation. It was a label typed into the row under the cursor. ⚠ A sweep of weekday-and-date phrases parses zero labels of this kind and reports zero, and a reviewer had to read the table against the movement list to find it, which is the argument for reading as well as running.** The boundaries are in this file and they are correct: **movements fall at 110, 120, 133 and 134**, and the four rows that carry them now say so on the right chapters.
