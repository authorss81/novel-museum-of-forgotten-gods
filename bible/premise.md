# Premise

## Title and Shape

**Working title:** *The Museum of Forgotten Gods*
**Genre:** urban fantasy / institutional horror
**Length target:** approximately 720 chapters across 14 volumes
**Lead:** Elias Venn, a night guard at the Morrowgate Museum of Quiet Saints

This is a mystery-led institutional fantasy about a city that made forgetting into public infrastructure. The museum is a real workplace with budgets, security cameras, flood damage, staff arguments, and ordinary visitors. Its exhibits are also the last physical containers of divine patterns whose communities have disappeared or deliberately broken their rituals.

## Logline

When night guard Elias Venn touches a bell that should be silent, he hears the last moment its forgotten god was believed in. The memory is a request, not a prophecy. To keep the museum—and then the city—from being consumed by what it has stored, Elias must learn the difference between preserving an object, respecting a god, and claiming authority over both.

## Core Promise

Each major object case opens with a concrete museum problem: a label that has changed, a donor with impossible provenance, a sealed room that appears in no floor plan, a staff member missing from the payroll, or a neighborhood that begins to disappear from public maps. The supernatural solution is never a simple identification. Elias must investigate the object's history, find the living people affected by it, choose a response, and live with the cost.

The series repeatedly asks:

> When a god is gone, who has the right to decide what its memory means?

## Protagonist

Elias wants a stable life, a permanent place at the museum, and proof of what happened to his missing father. He is good at noticing details, handling frightened people, and keeping a dangerous room from becoming a worse dangerous room. He is not naturally a hero. He keeps people at a professional distance because he believes that if he documents everything correctly, he can prevent another loss.

His deeper need is to accept that truth cannot be carried safely by one person. He must learn to ask for help, share authority, and let living communities decide the meaning of their own rituals without making their suffering serve his need for a final answer.

## Central Mystery

The public story says that the Great Hush killed the city's gods and that the museum is a neutral place to keep their remains. The truth is more dangerous: the Quiet Accord deliberately built the Hush Engine to stop rival worshippers from using gods as weapons. The engine split divine attention into object-echoes and used a civic network of names, labels, bells, and public records to keep them from reassembling. Over time, the mechanism began treating any renewed human attachment as a containment breach.

The First Witness beneath the museum is not a single god. It is a distributed consciousness built from the volunteered memories of 312 people who helped enact the first Hush. Elias's father, Tomas Venn, discovered that it was drifting toward permanent control and tried to break its central lock. His disappearance during the Casket Incident left Elias carrying a fragment of a counter-witness. Elias is not a reincarnated god or a chosen heir. He became capable of hearing objects because he once spoke the name of someone the records had erased, without being told that doing so would matter.

The mystery resolves in Volume 12. The final crisis is not the return of one forgotten god. It is the question of whether a city can let its gods and its dead be remembered without turning remembrance into ownership.

## Ending Target

The final resolution changes the museum rather than restoring the old order. The Hush Engine is dismantled through a distributed civic act: local custodians, families, shrine keepers, and museum workers acknowledge what they can carry and refuse what they cannot. Some divine patterns return as bounded relationships. Some choose to end. Most remain absent, not because the city has defeated them, but because no living community asks for them.

Elias gives up the exclusive power to hear every object. He remains connected to the museum as a trained guide and witness, but he is no longer the only person who can hold a dangerous memory. The museum becomes a public trust with rotating community oversight. Its exhibits may be studied or displayed; a god-pattern may be tended under shared trust, returned to external custodians, or recorded as left absent when no community accepts an active relationship. None may be privately owned as a god.

## Narrative Contract

- The story is male-led, with one slow-burn relationship between Elias and Mara Iles. There is no harem structure.
- Elias gains power through practice, relationships, failure, ritual study, and changed decisions. He never becomes a casual omnipotent solver.
- The supernatural is rare, local, physically consequential, and connected to human work and memory.
- System-like messages belong to the in-world Index and are short, incomplete, and fallible.
- Every artifact mystery has ordinary victims, institutions, and aftermath; not every object is a monster and not every god wants to return.
- A volume resolves a real local promise even when it opens a larger question.
- No prose is drafted from this document until the outline and batch phases provide chapter cards.
