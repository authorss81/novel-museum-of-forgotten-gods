# Chapter 0064 — The Hundred and Forty Years of a Rope

Nobody in the Museum of Quiet Saints could say what the rope line in the Hall of Former Faiths was for, and that is not a gap in anybody's knowledge that can be filled by looking harder, because four people were asked on the morning of Thursday the twenty-first of January 2025 and all four of them were competent and all four of them said a different true thing, and one of them was fifty-four years old and had been in the building twice.

**The Director asked them herself, in a corridor, on the Wednesday morning, standing up, with a notepad, because she had said in a public room the previous day that she would look, and she is a Director who has been caught out exactly once in nineteen years and does not intend to be caught twice.**

Fenn said: *It's a rope. I've made four hundred cards and I have never once been asked what it is for and I have never once wondered, and I will tell you why nobody wonders: because everybody can see it. You can see a rope. There is nothing on it. It's the only object in this building that doesn't want anything from anybody.* Then she said, after about four seconds, which is unusual for her: *And I'll tell you what I do want to say about it, and then I'll go back to the cutter. Somebody in this building has decided this week to write down what a rope is, and that's the first time in five years I've wanted to hand a job to somebody, and it's a stupid job and I'd do it for free.*

Mrs Farrier, who has done the key board for fourteen years and who has never in that time been asked a question about a physical object, said: *There's a bin of them in the store on the ground floor. Nine of them, in a bin, and about a hundred and forty came out of that store in 1994 when the terrazzo was done, and they were brass, and they were in a bin, and there's never been an inventory of them because they are not objects, they are furniture, and if I had put a list of them in a book in 1994 somebody would have taken the list out and looked at it and said Fenn's done a list of the stanchions, and I would have been the woman who did a list of the stanchions.* She said it without any temper at all, the way a person describes a road they have driven four thousand times.

Nunn, from Hallowgate Foyston, who came in at the Director's request and who is a transfer clerk and not a Museum employee and has never in his working life been inside a gallery: *I can tell you what it's for and you'll not like it. It's a crowd line. I've tied about forty thousand of them. You put a line in front of the thing so that the people coming through go past it one at a time and in order and at the same distance, and you do it because otherwise the ones at the back never see it and the ones at the front lean over it. It's not to protect the thing. I've never in my life seen a rope that was to protect the thing. It's circulation. It's a printing press with a walking speed.*

And the Director said: *I have had that answer three times today from three people and I am not going to be able to use any of them, because it is a true answer about ropes and not an answer about this rope, and this rope is about a hundred and forty years old and there is a knot in the middle of it.*

---

**And the knot is the whole of the morning, and it is in the middle of the floor of the Hall of Former Faiths at about half past nine on the morning of Thursday the twenty-first of January, and it took a woman of fifty-four the better part of ten minutes to find and four to be sure of.**

Hattie Bramm came in at about ten past ten. She paid a pound. Her name went in the visitors' book in the ordinary column and the time-out column is blank, and the front desk said afterwards that it was the first time in nineteen years that a member of the public had come in who was not there to look at a thing in a case, and Margaret Priest did not say that to anybody, and it is on the page because she has been keeping a counter for nineteen years and she noticed.

**She had not been in the building since the twenty-seventh of November. She was not Museum staff. She was not a witness. She was not on a list. She was not on a sub-group, and she was not on a sheet in a plastic wallet in a hall in Sillick Lane, and nobody in this city can direct her to do anything, and that is not a power anybody gave her — it is the consequence of her having refused six things in this case and being owed nothing for any of them.**

She came because the Director wrote to her on the Wednesday afternoon. One paragraph, on letterhead, with a cost in it: *the Museum will pay your pound and your bus and will not ask you for anything, and if you come you will be a member of the public in a public room and no minute of this Museum will record that you were here, and if that is not a thing you want, do not come.*

She said yes in about eleven seconds and she came on the bus and she was on the pavement outside the front door at about ten past ten in the morning because she had never been early to anything in her life.

---

"You asked a room full of people yesterday what the rope was for," she said, "and nobody knew. So I am going to tell you what I know and it isn't much, and then I am going to go and stand at the other end of it and you can ask me things there."

"Go on."

"Right." She had her hands in her coat. "**I have been in this room twice and both times since November and both times because of this business, and I have looked at that rope about forty times and I did not see it until the Director told me it was a hundred and forty years old, which is the only reason I saw it, and I want that said because I do not want to be the woman who noticed a rope.**

"It is not one rope.

"It is two, and they are joined, and the join is in the middle of the floor where the bell case begins, and I could see the join from the west end in about a second because I am fifty-four and I have been reading gates and loading bays and cloakroom rails for thirty-one years, and a join is a join. **Whoever tied it used a sheet bend. A sailor's bend. On a landlocked floor in a public museum in Low Ward, a hundred and forty years old, with about four knots of that kind in this building, three of them on objects, and one of them on a rope.**

"And the two halves are not the same. This end — " she nodded west — "is thinner. It's older. You can see the lay. And that end is about a hundred and forty and this end is about ninety, and somebody in about 1935 came in here with a coil and tied the two together with a knot they knew and went out again, and nobody wrote it down, because nobody writes down a rope."

She stopped there. She did not editorialise. That is not what she is for and she has never in thirty-one years pretended otherwise.

---

**And then she said the thing about the two numbers, and she said it before anybody else had thought to, and it is on the page because it is the single most useful sentence anybody in this case has said about documents in seven weeks.**

"Now. Before anybody in this building starts making a beautiful thing out of that. **Don't you dare make those two the same number.** That rope is a hundred and forty years old. I have got a hundred and forty names in a drawer in number fourteen and I am not giving them to anybody and I have said no about them three times, and if the two hundreds and forties get put in the same sentence in this building by some well-meaning person with a pen, then the rope becomes a piece of evidence and it is not, and my hundred and forty become a piece of furniture and they are not, and one of those two is a list of women who stood at a gate in the wet in 1974 and the other one is a bit of cord, and I will not have them married to each other to make a point."

"Understood," said Mara Iles, from about nine feet away, and she wrote it down, and she wrote it down in the open, on the top of a clipboard, in a public room, in front of the woman, which is not something she does.

"You write it down."

"I write things down in front of people. It is the only way I have ever known of making sure that what I wrote down is what they said."

---

**And the second half of the morning was the tape on the front of the case at plinth thirty-four, and it is fifty-five days old, and the Director asked the requester about it in a public room with about twenty people in it, and she asked it properly, and it took about four minutes.**

"Who put that panel up."

"I did. The twenty-seventh of November. It's mine."

"Then I have to ask you whether you want it taken down, and I have to ask you in this room, and I have to write down whatever you say and I am going to write it down whatever you say. Do you want it taken down."

And Hattie Bramm said no, and gave the reason, and the reason took her about eleven seconds and it was not the reason anybody in the room was expecting.

"**No. And I'd like it minuted that I was asked and that I said no, and I'd like it minuted that I said no knowing exactly what it does.** It is a bit of A4 with tape on it and it has been on that case for fifty-five days, which is since before your Director heard about any of this, and it is the only thing in this room that anybody has ever put on a public object because they were angry and were right.

"And here's the thing. **You cannot correct that card while that's over it.** You can correct it. You'll do it, and you'll date it, and you'll strike the three false lines through, and it will be a proper correction and it'll be in your schedule and it will be in a Board pack in March and it will be the right thing to do and you should do it. And nobody will ever read it, because it's under a bit of A4 with yellow tape on it, and a person who comes in here in 2040 will see the panel, and the panel will be illegible, and then they will lift it, and under it they will find a card with a body's name on it and three lines struck through and a date.

"**That's the point of it.** Not today. In 2040, and by somebody who lifts a thing off a case to see what's under it. And that is worth more than a hundred people in a room on a Wednesday, and I have been in this room twice and I have watched about a hundred and forty people walk past a card at knee height without stopping, and I know exactly which of those two things works better, because I have stood at a gate for thirty-one years and I have watched about four thousand people walk past a noticeboard."

"Will you tape the correction over the panel," said the Director, "when it's made?"

"**No.** You'll put the corrected card on the case and you'll leave the panel exactly where it is, and if the tape lets go in 2031 it'll let go and I'll not be here and I don't care. And you might think that's spite. It isn't. It's that a thing with a panel over it for fifty-five days is a record that somebody asked, and a card with nothing over it is a card."

And Anja Ward said: "**Minuted: the panel is to remain. The card beneath is to be corrected on the date of correction and not before, and the correction is to be visible to whoever lifts the panel, and the requester has been asked in a public room and has declined in a public room.**"

---

**And the thing that happened at about half past eleven, at the east end, is that a systems technician of twenty-five got down on the floor of a public gallery on his hands and knees to look at a knot, and a Director had to decide in real time whether to let him, and she let him, and it took him four minutes, and what he said when he got up is the last line of the chapter and it is not about a rope.**

He did not touch it. He counted the lays. He said it was a sheet bend and not a fisherman's bend, and that the difference is about four inches of spare cord on the working end, and that somebody had done it in about four seconds in a room with a stone floor and a good light, and that it held.

"Can you date it," said the Director.

"No. I can tell you it's not a hundred and forty and I'd not put it much past ninety, and I'd not put it much under seventy, and I would not put a date on it because I'm not the man who dates things, I'm the man who finds out that the thing I'd date is a thing somebody else dates. **There's a date on that rope somewhere or there isn't, and either way it's a piece of paper and not a knot.**"

"You could have looked."

"**I could have looked,**" said Ivo Pell, on the floor of the Hall of Former Faiths, on his knees, at half past eleven on the morning of Thursday the twenty-first of January 2025. "**But nobody in this building is going to be allowed to go into that store on the ground floor and open a box of minutes from 1935 on the strength of a systems technician with a Saturday systems day, and if somebody let me, I'd have to write a minute about it, and then it would be an event, and the rope would be an exhibit, and the one thing I have learned in seven weeks is that a thing stops being a rope the second time somebody writes a word about it in a room with a date in the minute.**"

He got up. He had dust on one knee of his trousers and nobody in that room thought about the dust.

---

**And Elias Venn was in the room for all of it, and he was the reason for all of it, and nobody said so, and he has written that down eleven times in six weeks and this is the twelfth, and the twelfth is the one where somebody said it out loud without naming him.**

Hattie Bramm did not look at him once. She looked at the Director, and at Mara Iles's clipboard, and at the stanchions, and at the tape.

What she said was this, at about a quarter to twelve, to about twenty people in a public room, and it took about nine seconds.

"You all keep looking at the man by the west end. **He is the reason I am here and I did not come for him and I have not spoken to him since the eighteenth of December, and he has not asked me a single question in six weeks and I want that in the record, because the first thing he did in December was ask me eleven.**

"**He is the only person in this city who has asked anybody anything about this and got nothing, and he has kept on asking, and the reason that panel is on that case is a woman of twenty-eight who said a sentence out loud in a public room in November when it would have cost her the room, and the reason the fourth line of the card under it says *Municipal Works* is that nobody in this building has read the bottom of a card in a public room in about a hundred and twenty years, and the reason there is a card on a wire stand in that room with a hole in it is a woman of fifty-five in a label room who cut it on the thirtieth of December and told them it was dangerous before she cut it.**

"And he is stood over there in a coat with a pound in his pocket and none of it is his and all of it happened because he wrote a notebook and then showed it to a stranger, and I have been a queue and a folder and a hard hand for thirty-one years and I am telling you the shape of it, and the shape is that **every single thing that has gone right in this business has gone right because a competent person said a true thing out loud in a room while a man with no standing was in it**, and he was in all of them, and he has never once been asked to account for it, and he is the only person I have ever met who has not asked me for anything at all."

**And then she stopped, and looked at the rope, and said the other thing, which is the last thing in the room and it is a question and not an answer.**

"Now. Somebody put that rope in about a hundred and forty years ago for a reason, and nobody wrote the reason down, and then somebody came in about 1935 with a coil and joined it on. **What I want to know is what they were doing in here in 1935, and I am not asking the Museum, because the Museum's got a hundred and forty years and about nine hundred objects in it and hasn't got a shelf of minutes in its head. I'm asking whoever's got the shelf.**"

Nobody in that room had the shelf.

---

**And on the pavement outside at about ten past twelve he stood for about eleven minutes, which is longer than he stood on the pavement on the thirteenth of December, and it was cold, and it was not raining, and he did not write anything down for three days, which is the longest he has gone without writing something down since the sixth of October and which he has recorded as a fact about himself and not as a virtue.**

> **Thu 21 Jan. No entry for four days, and the reason is that on Thursday I was in a public room in a Museum I am not allowed to enter, on a pound, in a coat, and a woman of fifty-four stood in front of about twenty people and told them, in nine seconds and without looking at me once, that I am the reason any of it happened, and I did not need to write it down because I have been writing it down for seven weeks and I have never once been in a room when somebody else said it.**
> **The rope is not one rope. Two, joined in the middle of the floor by a sheet bend, a sailor's knot, done in about four seconds by somebody with good light, about 1935. The west half is older. Fenn says nobody wonders because you can see it. Mrs Farrier says there are nine in a bin in the store and no inventory, because they are furniture. A transfer clerk from a haulage firm said a rope is a printing press with a walking speed. None of them knows what it is for. Four competent people, four true answers, and the one thing all four of them have in common is that each of them answered about ropes.**
> **And Hattie Bramm said the thing I have been trying to get at for seven weeks and did not know I was getting at it, which is that a list of a hundred and forty is not a rope that is a hundred and forty years old, and that if you put them in the same sentence both of them stop being what they are. She said it before I did. She said it before anybody. She has got a hundred and forty names in a drawer and she has said no three times and she came on a bus to a building she has not been in since the twenty-seventh of November and she spent the morning on a rope and the afternoon on a bit of yellow tape and she has still not given me anything and still owes me nothing and I do not have a debt any more, I have a debt and it has changed shape, and I do not know what to do with that.**
> **She will not have the panel taken down. She is right. The correction goes under it and nobody will read it until somebody lifts it, and that is the whole of what a correction is for, and I have been inside that building for seven weeks trying to get a thing into a room, and she has just told me that the room was never the point and the tape is the point, and I have been wrong about that every day since the thirteenth of December.**
> **What is a name field for.**
> **And now: what is a rope for. Because somebody put one in about 140 years ago for a reason and did not write the reason down, and then somebody else came in about 1935 and joined it on with a knot they knew, and an awful lot of people in this case have looked at it and not one of them has written down that it was two.**
