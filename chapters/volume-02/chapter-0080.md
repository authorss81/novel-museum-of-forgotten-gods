# Chapter 0080 — A Covenant Is Not A Miracle

The hall at 88 Sillick Lane on the afternoon of Sunday the twenty-eighth of February 2025 had about nine people in it and a mat on the floor with its worn side facing into the house, and a plastic wallet on a table with a sheet of A4 in it and about a dozen lines under four names, and a kettle that went on twice, and the light outside the front window going at about half past four, and the practice was seven weeks and six days old.

**Nobody in this case put the three weeks and a day into a sentence on the Saturday before and nobody put it into one on the Sunday either, and the Saturday before was the twentieth of February, which is the day a Director came down the hill on a bus. It was put into one at about twenty to four in the afternoon by a man of about sixty-five who has spent his working life standing still at a gate, who had been asked a question eight days earlier and had refused it in about four seconds, and who has said since that the last three weeks are the best weeks he has had in about nine years and that he is not frightened and not reassured and is sixty-five.**

**He had been asked on the Saturday of the twentieth, and he had refused in about four seconds, and then he had said the reason before anybody had asked him for it, and then the Director of this Museum had told him that the block he lived in goes into a programme on the first of April and his tenancy ends on the twenty-second of August, and he had said *I knew in November*, and he had put the date on the sheet because a date about a house is the only kind of thing that has ever gone on it.**

By then three things had happened that this hall did not contain. A registrar had put four lines on the sheet on the Saturday in her own hand, with his permission, to be read out on the step on the twenty-second of March, promising to say nothing. A conciliation service had asked this Museum for a nil return by the eleventh of March about a hearing on the eighteenth. And a solicitor in Irving Quay had said in a committee room on the Wednesday that if a witness's evidence cannot be produced then it cannot be tested, and that an untestable witness is a hole in the case with a person standing in it, and had asked a man of thirty-two whether he was sure, and had been told yes.

Elias Venn stood at the west end of about nine feet of floor with his hands behind his back from about ten past three until about twenty past four, and he said about four sentences in fifty minutes, and three of them were *yes*, and one of them was *thank you*, and he did not write anything down, and it was the fifth time in three weeks that he had not written anything down and it is no longer a thing anybody in this case notices him doing.

The thing that changed on that afternoon was not said by anybody who came to the hall. It was said on a telephone on Friday the twenty-sixth of February by a man of about thirty-nine with a hardback book, who was not coming and would not have come if she had been asked, and who gave as her reason that a commercial programme is a commercial programme and is not improved by being discussed on a Saturday in a hall. Then she said the thing that was actually useful, which was that she had it in front of her and would read it down the line.

"Right," said Sonia Boulton, down a telephone, on Friday the twenty-sixth. "**You have been asking everybody in this case for a date since the tenth of February and nobody has given you one except me and I gave you the wrong one, because the one I gave you was a window of five weeks in March and everybody has been standing in it since Wednesday.**

"**Here is the other one. A block does not go into programme until the first day of a quarter. Block six is in. Block seven goes in on the first of April. Block eight on the first of July. It is a cycle. It is on the programme paper of the twenty-fourth of August 2023, which any person may have at no charge at this office on any working day of the week, and I have never once had to ask anybody's permission to hand it over, and I have never once been asked, in eighteen months, and I put that in because it is the most useful sentence in this telephone call and nobody in your case is going to want to hear it.**

"**And the first of April is about five weeks away. No tenancy in blocks seven, eight and nine can end before the first of April, and the notices for block seven will be served on the first of April itself, because that is the earliest date permitted and my client always serves on the earliest date permitted. So there is a date on the end of it, and the date is the first of April, and it is not a deadline anybody is frightened of, it is a shop opening.**"

"And after the first of October," said Elias Venn, into a telephone in a rented room in Low Ward.

"There is no more of it in this ward," said Sonia Boulton. "**And I want to say the rest of it myself, because you are going to be asked in March and you should have it in the right words. It is a cycle and it is not a promise. Any of those households can be given a notice on any cycle date until the first of October. There is nothing in a programme paper that says anybody will not be, because I did not put it there and I was not asked to. **If somebody in the next nine months wanted to write a sentence saying that the tenancies in this ward will be protected until a certain date, I would tell them that the sentence they want is one their landlord has not agreed to, and I would be right, and they would go and find a landlord who had agreed to it, and that landlord would not be my client.**"

A man of about thirty-nine put a telephone down in an office in Low Ward, and a man of about thirty-two stood in a rented room with a receiver in his hand for about four seconds without putting it down, and then he wrote nothing down, which is the sixth time in three weeks and the second time in a week that the thing he most wanted to write down was a thing a professional had told him on a telephone and made him promise nothing in return.

**Because a date is only worth what is at the end of it, and what is at the end of this one is a first of April, and after that a first of July, and after that a first of October, and after that a letting agent in an office in Low Ward who has not put anything in a programme paper because nobody asked her to, and a company that has never been asked for anything in eighteen months except by a woman of thirty-nine who does it on the first day of a quarter.**

He put the telephone down and went up the hill on the bus, and the pound went on a return fare to Pemberton Row on Friday the twenty-sixth, and he has not told anybody that, and it is in the notebook, and it is the correct place for it.

In the hall, at about twenty to four, with the light going off the front window and nine people in it, Frank Dunning put the kettle on and then stood by the table with his hand flat on it, the way Hattie Bramm stands by things and the way a registrar stands by things, and said what has actually happened in three weeks and a day. It took him about eleven seconds and nobody in the hall had asked him.

"**Right. Three things and then the other thing, and then I'm putting this kettle on.**"

"We're here," said a woman of about fifty from the sheet.

"**One. There's a date. First of April, first of July, first of October, and after that it's done, and it's on a bit of paper in a letting agent's office that anybody can walk in and ask for, and nobody's been asked for it in eighteen months and I have now, and that's the whole of what a letting agent gave us and she gave it to us because a Director asked her a question in a letter and didn't ask for a list.**"

"Second."

"**Two. There's a boundary. It's rule three. Anybody taking part may stop at any point and nobody's asked afterwards why.**" He turned round and pointed at the sheet without looking at it. "**That's the boundary and it's the only one and it's been there since the eighth of January in a different hand and it's the reason this is not a programme, and a programme cannot have it, because a programme has a duty in it and you can't stop a duty at any point without a form. "Third."

"**Three. He put the kettle on, and it took about a minute and a half, and about six of the nine people in that hall did not say anything, and one of them said thank you, and it was the man in the coat.

"**And now the other thing,**" said Frank Dunning, over the kettle, "**and I want to be fair about it, because I've been in this hall about nine times and I'm the one who owns the table.**"

"Go on."

"**It isn't a rescue.**"

"**Nobody in this hall has been rescued. Not you, not Vera, not the woman from the school who said no three times, not that man in the coat, not me. "**None of that has moved an inch and this hall did not move it and we are not going to,**" said Frank Dunning. "**And if anybody in this hall thinks they've been rescued then I'd like to know their name, because I'd like to talk to them about it, and I'd do it nicely, and I'd do it in here.**"

"**And I'll tell you what it is, for what it's worth, and then I'm going to shut the door at half past four because that's rule two.**"

And I've been in that hall about nine times and I've watched about nine people find out what they think about themselves on a doorstep, and I have not got a single thing to say about any of them, and I would not swap it for a form. That's what I'm saying. There's no form for this and I wouldn't fill one in.**"

---

A man of thirty-two, who had been standing at the west end of about nine feet of floor for fifty minutes, said the fourth sentence he had said all afternoon. It is the fourth time in twelve weeks that somebody has offered him something to be responsible for, and the first time that the offer was not a piece of evidence.

Frank Dunning said: "**Who's going to keep it.**"

And Elias Venn did not answer.

And the reason he did not answer was not that he did not know, and not that he was afraid of the answer, and not that he was thinking about it. **The reason was that he knew, in about a second, that there were four answers available to him and that three of them were lies and that the fourth one was the truth and that the truth was that he did not know, and that saying *I don't know* in that hall, on that afternoon, in front of about nine people, would be the first correct thing he had said in a room since the sixth of December, and that he was not going to be able to say it out loud without it being a performance, and that it would become a performance inside about four seconds in that hall because that is what nine people do to a sentence.**

Frank Dunning waited, and then he told him not to do it, and he did it in about nine seconds and without any temper at all, and he did not look at him while he said it.

"**Don't.**"

"I wasn't going to."

"**You were going to say you don't know, and it would have been true, and you would have said it like a man giving a statement, and about nine people would have felt better and one of them would have written it down.**" He picked up the cloth again. "**You'd have done the thing you do. Gone quiet and made it a moment. And I have been in this hall nine times and I am sixty-five and I know exactly what that looks like, because I have done it four thousand times at a gate.**"

"Then what should I have said."

"**Nothing,**" said Frank Dunning. "**And I am not going to say *you are not allowed into the building* either, because that is the easiest sentence anybody has said to you in twelve weeks and it is worth nothing and you have been letting it stand in for an answer since October.**"
