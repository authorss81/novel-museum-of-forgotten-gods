# Chapter 0069 — The Ones with No List

The moral floor of this case is a sentence and it takes eleven seconds to say and about forty minutes to arrive at, and the person who said it was a registration officer of thirty-one standing on a doorstep in Broad Street at eleven o'clock on the morning of Thursday the twenty-eighth of January 2025, and she was not asked a question and she had not heard anybody else say it and she was not a mystic and she does not know the name of a single person in this case.

**Elias Venn had gone out on the Wednesday and come back on the Thursday, and the going out and the coming back is the whole of the reason the day happened, and he told nobody he was going on the Wednesday and he was on a pavement at ten to ten on the Thursday morning, which is what a person with a satchel told him to do on a doorstep two days before: you go back and you knock and you ask them if anybody's here and if nobody's here you write it on the back of your own form and you put the date on it and you keep it.**

He had no form. He had nine pages in a notebook that cost thirty-five pence, and there are three lines at the top of the first page in his own hand and the first of them says *a thing I can be got at over is a thing about me*, and he had not written anything down since the Wednesday and had not torn anything out, and he was on a pavement in Broad Street in Low Ward at about ten to ten in the morning on the twenty-eighth of January 2025 with nothing in his hands at all, which is the first time since the sixth of October.

Mairin Devlin came round the corner at ten past ten with her satchel and eleven forms in it and she recognised him in about a second and a half, which is what a doorstep person does for a living.

"You're the coat from the last time."

"Yes."

"You're not on my round."

"**No.**"

"Right." She did not ask him in and she did not tell him to go away, and she said, before anything else, the thing she had said last time and had clearly thought about since. "**I said spring and it is the twenty-eighth of January and I am five weeks early, and I have told my office I am on a different round and that is a lie and I would do it again.** Four of the nine, and I might get four. I've not got a list, I've got a street and a bit of the street, and I get the bit of the street by walking it."

---

**They did three of them over the best part of an hour and a half and the first two were nothing.**

Number one, no answer, and she wrote it on the back of her own form and put the date on it, in ink, in the way she had described, and put it back in her satchel, and said: "**That's it. That's the instrument. A bit of paper with a date and a house on it in a bag that a person carries.** And in about four years that bag is a skip and the bit of paper's a bit of paper and there's no code anywhere in this county that says *I didn't get an answer on the twenty-eighth of January at Broad Street*, because there is a code for it on my form and the code is not read by anybody except me, and I read it about nine times a day and I have never once been asked what it means."

Number two, no answer, same, and she did not say anything about it the second time.

Number three, a woman of about sixty at a door who said she was fed up of it, and they were on the step for about eleven minutes, and the woman came in off the step because it was raining, and they stood in a hall, and Mairin Devlin filled in a form.

**And that is the moment of the morning and it is a moment with a form in it and it is completely ordinary and nobody in it is a story.**

"Right. Name."

"**Well, that's the thing.**" The woman was about sixty and she had a cardigan on over a housecoat and she was not suspicious of anybody, she was bored. "I've lived here nineteen years. I've paid my council tax every month of it. I've never been on anything."

"Not been on the roll?"

"I've never had a letter about it. Not one. The doctor sent me one about my flu jab in November and I've had a bank letter and I've had two of them off the council about my bin and that's it." She looked at the form. "**What's the roll?**"

"It's the register of electors. It's a list of who's allowed to vote at this address."

"I've voted in every election since I was twenty-one."

"I know you have. **You can vote at a polling station with a photograph and a utility bill and you don't have to be on the roll. It's just a different door into the same room, and it's a longer one.**" Mairin Devlin put the form on the hall table and did not hand it over. "It's not a punishment. There's no penalty. It's genuinely just a list. And the reason I say that is because about one person a week asks me that question and about one person a week hears the word *register* and thinks it means *caught*, and it doesn't, and I say that on about nine doorsteps a week."

"**Is there a list then.**"

"There is."

"Is my name on it."

"Not at the moment. It'll be on it in about nine days when it goes down and there's a bit of a hang, and then anybody can look it up, including you, including me." She turned the form round. "**Now. I need the name and I need a date of birth and I need the qualifying date, which is the date you moved in, and the qualifying date is the only hard one and I can get it off a tenancy if you have one.**

"Nineteen years ago. **I need the month.**"

---

**And they did the fourth at about half past eleven, and it was nothing, and then they stood on the pavement in the rain for about nine minutes, and Mairin Devlin said the sentence.**

"Right," she said. "**That's four. That's the whole of what I've got in this street today and I've got nine on my pad and I've not had an answer at any of the nine since November and I will not have an answer at five of them today or in March or in September.**

"And I want to say the thing properly, once, and then I'm not going to say it again, and you're going to write it down or not."

"Go on."

"**There is no form in this country for a person at a house that no system has got a name for.**" She shifted the satchel. "I have looked. I am the person who would know and I have been doing this nine years and there is not one. Not a special one, not a hardship one, not a fixed, not an emergency. The forms are: register, change of name, remove, proxy, postal vote, and about forty for a European citizen who has moved and is subject to a different rule that I'd need to read you out and I'm not going to.

"And that is not a scandal. **It is an ordinary gap in an ordinary system and it is about four hundred years old and it is not anybody's fault and there is nobody in Northglass who has done anything wrong.** And the reason it is a gap is the reason you already know, which is that a form wants a name, and a name is what a person gives a stranger at a door, and if a person does not come to the door then the only person on this street who has ever spoken to anybody about that house is **me**, and I write a code on a form and the code means *I didn't get an answer*, and the code is in a system that a printer prints, and a printer does not need to know which houses those are.

"So I'll tell you what the actual position is, and it is worse than not having a form. **I have nine houses on a pad. I have always had nine houses on a pad. The pad is not the list and the list was never the pad, and in this district there are more houses than I could knock at twice in nine years, and there is not one of them I can produce on paper, and it is not because anybody has thrown the list away. It is because nobody ever kept it. You cannot take a list of a thing nobody kept.**"

---

**And then the second half, at about ten past one, in a corridor in Hall Lane, with a printer in it, and a woman of fifty-four who had come down from a meeting and who was not asked a question and answered it anyway.**

"Somebody rang me on Friday," said Marion Kelsh, "and asked me whether the thing in Sillick Lane could be done here. And I've said no to that three times now and I keep saying no and I want to say the reason out loud in a corridor one more time and then never again.

"**It's got a sheet.** That's the whole thing. That's what it is and that's what stops it and there's no way round it and I've been turning it over since Christmas.

"Every one of those houses on Broad Street is a place somebody has to have written down first. You stand on a mat, you knock, and you say a name — and the name is in your hand, on a bit of paper, in a plastic wallet, in a hall, and it came from somewhere, and the somewhere is a list. **Frank Dunning's list. That's all it is. It's a list of about fourteen doors and a man wrote it out and kept it on a table.** And I have got about two hundred and forty households in a book and it is the best name-and-place record within four miles and I am not going to use it, and if I used it, which I won't, it would reach my children and about nine houses in this city in about a week and it would be a list I made, and a list I made is a list with a name on it, and a name on a list is a hook, and I have been told that by two people I respect in the last eight weeks and I have arrived at it myself.

"**And Broad Street is not on it. Broad Street will never be on it. You cannot get to a house by knocking at it if nobody has written it down, and the only person in this city who writes houses down for a living goes round with a satchel and gets about a third of them, and she is the one who just told you she has no list either.**"

"You want me to say something about that."

"I want you to say it out loud to somebody with a pen, because **nobody in this case has ever said it plainly and everybody has got it in a corridor.**" She put her hands flat on the table. "**The ones with no name in any system cannot be counted, and cannot be listed, and cannot be reached, and cannot be found later, and there are about nine of them on a road called Broad Street in this city this morning and there might be four times that many on four other roads and there is no way on earth to know, and the number of people in this case who cannot be helped is not a number and it is not going to become a number, and every single attempt this case has made to turn it into a number has produced a worse number.**

"That's the floor. That's it. That's the thing the whole of this is sitting on and I have said it now and I'm not having it argued about, and if anybody in this case wants to do something about it they can go and knock on a door, and if they knock on a door they had better have written it down."

---

**And the third part of the day was at about a quarter past three in a hall in Sillick Lane, at a table, with about four names on a sheet of A4 in a plastic wallet, and it is the last time anybody in this case said anything about the practice as a thing, and it was said by the man who keeps it, and he was not distressed, and it is the last image of the ten days.**

Frank Dunning put the plastic wallet on the table. It has been on that table since the fourth of January. There are about nine names on the sheet, in four hands, and about a dozen lines under them, and a date and a time against each, and the third rule is written at the top in a different hand from the first two.

"Right," he said. "**You want to know how long this lasts. Everybody's been at me about it since Christmas and everybody's stopped now and I'm telling you because I'm sixty-five and I'm not stupid.**

"**It's three weeks and three days old. It started on Monday the fourth because a woman of eighty-one stood on my step and said my name out loud and it went round like that. It runs from three o'clock until you can't read a number off a door, and that's about an hour and a half now and it'll be nine in June. Three rules. Named, timed, anybody can stop and nobody's asked afterwards why.** Vera's on the sheet four times and Vera's not allowed back till April and everybody knows it and nobody's asked her either, and if anybody asks her I'll tell them she stopped and that's all she gets.

"And here's the thing and it's the only thing I've got and it's not a complaint. **This is a piece of A4 in a plastic wallet on a table in my hall, and it dies with me.**

"Not metaphorically. **I'm sixty-five and there'll be a day the same as there's a day for everybody, and after that there's a plastic wallet in a cupboard, and then there's a man in a house deciding what to do with a plastic wallet, and I know what men do with plastic wallets. They do not ring anybody. They do not ask anybody. They do not take it to a library. They put it in a bin.**

"And nobody's going to come and take it off me, and that is the correct arrangement, and I'll tell you why, and then you can all go home. **If somebody came and took that sheet off this table then it would be a thing somebody was keeping, and then it would be a department, and then I would be a man in a department, and about nine people on a pavement would be people in a department, and it would be finished.**"

"Who keeps it," said Elias Venn, "if you don't."

"**Nobody. That's the answer and it took you four seconds and you've had it since December.**" He closed the wallet. "And I'll say the other half so you can both go and tell people. **I have not been asked a question about this since it started. Not by the museum, not by the school, not by the woman at the counter, not by you. Nine people have stood on a pavement and not one of them has ever asked me how it's going and I would have told them, and I have told nobody, and that is the best three weeks I have had in about nine years and I am sixty-five.**

"So there's your answer. **The ones on the sheet we can get to. The ones off it we've not got a door. And the sheet's on my table, and I'm not frightened and I'm not reassured, and if I stop doing this, it stops, and that's the correct arrangement and the worst arrangement in the world in that order.**"
