# Chapter 0086 — The Three Answers

At about three o'clock on the afternoon of Wednesday the seventh of April 2025 a woman of thirty-one with a satchel stood on a mat in a hall in Sillick Lane and asked about nine people one question, and she asked it in about four minutes each, and she wrote nothing down, and over the three days from Wednesday the seventh to Friday the ninth she got three answers that had nothing to do with one another, and none of the three people was a fool and none of the three people was a villain and one of them said no.

**Nobody asked on behalf of this Museum. That is the first fact about the week and it is the reason it worked, and it is the reason it was nearly not done at all, because the person who asked has a duty to about forty households for her own reasons and was asked to do it by a man of sixty-five on a Monday afternoon, and she said yes, and she told her office, and her office said nothing, which is what her office does.**

---

The question was four words long and the man with the table made her use that one.

"**You knock, and then you say what you want it to be,**" he said. "**Not *how do you feel about it*. Not *what do you think we ought to do*. **What do you want this to be.** That is the only question there has ever been in this hall and every time I have let somebody ask a different one we have ended up talking about me.**"

"Why me," said Mairin Devlin.

"**Because I am the man with the table and if anybody asks about me then they are asking a man of sixty-five whether his feelings are all right, and they are, and it takes forty minutes.**" He put his hand flat on it. "**And because I asked you, and because I am not going to be the last person in this to find out that a person is in something they did not agree to. I found that out on the twenty-second of March at ten to four in the afternoon in front of nine people and I would rather it had been nine weeks earlier.**"

She asked it nine times over three days. She did not ask it of the woman of about fifty from the sheet, who had said on the twenty-second of March that she had assumed four times that somebody somewhere had decided she was in it, and she did not ask it of the man in the coat, who said he had never stood on the mat and could not. **Eight people, and one of them is a man of fifty-one who has a bad chest and was not asked because she did not want to stand a person like that at a door for four minutes on a Wednesday, and she wrote that omission on the back of her own form and dated it, and nobody in this case knows it happened except the form.**

## Wednesday the seventh, about twenty past three: the money

The first answer came from a woman of forty-four who has been in a first-floor flat on the east side of that street for nineteen years, four doors past the row, in a stair with a light in it that works, and who has paid rent into a counter in a book every month since about 2006 without a gap.

"**The money,**" she said. "**I want the money.**"

"**Which money,**" said Mairin Devlin, because she had asked the question and it had come back with a word on it and she wanted the rest of it.

"**The money that the other people got.**" She did not sit down. "**There is a list. I have seen it. It is a public thing, anybody can ask for it, it is on a board and it has got names on it and about fifty of them and I am not one of them. And there is money on it, and I read in the paper that it is eight hundred pounds each, on account, and that somebody who was owed money by that mill for years got it, and I have paid rent to somebody in this ward for nineteen years, every month, and not one month of it has ever been late, and I have never had a penny of anything out of it, and I am owed it by nobody. Nobody owes me a thing. I have been told that. So I want the money and I want it because if I cannot have the money then what has anybody got, and the answer is nothing, and everybody in this hall has been talking for three months as though the answer is not nothing.**"

Nobody in the hall argued with her, and that is the first thing to say about the seventh of April, and it is because the woman who answered her was a registrar of twenty-eight standing nine feet away with her hands behind her back and who had not been asked anything.

"**You are right,**" said Mara Iles, "**and I am going to answer you properly and I am going to answer you in about forty seconds and I am not going to be kind about it, because being kind about it would be a lie and you would see it in about nine days.**"

"Go on."

"**The money is for people who can show that a named employer owed them a wage in a year that is now fifty years ago. There are about fifty names on that schedule and every one of them is supported by a document. **You cannot be on it. You are owed nothing by anybody, because no employer of yours has ever existed, because you were not at that mill and you were not a child in it and nobody has ever owed you a wage.** And you are owed something, and it is not a wage, and the thing you are owed is that you paid for nineteen years to live at an address and the only record of that anybody kept is a line in a book in an office in Pemberton Row that is destroyed on a Friday, and that is not fundable, and I am not going to pretend it is, because if I pretend it is then in about four years somebody will design a scheme and it will be for people like you and it will be paid by people like the fifty, and that is a thing that has happened in this country about nine times since 1945.**"

"So it's no."

"**It is no, and you are owed it, and both of those are the same afternoon,**" said the Registrar, "**and I have been in this business five years and I have never said a sentence like that to a person standing in their own doorway and I would like it recorded that I said it and that she did not sit down.**"

"I'm not sitting down because I'd be standing up for about an hour," said the woman of forty-four, "**and because the last person who sat down in this hall was me, and I paid the rent and the sitting down cost me nothing and I have decided that I would rather not be comfortable.**"

## Thursday the eighth, about ten past four: the fight

He came at ten past four on the Thursday and he was about fifty and he was not on that round any more and he did not know it and nobody in that hall was entitled to tell him and there was no instrument in the city that could tell him without his asking.

Roy Tilley had a coat on and a bag over his shoulder and he had come off a round and he had not been asked in advance and he had not wanted to be asked in advance.

"**I already told you in January,**" he said. "**I'm not doing anything with this. I've said the once and I've said it right and I'd rather be a man who got moved than a man who knew.**"

"**Nobody is asking you to do anything,**" said Frank Dunning. "**That's the whole question. Nobody's asking you to do anything. Ask me if that's not true and I've been saying it for three months.**"

"Then what."

"**She wants to know what you want this to be.**"

Roy Tilley thought about it for about eleven seconds, which is longer than the other two took, and then he said the only thing anybody has said in this case that is about the future.

"**Right. Then the thing nobody in this hall has thought of, and I'm not being clever, I'm a postman, this is the thing I know.**" He put his bag down on the mat, which nobody had done. "**I walk about four hundred doors a day. Same round, same order, every day, and the order is on the sheet and the sheet comes out of a rack on a Monday and goes back in at the end of that week, and there are about nine hundred of them in that rack and two years on the shelf and then they're binned, and nobody's job is to know which one is which.**

"**And the thing is: a door that doesn't answer on a Tuesday answers on a Thursday, because he's in on Thursdays. A door that hasn't answered for six weeks hasn't answered for six weeks. And there are about nine doors on a round like this that I could tell you about without looking at anything, and I have never once written any of it down, and I am not going to now, and that's not modesty, that's the job.**"

"That's not an instrument," said the systems technician of twenty-five, who had come along and had said nothing for an hour.

"**It's the only instrument in this country that reaches every household on a route in a fixed order on a day a man is already standing there,**" said Roy Tilley. "**Not a list. You can't make a list, I've tried, everybody's tried. A sequence. Four hundred doors, in order, one time, on a Tuesday, on a round that already goes past them anyway, and nobody writes anything and nobody goes twice and nobody rings a bell about it. That scales. That is the only thing anybody in this case has said that scales, and I have been a postman for twenty-six years and I worked that out about eleven weeks ago and I have not told anybody because I did not want to be a man who knew.**"

---

**And then he said the part that made a registrar of twenty-eight go and stand at the back of the hall, which is that he knows it is not admissible and he has known it for eleven weeks.**

"**And I know the flaw, and it is not a small one, and I am not going to pretend it is not there.** A postman is a council employee. What I know about a household is not evidence. It is a man on nine pounds an hour who walks past a door and sees whether the light's on. **If I say in a room that a door on a round does not get answered, that is a council officer reporting on a household, and that is the exact thing that has already happened to about nine people in this ward, and it happened with a form, and the form was correct.**"

"So you are describing the thing."

"**I am describing the thing and telling you I am the thing.** That is the whole of what I have got and I have been carrying it since the ninth of February and I have not said it out loud to anybody until about ten past four this afternoon, and if I say any more than this in a room I will be a man who knew, and I would rather be a man who got moved." He picked his bag up off the mat. "**And I'm not writing it down. I have not written it down. If one of you writes it down then I have made a list, and I know what happens to lists, I've got nine of them in a rack.**"

"**Then it is not admissible and it is not a record,**" said Mara Iles, from the back of the hall, "**and it is the first thing anybody has offered this street that could be said out loud in about four years without a lawyer.**"

"That's a strange thing to say to a postman."

"**It is a strange thing to say to anybody, and I have said it four times in fourteen weeks and I am not going to stop now because this one is the only one that has come from outside this building and it came from a man with a bag over his shoulder and no form at all.**"

## Friday the ninth, about half past three, and a corridor: the silence

**Hattie Bramm is fifty-four. She has a hundred and forty names in a drawer in number fourteen and she has refused this Museum twice — once on the twenty-third of December over a printed form, and once on the twenty-seventh of January in about eleven seconds — and this Museum has refused her once, in writing, in a corridor, in December, and it was correct.**

She was not on the mat. She was in a corridor off the hall with a door open behind her and a Director of the Museum of Quiet Saints standing about four feet away with her hands behind her back, and the two of them had about nine seconds and neither of them had asked for it.

"**I'm going to say the question,**" said the Director.

"**You've come to ask me a third time.**"

"**I have come to ask you a third time and I have already worked out that I am not entitled to, and I have come anyway, and I would rather you told me no in nine seconds than that we both spent twenty minutes being careful.**"

"And she said it in about nine seconds, and the reason she gave was the reason she gave on the twenty-seventh of January, and she told the Director so before the Director could work it out.

"**You have not asked me a third time. You have come and stood in a corridor and said you were not entitled to, which is a different thing, and I have been doing this for thirty-one years and I can tell the difference between somebody being refused and somebody being let off, and nobody has ever let me off.**"

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"**Because a list of people is not evidence until it is in a file, and the second it is in a file it can be used, and you would be the second time somebody has put a box in front of me and I have been in a queue for thirty-one years and I am not going to be a queue again.**"

"That is the same reason as the second time."

"**It is exactly the same reason and I have known that since about eleven o'clock this morning and I have been walking up the hill thinking about it,**" said Hattie Bramm, "**and I am going to say the other half, because you are going to go away and be right and I am not going to let you go away without it.**"

"Go on."

"**For a house that no system has got a name for.**" She said it flatly and she did not explain it and she did not look at the Director. "**That's eleven words and it is a registration officer's, not mine, and I have heard it twice and I am not saying it as mine, and I am saying it because you are going to write it down somewhere and you will not write it down because it is mine.**"

"**I am not going to write it down,**" said Anja Ward.

"**I know. That is why I said it.**"

And then Hattie Bramm said the thing that made the chapter, and she said it in a corridor in about nine seconds and she did not soften it.

"**And the hundred and forty are in a drawer and they are going to stay in a drawer, and there are nine people on that street who have never had a letter and there is no list of them and there is never going to be one, and I have known that since about 1994 and I am the only person in this city who has known it since about 1994, and you cannot take a list of a thing nobody kept.**"

"That is somebody else's sentence," said the Director.

"**It is nobody's. It is mine, from a corridor, in 1994, and she said it in a hall in January and neither of us knew the other had it, and that is the whole problem and it is not a communication problem.**"

And then she said the last of it, and it was nine seconds, and the Director of a public museum stood in a corridor and took it and did not answer it.

"**No. I will not give you the hundred and forty. Not to you and not to a woman with a satchel and not to a postman, and if you build a form I will not fill it in, and I have said no twice already to somebody in your building and both of the times were about eleven seconds and both of them were right, and I am not going to be asked a third time by a museum.**"
