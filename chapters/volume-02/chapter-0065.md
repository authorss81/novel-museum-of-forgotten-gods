# Chapter 0065 — Two Pounds

The mat on the floor of the Hall of Former Faiths had been there for twenty-three days when a woman of twenty-eight knelt down on it with a steel rule on the morning of Friday the twenty-second of January 2025 and established that the card beside it was in the wrong language, and she did it in about four minutes, and she was not looking for anything, and what she found was her own handwriting.

She had written the description of a door mat in the same room on the twenty-second of December. It was on Museum notepaper, in a folder on the first floor with nothing written on the front of it, four minutes and eleven seconds long, and it had been read out loud to a man of about sixty-five who was standing on the public side of nine feet of floor. Fenn had cut a card from it on the thirtieth of December at about four o'clock, and the card was on a wire stand in a public room, and it was the first card in about a hundred and forty years with anything in the field where a name goes.

**Mara Iles put the two side by side on a table in a conservation office at about half past nine on the Friday morning and found that the card said the same thing she had said eight days earlier, in the same words, in three places, including two staples.**

- Backing: perished at two corners, one corner re-laid. One edge repaired, fibre of a different colour and a different lay, held with two staples.

*That* was her sentence, and she had written it about Frank Dunning's mat on the twenty-second of December, and the mat that came to Civic Hill on the thirtieth of December is a different object and it happens to have been re-laid at a corner by somebody's grandmother and held with two staples.

"You're not going to believe this and I'm not going to explain it," she said, to nobody, in an empty room, and then she said it to Ivo Pell at about ten past ten when he came in with a mug. "**I've written a card about a mat that describes every mat. That's what a description is. A description is a class. And I have spent nine days being proud of it.**"

"Is that bad."

"**It's the best sentence in this building and it is why the whole of this has happened and I have not got a way of saying both of those at once.**"

---

**And then she did the thing she is actually good at, which is measure, and the measurement took the best part of ten minutes and it is the only new fact about an object in this case since the twenty-second of December.**

She put the rule across the wear band. It is about three hundred and eighty millimetres wide and its centreline is about a hundred and twenty millimetres from the edge nearest the door, and that is in the card and that is right, and that is what a mat outside a door looks like.

Then she turned the mat over.

"The wear is on both faces," she said. "**It's on both faces and it's in the middle and it doesn't know which way a door goes.** On the twenty-second of December, in this room, I measured a mat in Frank Dunning's hall and the wear was on one side only, and the side was the one facing into the dwelling, and I said out loud in a public room that the person doing the standing is the person being let in, and I have been right about that for five weeks and I am not going to stop being right about it.

"And this one has been on a floor in a school corridor that about three hundred and eighty children a day walk down, and they stand on it going past, and there is no inside to it and nobody is being let in anywhere, and the wear has gone to the middle and spread about four inches each way, and **a card cannot hold that.** A card has got five ruled lines and a heading and it can say *one band, approx. 380 mm, 120 mm from the edge nearest the door*, and the edge nearest the door is exactly that edge whichever way you turn it, and both of those sentences are true of both of these mats and neither of them is about anything."

"Write it down."

"I'm not writing it down on a card." She put the rule back in her coat. "**A card is not a place and I have known that for five years and I cut a card on the thirtieth of December with a hole in it and wrote four words under the hole, and if you want to know what a mat is for, that is the wrong instrument and there is no right one in this building.**"

---

**And the thing that came up the hill on the Thursday at about half past eleven, in a telephone call, was that somebody has bought a second mat.**

Frank Dunning rang the front desk, which he has never done, and Margaret Priest took the message, which is what the board is for, and she wrote it on a card and put it on the board, and it was picked up at about one o'clock on the Friday by a registrar and not by anybody else for about three hours, which is the correct behaviour of a board.

"**There's a mat at the bottom of the lane,**" he said. "Not mine. Somebody's brought one down. Two quid, off the shelf in the place on Fisher Lane, and it's lying by the wall at about number forty and it's been there since Saturday, and about nine of us have stood on it.

"And nobody's told anybody. That's the bit I want to say. **Nobody's told anybody and nobody's asked anybody and it isn't mine and I didn't buy it and I don't know who did, and about nine of us have stood on it anyway because it was there and it was a mat and you stand on a mat.**"

"Are the people on the sheet still on the sheet."

"Aye, the same fourteen doors."

"And the practice."

"**The same three rules.**" A pause of about two seconds, on a line from Low Ward to Civic Hill. "That's what's doing my head in. Somebody's put a mat down and the rules didn't change. Nobody wrote a new sheet. Nobody asked. It's the same three rules on the same wallet in my hall and there's just — a second mat. And I've been sitting here since Saturday working out what that is and I can't work out what that is, and I've been a gate clerk for thirty-one years and I've never not been able to work out what a thing is."

---

**What it was, is that a practice with a mat in it is not portable, and a practice with a shop in it is.**

Nobody in this case had ever said that and it took a systems technician of twenty-five the best part of ten minutes in a corridor on the Friday afternoon to say it, and then four other people said it after him, and by the end of the day it was on a piece of Museum notepaper in a corridor on the fourth floor, and nobody wanted it there.

"You've got a mat in a hall in Sillick Lane," said Ivo Pell. "**That is not a practice. That is a practice with a piece of furniture. The practice is three rules and a sheet and a knock, and the mat is — "** he stopped and did the thing he does, which is to say the other thing. "**No. The mat is not a piece of furniture. Don't write that down, it's wrong, and I nearly wrote it down because it's the sort of sentence that sounds clever. The mat is the only thing in that hall that means somebody lives there. It's the only object in this entire business that anybody can stand on.**

"And somebody's bought a second one. And the rules are the same. And there is nothing anywhere in this city — nothing, not a form, not a clause, not a Registry classification, not a condition in a Director's standing orders — **that says where a mat goes.** And that is not because a hundred and forty years ago somebody decided it. It's because nobody has ever needed to say it. And on Saturday somebody needed it and said nothing and put a mat down by a wall at number forty and the practice ran on it, and that's it. That's the whole event. **And about nine mats have been sold in this city since the fourth of January.**"

"Who's selling them."

"A shop on Fisher Lane. Near the top, by the bus stop. Two pounds. They are not special and there are about four hundred of them in the county and they are the same mat." He put his mug down on the ledge. "**And here is the thing and then I'll go and do my jobs. Nobody's job is to know that nine of them have been sold. Not the Museum's. Not the district's. Not the Registry's. Not the shop's, and the woman behind that counter is about nineteen and she has sold nine mats and four of them were to people who live in Sillick Lane and she could not tell you that and I would not ask her to, because she is not obliged to know and she is not hiding anything.**"

---

**And then a woman of fifty-four had to decide something, and she decided it in a corridor in Hall Lane at about half past three on the Friday afternoon in about four minutes, and she was asked by a woman of fifty-four who telephoned her on the Thursday, which is the only time in this case that has happened.**

Marion Kelsh. School business manager, St Anselm's (Hall Lane), nineteen years in post, a premises inventory in a hardback on a corridor wall, a fire risk assessment with the word *including* on it, and a girl of nine whose mat is on a floor in a public museum four miles away and cannot be got back because the Director's second condition requires a countersignature by a person who is not an officer or employee of the Museum and the only such person is the child.

"**They've asked me three times this month,**" she said, "and I've said no twice and I've been thinking about it since Christmas, and I'll tell you what I decided and then I'll tell you why I decided it and I want it in that order because the second half is the interesting half and the first half is just me saying no to a woman who was very decent about it."

"Go on."

"**No. The thing in Sillick Lane is not ours and I am not having it in this building and I do not want one of our mats, and there is a mat of ours in a gallery on Civic Hill that I would like back more than anything in this conversation and I am not going to ask for it, and I want you to notice that I have said both halves of that in one breath and have not noticed until just now.**

"Here's the why. **That thing works because somebody lives behind the door.** That's it. That's the whole mechanism and there is no god in it and there is no mystery in it and the reason it works is that when a person stands on a mat and says a name out loud, the person who answers is a person who was already expecting to be answered. Vera Pilling's correction. You have to knock first. **And a school has three hundred and eighty doors and not one of them is anybody's.** Every child in this building can be let in and not one of them is being let in, and about forty of them have a mat on the floor of their own home and about three hundred and forty do not, and I am not going to put a name on a card for the forty and I am not going to hand a mat to the three hundred and forty, and that is not a moral position, it is arithmetic, and I have been doing arithmetic in this building for nineteen years and I am good at it."

"So you'd take the mat back if you could."

"**I'd take the mat back and I'd not put it in a corridor,**" said Marion Kelsh. "I'd have it on a shelf in an office, or I'd have it at home, and I'd not make a thing of it, and I would say to that girl what her gran said to whoever mended the corner of it, which is *that's yours, put it by your door*, and if she asked me what it was for I'd tell her it's for wiping your feet on and I would not be lying very much. **And the mat in the gallery can stay there for ever. I would like it back. I'm not going to be the woman who made it a thing.**"

---

**And on the same afternoon, on the fourth floor, in a corridor, a Director said a sentence out loud that she had been thinking about since the Wednesday, and a registrar of twenty-eight said no to it before she had finished it, and the reason she gave is the reason the whole of this case is a case and not a campaign.**

"Say that again slowly," said Mara Iles, "because I want to hear where it is going before it gets there."

"I was going to say that we could lend them a mat."

"**No.**"

"Mar—"

"**No, and I am going to say the rest of it in a corridor on a Friday afternoon and I would rather say it here than in a committee room, because in a corridor there are two of us and in a committee room there are five and I want two.**" She had her hand flat on the wall, which she does. "Here it is. The Museum has about nine hundred objects and a hundred and forty years and a Director and a Registrar and a schedule of collections that has been incomplete since the ninth of December. **If this institution lends a mat to a street, it acquires a duty to that mat.** A duty means somebody is responsible for it, and responsible means it turns up on a schedule, and a schedule means it gets counted on a Friday by somebody whose job it is, and the count is the whole of how this building works and it is the only reason this building works.

"And then a woman of about sixty-five has a mat in her hall and she is eighty in eight months, and the mat goes to a cupboard, and nobody comes, and **somebody in this building has been responsible for a mat in Low Ward for two years without anybody knowing and it cost a gallery and about ninety people on a Friday and I have a minute about it.** I am not doing that again with something a person stands on.

"**And the second thing, which is worse and which I have thought about for about four days. A mat on a floor is an obstruction. I know that because I have read Marion Kelsh's fire risk assessment and it is eleven pages and the word *including* is on it, and she is better at this than I am and she has been doing it for nineteen years. A mat in a hall in a street is somebody's front step. A mat in this building is on a schedule in about four months, and a schedule means a Friday count, and a Friday count means somebody in this building has to decide what happens when a mat is not there any more, and **nobody in this case has worked that out for the mat we already have.** If we start lending them, the day we start thinking about where they go is the day this stops being a street and becomes a programme.**"

"**It is not a Museum practice,"** said Anja Ward, in a corridor, at about four o'clock on Friday the twenty-second of January 2025, and put the words in her own mouth deliberately, which she has never done in nineteen years. "**It is not a Museum practice and I am not going to call it one and I have had my own minute about a card with a body's name on it on it since the thirtieth of December and I am not going to put a practice with three rules and a sheet of A4 on a letterhead of this institution when I cannot say what it is for.**"

"No," said Mara Iles. "And it isn't mine either and I have said no to five institutions about it in January and I have said the same sentence five times and the sentence is *it is not a practice, it is a relationship, and this institution has a category for the first and no category for the second.* And I said that to a woman of fifty-four on a telephone on the seventh and I have said it to a Director in a corridor today and **it is the same sentence and it does not get better and it does not get worse and it is the only true thing anybody in this case has said about it.**"

---

**And the last thing on the twenty-second of January was put to him in a corridor on the fourth floor at about ten past four, and it is on the page because he refused it, and because the refusal cost him something, and because he wrote the cost down that night.**

"You haven't got one," said Ivo Pell.

"Haven't got what."

"A word. You haven't got a word. Mara said." He was looking at the mug. "**Look, I'm not asking for it and I'm not offering you a professional opinion, I'm saying — you've got nine pages and a pencil and you could have one in about four seconds. Anything. Make it up. It doesn't have to be a good one, it just has to be a word, because a thing you can stop yourself is a thing that got interrupted, and — "**

"No."

"**All right.**"

He did not say anything else. He put the mug down and picked it up and went off to do nine jobs, and he did not push, and he did not look at him again, and **that is the whole of what happened and it is on the page because a man of twenty-five was about to be kind to him and a man of thirty-two said no.**

Elias Venn stood in a corridor on the fourth floor of a building he was barred from, at ten past four on the afternoon of Friday the twenty-second of January 2025, having been in it since about ten, on a pound, and he said no to a word.

**It was the third time in seven weeks that somebody had offered him something and it was the first time in seven weeks that he had been able to say no, and both of those are growth and he has not got a word for either and nobody in this city is going to give him one, and Mara Iles told him on the seventh of January that she is the person who would have to and has decided not to, and it was not a kindness, and she was right, and the practice's word is a permission and it belongs to a street and it is *anybody taking part may stop at any point*, and that is not a word, it is a door, and a door is not his.**

He went and stood by a window for about eleven minutes and then went down the stairs and out of the front door because the front door is the door the public uses and it costs nothing.

---

**And the last thing on the twenty-second of January was a registrar of twenty-eight sitting in a conservation office with the door shut, writing to a woman of about forty-five in Northglass, and the letter is three paragraphs, and it is the first communication anybody in this city has ever had with the Quiet Accord Registry that was not a refusal, and it is on the page because the question it asks is the question nobody in this city has ever answered, and it has a date on it, and the date is in a fortnight.**

> **To: S. Tully, assessor, quiet accord registry. Ref QA/CR/1426.**
>
> **The mat on the floor of the Hall of Former Faiths is not going to be given a name and is not going to be given an accession number and the card will continue to read *no name available*.**
>
> **Your acknowledgment of 30 December said that the schedule has no classification for activity observed outside a museum context. I am writing to confirm that this Museum accepts that, does not contest it, and is not going to re-propose.**
>
> **I am also writing to ask whether your office would receive a question on 6 February 2025 about what an object is when what it does happens in a doorway and not in a room, and I am asking whether the answer would be *this schedule has no box for it* or whether it would be something else. I am asking because if it is the first, then this Museum will know by the sixth of February and can stop asking, and that would be the most useful thing anybody has done for this building in seven weeks.**

> **M. ILES, Senior Conservator and Accession Registrar. 22 Jan 2025.**

**And it went out with the Museum's post on the Friday afternoon, and it is the first letter this building has ever sent to Northglass that was not a reply to something, and nobody in this case has ever spoken to S. Tully, and the sixth of February is a Saturday, and the first inspection slot on a rota on a wall in Northglass is on the sixth of February at eleven, and neither fact was arranged by anybody.**
