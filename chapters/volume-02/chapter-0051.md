# Chapter 0051 — The Fourth Day

It was the fourth day since a woman had written his name on a piece of paper and put it through a letter box in Lantern Row, and he had not known, when she did it on Saturday afternoon, that it was going to be a countable thing, and now it was one, and he had counted it four times before breakfast without deciding to.

The post came up through the slot at about half past six and he did not get up for it, and it was still on the table at eight.

Three things on the table.

One was a printed card the size of a postcard, with a date in a box on the left, which was the acknowledgment of delivery for a registered article that had gone to Northglass on the twelfth of December at about eleven in the morning, eleven stamps, four pounds and sixty, and had been signed for on the same morning by a person whose job it was and whose name was not on the card. He put it in the plastic wallet with the copy shop receipt and did not look at it again. It was the only thing he had done in eleven weeks that was in his favour and it had produced no letter.

Two was a folded leaflet from a letting agency on Sillick Lane, about six feet from where he had stood on Sunday morning, with a picture of a kitchen on the front of it and a phrase about *key-worker rates* in a font that had been chosen.

Three was a sheet of Museum notepaper with no number on it.

He read the third one standing up.

> **Sunday 13 December, 09:50. Hall of Former Faiths reopens, and then, at the east end of the Quiet Wing corridor on level four, the Director opens the door.**
>
> **There is a clause for you. You are not Museum staff and you are not a member of the public. Come at half past nine. Bring the notebook. Do not bring it into the room.**
>
> **— M.I., 12 Dec 2024**

He read it four times, which he was aware of.

The thing it was an invitation to had happened at twenty to ten on Sunday morning, two days before the postman had stood at the corner of Sillick Lane with a bundle in a sack, and the note had been in a box in Lantern Row since about four o'clock on Saturday afternoon, and the post had come on Wednesday, and the postman on Sillick round was on Fisher Lane and Sillick and had been on Fisher Lane and Sillick on Sunday.

He worked out the rest of it at about five minutes past nine, standing at the table, and it took him about nine seconds, and he was not proud of the nine seconds or of what they produced.

The bar says: barred from the building as from the first of December as a security matter, save attendance at any hearing or inquiry at which he is a party or a witness, on notice, in company, not before eight in the morning.

The note says: you are not Museum staff and you are not a member of the public.

A person who is not Museum staff and is not a member of the public is a person to whom none of the exceptions in line three of a four-line minute applies, and a Director who had run a building for nineteen years knew that, and a registrar of twenty-eight knew it, and it is why the sentence in the note was in the note. It was not an oversight and it was not a kindness. **It was the only category of person in Morrowgate with no lawful door into that corridor, and the clause had been written to name it.**

He had been invited, in a way that could not be accepted, by a woman who had known it on Saturday afternoon, and the reason he was finding that out on Tuesday morning with a piece of notepaper in his hand was that the note had gone into a box on a Saturday and the box is a thing that holds things for about two days and there is nobody in this city whose job it is to know what is in a letter box on a Tuesday.

He was not going to go to the building. He had no reason to go to the building. There was a door on the fourth floor of a building he was barred from as from the first of December, and a room behind it that he would never be told about, and an inquiry whose item one had been reserved to a Director, and he was going to have to find out about it in a corridor, in four words, from somebody who did not owe it to him, or not at all.

He put the note in the plastic wallet with the acknowledgment, because there was nowhere else for it, and then he took it out again and put it in the wallet with the copy shop receipt and folded the leaflet into the other pocket, and he did all of that in about four seconds and he could not have told anybody what any of it was for.

He had a pound. He had had two pounds on the eighth of December and he had spent one of them to walk the length of the ground floor of his own building, and this pound was a different pound, because he had bought a newspaper on Saturday morning for forty pence and had not eaten it, and the newspaper was in a bin in a laundrette and the pound was in his pocket and he had been carrying the same pound since Friday and it had not gone anywhere and neither had he.

---

**The agency on Sillick Lane opens at nine and he got there at nine because he wanted to be the first person in and did not want to be the first person in.**

It was four rooms above a shop that sold keys and had a sign about keys. The sign in the window said *KEY CUTTING WHILE YOU WAIT* and underneath it, in a different ink and a different decade, *SILLICK LANE LETTINGS — RENT COLLECTED MONDAY*.

There was one woman in it and she was about thirty-nine and she had a book open on the counter that was about four inches thick and had a soft spine and a ribbon in it, and she was writing in it with a biro that had a chewed cap, and she did not stop writing when he came in.

"Can I help you."

"I'm looking for work."

She wrote four more words, and put the biro in the ribbon, and closed the book about two-thirds of the way, which is a thing people do.

"Are you after cleaning?"

"Where would I look for that."

"Sillick Lane, Quay Street, the bottom of Fisher Lane, and about nine people I know who want their stairs done who can't get anybody." She turned the book round and did not push it towards him. "And before I put your name down: current employer."

"I don't have one."

She wrote something. It was four words and one of them was a dash.

"Employer, then. Who's paying you."

"Municipal payroll bureau. Low Ward, cost centre M.Q.S. 4."

The biro stopped for about a second and a half.

"That's not an employer, love. That's a place the money goes through." She said it without any pleasure in it at all, the way you read a line off a form. "Right. So you're on a list."

"I'm on a list."

"Being on a list's not the same as having somebody ring you up, though, is it. That's the bit people get wrong about being on a list. A list tells somebody what you did. It doesn't tell anybody what to do about you." She turned the book back. "M.Q.S. 4. That's a municipal number. That'll be the bureau, and they'll have got a record of you and they won't have got a word to say, and that's not them being difficult, that's what they're for, they pay people and they don't know why."

She wrote something else and then looked up.

"Reference. Any employer in the last three years, and I'll ring them myself, I don't leave it to the applicant, that's where people get caught out and I'm not losing a place over it."

He had it in an inside pocket. Four lines on a sheet of A4 with a copy stapled to the back of it, dated the first of December, signed by a Director, and no finding of fault in it anywhere, and a sentence at the bottom that was a prohibition.

He gave it to her. She read it standing up, which took about eleven seconds, and she read it a second time, which took about nine, and then she put it down flat on the counter with both hands on it.

"This doesn't say why you left."

"No."

"Then whoever I ring is going to ask me why you left, and I'm going to have to say I don't know, and then they're going to say *that's not an answer* and it won't be their fault." She looked at the sheet. "It says what you did. That's not nothing, because most people can't even get that, I've had a fella last month whose reference said *duties as previously* and I rang and the word *duties* was doing all the work. This one's got a job in it. It's the why it's got a hole in it."

"I'm not going to be able to fill the hole in."

"No. I don't suppose you will." She wrote something and he could see, because the book was open and she had not thought about it, that she had written *Venn* and then stopped and put a line through it and written it again lower down, which is what a person does when a name in her book has a star against it and she has just worked out why. "You get a place first and you fill it in after. I can't do it the other way about and neither can anybody in this street."

**And then she said the thing that was the whole of the morning, and she said it as a question, and he answered it, and it is the reason he was still in Low Ward and not on a bus to somewhere with a factory in it.**

"Where were you before this?"

"About three years. Night officer. A museum."

"Right." She did not look up. "And what did you do there."

And he said it, and he said it exactly, in one sentence, in a room above a shop that sold keys, to a woman with a book, and then he stopped.

"I was a night officer in a public museum for three years and I was good at doors and clocks and locks and small repairs and I believed those were sufficient."

"And then?"

"And then stop."

She waited to see whether there was an and then something. There was not, because there was not, and he had been told on the second of December in a corridor by a woman of twenty-eight not to add anything and not to explain it and not to tell the next one about the note on the card, and he had told the one before this about a card, and the one before that about a lock.

"All right," she said. "That's a clean answer. I'll tell you now, that's the answer that gets a person a start, because a person who says *I was good at doors* and then stops is a person who is telling you the truth about the size of it, and I've had four men in this office in nine years who went on and I could hear them going on." She put the ribbon back in the book. "It's still not a reference."

"No. It's the sentence you put after the reference."

"Aye. Well. It's what I've got."

She turned the book round again and there was a line on the page with his name on it and a star against the name and nothing in any of the boxes, and he looked at it for about four seconds and she saw him looking and did not put her hand over it.

"That goes on the stair list tomorrow if you want it. It's Thursdays and it's cash and it's a woman on Fisher Lane who'll pay you Friday. It's not a job. It's the thing you put on the reference while you get a job." She put the biro in the ribbon. "You'll want a boiler, though. Have you got a boiler."

"I've got a boiler in the room. The landlord's boiler. It's got a shelf above it and my mother keeps the letter on the shelf and there's nothing wrong with the boiler, it's the only thing in the room that's been the same for eighteen years."

She did not ask about the letter. He had not meant to say it and he could not work out afterwards whether she had heard it or had simply been polite, and it was one of about nine things in six weeks he had said that he had not decided to say.

---

**He was at the bottom of Sillick Lane at ten past twelve.**

There is no bus down Sillick Lane. There is a tram that stops at the top of it and a road that goes down to the water and about two hundred yards of pavement with eleven letter boxes on it and a row of flats on the east side that is the oldest housing on that stretch and the reason the street is called what it is called.

He had been up that street at ten to twelve on Sunday. He had been up it at half past eleven on Sunday, and had spoken to a man of about fifty for about four minutes, and had written four lines in a notebook, and had knocked on a door and gone away without knowing whether anybody was in.

He knocked again.

It took about eleven seconds and then a bolt went back and a door came off the latch about four inches and stayed there, and a voice said, "It's open."

The hall at 88 Sillick Lane is a hall. There is a tiled floor with a doormat on it, and a stair going up with a newel post and a handrail on one side only, and a table against the wall under the place where the post would have been in a house built for a bigger family. On the table there was a shoe, and a key, and a folded jumper, and a plastic wallet with a bank card in it and a folded piece of paper sticking out of the fold.

"Sorry," said the voice. "I'm not — hang on."

The door went to about two inches and stayed there while a man of about sixty-five in a cardigan with no buttons did something with a latch, and then he opened it and stood in it.

"Frank Dunning," he said. "That's the name. I don't know what you want and I'm not saying come in."

"That's all right. I'm not selling anything and I'm not from the council."

"Then what."

"My name's Elias Venn. I was in a museum on Civic Hill until the eighth of December and I'm not any more." He stopped, because there was no version of that sentence that ended anywhere. "I was on the pavement at the bottom of this street on Sunday morning talking to your postman."

"Yeah." The door came another inch. "He's stood there. He's stood there about four times this month, always looking at the sheet, and he doesn't knock, and I've watched him not knock." The man in the doorway put his hand flat on the door. "You're the fellow that was stood there with him."

"I was."

"Right." He considered this. "Come in, then, if you're stood there in the cold, and shut it behind you, there's a draught off that door that's been coming in since about 1994 and I never got round to it."

---

The hall at 88 Sillick Lane has a table under the newel post and the table has nothing on it that is a document.

Elias Venn looked at it. He looked at it for longer than he needed to, and the reason he looked at it for longer than he needed to is that he had spent six weeks learning that the only instrument that had ever held anything in this business was a record with a name against it, and he had walked into a hall expecting to find out what a man in a row of flats kept on his table, and there was nothing there, and that was the finding, and he had to not ask for it, because asking for it would be asking a man to produce a document and there was nothing to produce.

"What's on your table," he said, and then corrected himself out loud, which he had not planned. "No. I wasn't going to ask that. Never mind."

"You weren't going to ask it," Frank Dunning said, "so you can ask it now, and I'll tell you, and it'll be nothing, and then you'll go."

"There's nothing on it."

"There's never been anything on it." He said it without any weight at all, the way you say a thing you have said to yourself a lot. "I've never had a letter. I've had a bin bag and a bank."

---

The bin bag was behind the door, and it was a black bag, and it was the kind of bag you get from a shop, and it was about a third full, and on the top of the pile, face up, which is how a bag of returned post ends up, was a bank statement.

"That's Friday's," said Frank Dunning. "That's the one that comes on the second. It goes in the bag, because the bag's what I do with them."

Elias Venn did not touch it. He stood in a hall in a rented pair of shoes and looked at a bank statement on the top of a bin bag, and he asked, and he waited for the answer, which is the thing he had not done in eleven weeks.

"Can I look at it."

"You can look at it. You can't have it."

He did not pick it up. He read it upside down from about a foot away, and it was a perfectly ordinary statement, printed on a machine, on two sides, and it is worth putting the whole of the face of it down, because that is the object and everything else in this business is a room the object was in.

> **COUNTERPATH — STATEMENT OF ACCOUNT**
> **Account 40-**·**·**-**·**98 · sort code ·· · · · · ··**
> **Correspondence address: 88 SILLICK LANE, LOW WARD.**
> **Period 1 Nov 2024 to 30 Nov 2024. Statement issued 2 December 2024.**
> **D. DUNNING, F. — current account**
> **Paid in** 1,402.00 **· Out** 1,388.40 **·** *Direct debit, CWK ENERGY, 2 Dec, 88 Sillick Lane*
> **Balance 74.20**

And in the top right corner, in a rubber stamp, in a box, five words and a dash:

> **UNDELIVERABLE — ADDRESSEE —**
> **Sillick Lane — 8 Dec 24**

**And the dash is the whole of it.**

That is the wrongness, and it is the whole of the thing, and there is nothing supernatural in it and there is nothing haunting about it, and Frank Dunning was not distressed and had not been distressed since the sixth of December, and says so again in this book exactly once.

A piece of paper came out of a bank in this city on the second of December with his name on it four times and his address on it once, and it went into a box on the third, and on the sixth of December the field that a postman works from at 88 Sillick Lane stopped having a name in it, and on the eighth the paper came back through a machine with a stamp on it, and the stamp has five words and a dash where the sixth word would be.

The money went out on the second. The letter came back on the eighth. Both of those are correct. The bank is not a villain and the post office is not a villain and Frank Dunning has not done anything at all.

"That," said Elias Venn, "is the wrong document."

"Aye."

"That's the wrong document because it's got your name on it and my — because it's got your name on it four times and it's got the address on it and it's the only bit of paper in the world that's got both, and it says there's nobody at this address."

"Aye," said Frank Dunning. "That's the machine saying it. That's not me. I can't get at that. I go down on a Tuesday and I pay the money at the counter and the woman gives me a bit of paper and I put it in my pocket, and if I'm lucky the bit of paper's got a name on it and if I'm not it says a dash." He put his thumb on the corner of the table. "And there's nine years of that in the bag, and about four or five in ten of them's come back, and I've never once thought about it, and I'm not thinking about it now, because you came in and it's cold and I'm fifty past twelve."

Elias Venn stood in the hall and worked out four things in about nine seconds, in the order he worked them out, and none of them was about a god.

One: the money goes out on a date. The money has gone out on the second of every month for years, and it does not need him and it does not need a name, because a direct debit is a sort code and an account number and a date.

Two: a letter needs a name. Not a name for the person. A name for the field.

Three: nobody has taken him off anything. That is the thing he could not get past for the whole of Sunday and Monday and Tuesday morning, and it was sitting on a table in a hall on a mat in a pair of rented shoes: **nobody has taken Frank Dunning off anything. He has not been removed. A field that was full has become empty, and everything that reads the field has been doing the correct thing ever since.**

Four: and the only person who can put a name back in it works in a building four miles away that he is barred from, and does not have a form for it, and has not been asked.

And then he asked the question, because he had decided to and he had not been asked to and he wanted to be asked as well.

"What do you want me to do."

Frank Dunning looked at him properly for the first time, and the hall was cold, and there was a draught off the door that had been coming in for thirty years.

"Nothing," he said. "There's nothing anybody can do. I go down on a Tuesday, I pay the money, and if the bit of paper's got a name on it I put it in the bag with the rest, and if it hasn't then it says a dash." He shut the door behind them, and the draught stopped, which was the first thing that had been fixed all day. "And the money's gone out on the second. That's the bit that gets people. The money's gone out on the second and I don't know it, and it goes out on the second whether I know or not, and that's the bank working, and I'd not have the bank do it any other way."

"That's the bank working."

"That's the bank working," said Frank Dunning, "and the rest of it's a bin bag."

And then he said the sentence that Elias Venn wrote down at about five past one at a table in a rented room and did not show to anybody, and it is the fourth line in the book now, and it is the only entry in Volume 02 that anybody else will ever read:

"**Nobody's asked me anything about it, and there's nobody to ask, and the last one that asked me anything was the fella from the clinic about my blood, and he's not coming back till March.**"

---

He was at the corner of Fisher Lane and Sillick at about twenty past twelve, and there was nobody at it, and there was a lamp post and a pillar box and about eleven feet of kerb.

A man of about fifty had stood on that kerb on Sunday morning with a sack on his shoulder and a handheld in his hand, and had said *a name field doesn't go empty by accident, somebody's typing*, and had gone up the street at about twelve.

Elias Venn stood on the kerb and looked at the two letter boxes on the corner. He counted them, and he was embarrassed by the counting, and he counted them anyway, because that is what he does.

There are eleven letter boxes in the row's length that he could see from the corner, and two of them are on the corner, and one of the two has a name on it, a printed strip in a plastic holder, and the other has a printed strip in a plastic holder and the strip is blank.

Not worn. Not dirty. Not weathered. **Blank**, in the way that a field is blank when nobody has typed into it, which is a different thing from all of the others and is the only one of them he can prove.

Then he walked up the street, and he did it slowly, and he did it because he had ten pounds of nothing in his body and one pound in his pocket and a Tuesday afternoon in front of him and nowhere at all to be, and because the street is about two hundred yards long and there are eleven boxes on it and about nine flats on the east side and the numbers on the east side run 84, 88, 90, and there is no 86, and there never has been, and the numbers on the west side run 1, 3, 5 and 6 and 14 and they do not run in order either, and this is a street that grew around a mill gate and has never once been renumbered.

> **Tue 15 Dec, 12:55. Fisher Lane / Sillick. 88 Sillick Lane, Frank Dunning.**
> **Bank statement, Counterpath, issued 2 Dec, DD taken 2 Dec, 74.20 balance, address 88 Sillick Lane, name on it four times.**
> **Returned 8 Dec, stamp reads UNDELIVERABLE — ADDRESSEE —. Dash where a name goes. He says four or five in ten of them come back, for nine years, and he has never once thought about it.**
> **There is no 86 and there never has been. Box on the corner, second from the lamp post, printed strip in a plastic holder, strip blank, not worn.**
> **Nobody has taken anybody off anything. A field that was full has become empty and everything that reads it has been correct all along. That is the whole of it and I have got a pound and a cardboard folder and no instrument, and the only place in this city that prints a name on a card is four miles away and I am barred from it.**

He put the notebook away, and he went down to the tram, and he had a decision to make on the top of the hill, and the decision was not about the Museum and it was not about the door on the fourth floor.

It was that there is exactly one address in this city where a person's name is held in a printed field on a sheet of paper in a building that four hundred people walk through every day, and it is a delivery office, and it is open until six, and he has a pound, and he has not been asked to go by anybody, and the man who stands at the kerb on Sundays did not ask him either.
