# Chapter 0078 — The Party

There are two meanings of the word and on Wednesday the twenty-fourth of February 2025 both of them were in use in this city, and one of them was on a letterhead and the other of them was about nine hundred yards of Low Ward on the Friday before, and neither of them was a party anybody had been invited to.

**The first letter came on Friday the nineteenth of February, by hand, at about ten to four, in a padded envelope, and it was signed by a partner and it was four pages and the first page is the reason any of this happened.**

> **HALLIWARD GRISWOLD & REEVE, Solicitors. 22 Pike's Buildings, Irving Quay.**
> **19 February 2025.**
>
> **To: The Director, Morrowgate Museum of Quiet Saints. By hand and by post.**
>
> **We act for Newton Bay (Estates) Limited. We write in that capacity. We ask you to treat this as a request and not as a demand. We do not ask you to reply within any period. There is no deadline on this letter and we would rather there were not one.**
>
> **1. On the twenty-seventh of January 2025 this Museum minuted, under item six of the inquiry's terms of reference, that it is a party to the matter of the practice at Sillick Lane and not a bystander, and that it will answer for being that in due course. The minute is a public document. We have read it four times.**
>
> **2. Our client administers a programme of tenancy management in the district in which Sillick Lane stands. We do not contend that the practice referred to in your minute has any connection with our client's programme and we would be obliged if you did not suggest that it has.**
>
> **3. Our client's programme operates on quarterly cycle dates and the next cycle date falls on 1 April 2025. We mention the date only because it is the reason this letter has come faster than a letter of this kind normally would.**
>
> **4. We would be grateful for a meeting at the Museum's convenience, with the Museum's Registrar present, on any weekday in the week commencing 3 March 2025. Our partner will attend. Our client will send nobody and does not need to.**
>
> **5. Nothing in this letter requires disclosure of any information about any household, and nothing in the proposed meeting will require it, and if it does we will stop.**

**And the second letter came the same afternoon, at about four o'clock, at the same desk, and it is one page, and it is the first request this Museum has received in eleven weeks that asks for the absence of something.**

> **CONCILIATION SERVICE — MORNINGSIDE. In the matter of a question proposed to be listed under rule 9.**
> **19 February 2025.**
>
> **To: the Director. Copy: the Board Secretary.**
>
> **1. A matter will be listed for a preliminary hearing on **Tuesday 18 March 2025 at 10:30**, under rule 9. The listing is provisional and is at the direction of the officer. The Museum will be notified as a party. The Museum has already recorded that it accepts that position, on 27 January 2025, and that record is the reason for this letter.**
>
> **2. Under rule 9, a party must state in writing, not later than seven days before the hearing, what documents it holds that are relevant to the matter.**
>
> **3. **A nil return is a valid return.** The officer would be grateful for a nil return in those words. A party that returns a document it does not wish to produce is worse for that party than a party that returns nothing, and the officer has had that argument twice this year and does not enjoy it.**

**Nobody in that building had ever been asked for a nil return. Every request that had come into that building in eleven weeks had asked for something, and this one asked for the absence of something, in a form, with a date on it. And the date was the eleventh of March. And the schedule of collections was going to the Board on the eleventh of March. And a nil return was due on the same day that a dead man's name was going in front of a board, and nobody in that building had worked out at ten past four on the Friday afternoon that those two things were the same afternoon.**

---

**And the man from Irving Quay came on the Wednesday the twenty-fourth of February anyway, a week earlier than he had asked to, and the reason was on page three, and the reason was a cycle date.**

The room was a committee room on the fourth floor with the blinds up. There were five people in it. Three were Museum staff. One was a letting agent of about thirty-nine who had not been invited and had come because a letter to a museum had gone past her office on the way and she had read the envelope, and she has declined to give a better reason and it is her business. The fifth was a man of thirty-two standing at the end of a table, because a printed card with the word WITNESS in a box said he had to be in company and not before eight.

"**I am four days early and I would like to say why before I sit down,**" said the partner. "**My client's programme turns over on the first of April and about forty households on a street two hundred yards from your front door will get a piece of paper through a door on that morning, lawfully, in advance, and not one of them has ever heard of this Museum. And on the eighteenth of March there is going to be a hearing at which this Museum says it is a party to something, and I have spent a fortnight working out what you think that something is, and I would rather do it now than on the eighteenth.**"

"No," said Mara Iles. "**You would not.** You would do it on the eighteenth, in front of nine people, and it would be worse for us."

"**It would be better for us,**" said the partner. "**But thank you. I am going to do this in a strange order and I would like to say why, and then I will stop talking.**"

"Go ahead."

"**I have read your minute of the twenty-seventh four times. I have read the two decisions your Director took on the tenth of February as a result of it. I have read the finding your Registrar minuted on the third of February with a dead man's name on it, which is a public document because it went into a schedule.** He squared the papers. "**And I have been sitting in a room for a fortnight working out whether she is right.**

"**She said something in a public room in November that I have not been able to put down since, and the sentence is that a museum cannot put a name on a public card without making a hook of it, and my client has about four hundred and twelve addresses in that ward and every one of them has a name in a field somewhere, and I have been trying for a fortnight to work out whether she is talking about her own building or mine.** And I have come here to be told by a woman who is not frightened of me, because she has not been frightened of me for about ninety seconds and I would like to go on not being frightened of her."

"I am not frightened of you," said Mara Iles. "**But I am extremely careful of you, and those are different, and I learned the difference the hard way in this building about eleven weeks ago and it cost us a gallery and about ninety people with a pound each.**"

"Then we are the same, and I will take that."

---

**And then he asked the question nobody in that building had prepared for, and it took about nine seconds, and it was asked pleasantly and without any weight.**

"You held a meeting in the Hall of Former Faiths on the seventeenth of February. Public meeting, doors propped, a sheet of A4 on a stand, about a hundred and ten people, and a letting agent read a commercial programme out loud from the wrong side of your rope line."

"Yes."

"**Was that authorised.**"

"**It was not authorised and it was not minuted and no member of this Museum was under any instruction to be there,**" said Anja Ward. "**It was a Director standing in a public room at one o'clock on a Wednesday telling about a hundred and ten people the truth about her own institution, and a member of the public answered a question, and if you are about to tell me she disclosed your client's confidential information then I would like it recorded that the room had no ticket and no noticeboard and no form, and that I have spent eleven weeks trying to find a door that reaches a household and I did not find one, because the door was a public room and it has been there for a hundred and forty years and I have been standing in the wrong corridor.**"

"I was not going to say that," said the partner. "**I was going to say that my client's programme was described in public on the seventeenth of February by its managing agent, at a meeting called by you, in a room you control, in front of about a hundred and ten people, and that it is therefore out, and that it came out of your building, and that I would rather you knew that today than on the eighteenth.**"

"Thank you."

"**And I would like it noticed that I said that as a courtesy and not as a threat, and that I did not have to come to this room to say it.**" He turned a page. "**My client's position in the matter is that it has no position, because it has no interest in a practice at a door and no interest in a mat and no interest in a sheet of A4. I am instructed to say that and I am saying it. What I am instructed to ask is what the Museum thinks the matter is about, because a preliminary hearing in front of nine people is a very bad place to find out.**"

"We think it is about a knock," said Mara Iles.

"I am going to write that down," he said, "**and I am going to ask you to say it again, in the same words, in about three weeks, in front of nine people rather than to five in a committee room. And I am telling you now that I am not frightened of it. And I am telling you now that if you say it again in the same words in March then I will believe you, and if you say it differently I will notice, and I will not mention that I have noticed, and you will spend the rest of the hearing wondering whether I noticed.**"

"That is a threat."

"That is a description of how a hearing works," he said. "**And I am not going to apologise for it, and if it is a threat then you will find that out in about eleven weeks rather than in nine days, and I would rather you found out in nine days.**"

**And then he turned to the man of thirty-two at the end of the table, who had been in the room for ninety minutes without saying a word, and it is on the page because it is the first time in this case that anybody who is not in this building has asked Elias Venn a question.**

"You are the witness on the notice," said the partner. "**Are you the person who writes it down.**"

"Yes."

"Where."

"Nine pages in a notebook. It cost thirty-five pence. It is in my coat."

"**Is there a copy.**"

"**No.**"

"Is it filed anywhere."

"**No. It is deliberately not in a Museum file and it is not in a council file and it is not in yours, and I did that on purpose, under a standing instruction of this Museum that says unsolicited documents go into files. I refused to let mine go into one. And a Director has told me in writing that she will not take it off me.**"

"And if I asked you for it."

"**You would not have it, I would not give it to you, and you could not serve a request for it, and I know that because I have read this case for eleven weeks and the only instrument that has ever caught anything in it is a record with a name on it, and mine has a name on it in a rented room in Low Ward and it dies with me.**"

"**Then I am going to say three things carefully, and I am not going to enjoy saying them.**" He put his pen down. "**One. I could probably get it. Not lawfully. Not easily. But there is a version of this in which a party to a listed hearing asks a witness for his papers, and a court expresses a view, and a man of thirty-two with no employer and a pound in his pocket makes a decision about his own notebook in a corridor. I want you to know that version exists. I am telling you that I know it exists.**

"**Two. I am not going to do it, and I am not going to try, and I am telling you that I am not going to do it, and I want you to hear the shape of that sentence, which is *I am telling you*, and not *I won't*, because I could and I won't, and those are different words and I would like the difference noted.**

"**Three, and this is why I came to this room instead of writing another letter. If your evidence cannot be produced then it cannot be tested. An untestable witness in a listed hearing is not a protection. It is a hole in the case with a person standing in it.** You will be asked, in March, whether you are sure, and if you are sure you will say so, and if you are not sure you will say that instead, and either answer is worth having and both of them are better than what you have now."

"I am sure," said Elias Venn.

"**Good. Thank you. That is the whole of what I came for.**"

**And the rest of the day was the schedule of collections, which is going to a board on the eleventh of March with about eleven entries and the entries for 1974 marked incomplete, and whose second entry is not an object, and it has a person's name on it, and on the Wednesday the twenty-fourth of February at about half past three in the afternoon a woman of sixty-four who chairs this Museum's board asked the Registrar to come and explain it to a trustee a fortnight before the Board, and it took about four minutes and it is the shortest exchange in the business and the hardest one.**

The trustee is about seventy and has been a trustee for about nine years and he is a different person from Mrs Vessey and he has said almost nothing before lunch on the day itself and there is no reason to give him a name.

"**This entry,**" he said. "**It is a comparison sheet. It has a man's name on it. *Walter Naylor, of 80 Sillick Lane*, dead since about 2019, roughly dated. It has a registrar's initials on it and a witness's. It says *no accession number* in bold. And it is in a schedule of collections.**"

"Yes."

"And what is it an accession of."

"**Nothing. It is a comparison between four mornings of this Museum's own subscriber file, three print runs, and the state of our block, and it is in the inquiry file as item six material, and it has gone into the schedule of collections because the schedule is the only public index this Museum has and I would not have it in a drawer.**"

"And a man's name is on it."

"**A dead man's name is on it. It is the first time in about a hundred and twenty years that this institution has put a person's name on a document on purpose. I wrote it on the fourth of February, a systems technician witnessed it, I wrote it out by hand, and nothing was photographed.** And I would like to say the cost out loud before I ask you for an opinion, because that is what I do and because you have not asked me for one and I am going to give you one anyway."

"**Go on.**"

"**If it goes in a Board paper it may be requested by a member of the public, and if it is, then a dead man's name and the house he lived in for about forty years will be read by a stranger, correctly, in a document produced by this Museum, for ever.**" She had her reading glasses on, which she does when she is reading something she intends to be quoted. "**And the alternative is a document with a hole in it. And I have been told by a woman in a label room who has cut about four hundred cards in this building that a hole is the most dangerous thing a card can have on it, because a hole lets whoever comes next fill it in. And there is a body called Municipal Works on the foot of another card in this building which has been there about a hundred and twenty years and is going to be struck through on Thursday and put back under a panel, and it will be there for ever and nobody will read it.**"

The trustee thought about it for about as long as it takes to read it.

"**My objection is not the name,**" he said. "**The name is correct and it is supported and I have read the support. My objection is that this Museum has just told the public, in a gallery, twice, that it will not put a person's name in a public document — and it did that on the seventeenth of January in a meeting and again in a minute on the twenty-seventh — and here is a man's name going into a Board paper.**"

"That is fair."

"**It is not a legal objection. I am a trustee, not a lawyer, and I have nine minutes before the sub-group wants me.**" He squared the paper. "**I would like it read into the minutes that I objected, and I would like the word *hook* used, because that is the reason and I do not want the minute to be tidy.**"

"It will not be tidy," said Mara Iles. "**And I will tell you what I am going to do, and then you can tell me whether I am a coward. The corrected card for plinth thirty-four goes back under the panel on Thursday morning and the correction is dated and struck through and nobody will ever read it, and that is right, and the requester asked for it in a public room in January and was refused in a public room in January, and the reason is that a thing with a panel over it is a record that somebody asked.**"

"And the comparison sheet."

"**The comparison sheet goes to the board and it may be public and I would like it to be, and I have written that down, and if I am wrong then in about four years there will be a document in this building with a dead man's name and a house on it and my initials on it, and that is a person and a house and a date and about forty years, and I would rather that be on the record than be in a drawer, and I have been in this business five years and this is the first document I have ever put a person's name on and it is the first one I have ever been frightened of putting in an index.**"

The trustee wrote for about nine seconds.

"**Then I have no objection, and I would like it minuted that I objected first, and I would like the minute to say that the Chair of the Board asked for the explanation and did not give an opinion, because she is a lawyer and she will want it recorded that the Chair had no view.**" He put his pen down. "**And Registrar — I am a trustee of a public museum and I have been on this board for nine years and I want to say that this is the first document in that schedule I have ever read that is about a person, and I have been going to look at a building for nine years and I did not know that it was possible.**"
