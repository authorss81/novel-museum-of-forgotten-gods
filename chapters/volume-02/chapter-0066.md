# Chapter 0066 — The Person on the Round

The walk sheet for a delivery round is a piece of paper about the size of a broadsheet page and it is not kept by the delivery service, it is carried by the postman, and on the morning of Monday the twenty-fifth of January 2025 it was in a man's inside pocket about a quarter of a mile from a delivery office on Nether Kiln Lane, and it is the only object in this entire case that has ever been held by the only person in this entire case who is legally entitled to be carrying it.

Nobody had ever asked the man about it. That is the whole of it and it is on the page in that form because it took a man with no standing in this city about four minutes to decide to go and there is no form for the other half of it, and the man is Roy Tilley, he is about fifty, he has walked the Fisher Lane and Sillick round since the summer, and he is one man because the other one is on long-term sick and that is not his business.

**Elias Venn met him at the bottom of Sillick Lane at about half past nine on the Monday morning, on the corner outside number 88, which is the corner where a postman of about fifty was standing on Sunday the thirteenth of December with a sack on his shoulder telling a stranger that there was no 86 on the job sheet and that the 88 had no name on it and that somebody was typing.**

He came down the hill for nothing. He had no appointment. He had told nobody he was going and there was no form for it and he went anyway, and the reason he went, which he wrote out that night, is that a man who is about to lose the only job in this city where a person can see a name field empty every day of his working life is the single most useful person in this case, and that there is no instrument in the world for finding that out except turning up.

"I haven't got a reason," he said. "I want to say that first. I've come down here to stand next to you for twenty minutes and I haven't got a reason and if you'd rather I went I'd go."

"You've come down here to stand next to me," said Roy Tilley. "**That's a reason. You've just not dressed it. Nobody dresses them.**"

**He was the same man and the coat was the same coat and the handheld was in the same hand, and the first thing Elias Venn did was not look at the handheld, and both of them noticed, and Roy Tilley noticed in about a second and a half, which is the length of time it takes a man of fifty who has been in a job for twenty-six years to work out that a stranger is refusing to look at something.**

"You've not asked about the sheet," said Roy Tilley.

"No."

"You asked about it in December."

"**I asked you to turn a handheld round on the thirteenth of December and you did it, and that is the last time I am going to ask anybody for a thing like that and I did not know that on the thirteenth of December and I know it now.**"

"Alright." He shifted the sack. "**That's the correct answer and I'll tell you why it's the correct answer, and then I'm going to tell you the thing I actually want to say, which I've been wanting to say since the first week of January and I've got nobody to say it to.**"

"Go on."

"The sheet's in my pocket. It's got about two hundred and ten addresses on it and nine columns and one of the columns is a name, four characters wide, and about twelve of them on my round have gone from having a name in them to not having a name in them since about the beginning of December." He said it the way he said everything, which is as though he had already had this conversation eleven times. "**And there are four more since the Friday. Sixteen. Three of them's on Fisher Lane and one of them's off the top end and I couldn't tell you the address of any of them from memory but they're on the sheet and I could read them to you.**

"And I can't give you the sheet."

"I know."

"**No, I don't think you do, so I'll tell you why.** The sheet's not mine. It comes out of a rack in the office on a Monday and it goes back in the rack on a Friday, and there's about nine hundred of them in that rack, and they keep them two years for audit and then they go in the bin. **Two years.** And when my round changes on the ninth of February, this one goes in the rack, and it will be in a rack in a building four miles from a museum until the ninth of February 2027, and then it will be a piece of paper in a skip, and nobody will ever know that it was different from any of the other eight hundred and ninety-nine, and there is not one person in this city whose job it is to know that it was different."

---

**And Elias Venn stood on a pavement in Low Ward at about twenty to ten in the morning on the twenty-fifth of January 2025 and worked out the entire shape of the next eleven days in the time it took a bus to come, which is a thing he does, and which he is not proud of, and which he wrote down afterwards without crossing anything out.**

*There is no document in this case about the delivery side at all. There is one. It is four lines in a woman's handwriting on the back of a counter receipt and it is not a copy and it is not a release, and a woman of about twenty-seven wrote it out for a stranger in a room with a printer in it on the sixteenth of December and said it would be a weapon with no name on it if it went in a bin bag in this street.*

*And now the only copy of the thing itself goes into a rack in nine days' time and stays there two years and goes in a skip, and there is a district office four miles away that has kept every file it has ever sent anybody for twenty-four months, and a delivery office that keeps the primary document for twenty-four, and between the two of them there is nothing, no line, no cross-reference, no person whose job it is.*

*And a man is going to be moved on the ninth of February and he does not know it and nobody has told him and nobody has asked him, and I am the only person in this city who could ask him, and there is no form.*

**And then the thing he had come for, and it is the only thing he got, and it is four sentences long and it is on the page because it is the only lawful offer anybody in this case has been able to make to a man who is losing a round.**

"Is there a notice."

"What?"

"About the change. In February. Do they give you a notice, or does it just happen."

Roy Tilley stopped on the pavement.

"**You don't know that I know about the ninth of February.**"

"No."

"**I don't know it either, mate. I know they move you. Everybody knows they move you. There's a list on a wall and it's not secret and it's not written down for us, it's just on a wall.**" He shifted the sack again. "You get a week's notice if you get a week's notice. Sometimes it's four days. Sometimes a fella's round changes on a Monday and he's standing in a kitchen at half past six in the morning with his hood up wondering which one of them is his. **It's not a punishment and it's not a reward and it's not a conversation. It's a Monday.**

"So what you're asking me is whether I can find out, and the answer is yes, I can find out, and I'm not going to, and you know why I'm not going to."

"Tell me why."

"**Because if I find out, then on the ninth of February I'm a man who knew.**" He said it as though he were explaining a rule of the road to a foreign visitor. "And then the office knows I've been asking. And then it's a thing I've been asking about. And I'd rather be a man who got moved."

Elias Venn stood in the road with his hands in his coat.

"That is the best answer anybody has given me in seven weeks," he said, "and I have no idea what to do with it, and I came down here to ask you a question and a wait is all I've got and I have not got anything else, and I am not going to pretend I have."

"You've not got a form."

"**I have got no form. I have got nine pages in a notebook that cost thirty-five pence and a pound and a mother with a laundrette and a municipal envelope on the first of the month for a post that doesn't exist, and I am not the subscriber to anything, and I have not been in a room as anything except a witness since the eighth of December.**"

"Then there's your answer," said Roy Tilley. "**You're the only person I've ever talked to about this who's told me the truth about what he's got. You know how many haven't?**"

**And then he offered, and the offering is on the page because Elias Venn said no to it and the no is the growth, and there is nothing else in the morning that is.**

"Come back at one," said Roy Tilley. "I finish at one and the depot shuts the cage at half one. I'll write them out for you. **The sixteen. Addresses, dates, which column.** On a bit of paper. In my hand. And you can take it and do what you like with it, and I'll not put my name to it and I don't want my name on it, and there's nobody going to come and look at it anyway."

"No."

"**It's the dates you want. That's the whole of it. There's a date against every one of them on the sheet in my pocket and you're in a museum four miles away that's got nine of them written down with no dates at all, because their computer can't remember.**"

"I know what they are," said Elias Venn. "I was in a systems office on the eighth of January and I saw the state of our own block on the twenty-first of December with nine blanks in it and I saw the blankest bit of it and the bit that was blankest was on one street, and I have been carrying a date for that one night ever since and I got it off a woman on the telephone in an office on Marsh Lane in about four minutes, and it took her four minutes, and she has had it filed by date in a drawer for six years and nobody has ever asked her for anything."

Roy Tilley looked at him for a second and a half.

"**Then you'll not want mine.**"

"**I want yours more than I have wanted anything in seven weeks.**" He said it flatly, in the road, in the cold, at about a quarter to ten in the morning, to a postman. "And I can't have it, and here's the whole of why, and it's the only thing I know how to say.

"**A bit of paper with sixteen addresses and sixteen dates and no name on it is a weapon with no name on it.** And it would be in a coat in a room in Low Ward in about four days and then it would be in a file in a building four miles away in about four weeks, because that is the only direction anything goes in this city, and at the end of it there'd be a man in a wig in March holding a piece of paper that a postman wrote out for a stranger in a street, and he'd ask where it came from, and the answer would be *a man in a coat asked him on a pavement*, and then it is a piece of paper about a man in a coat, and not one word of it is about a name field.

"**A record with a name against it is the only thing in this business that has ever caught anything. A record with no name on it is the thing that catches the person holding it.** You told a woman of about twenty-seven that in a room with a printer in it in December and she wrote it on the back of a receipt and she was right and I have thought about it every day since."

Roy Tilley put the sack down on the wall.

"**Then what am I going to do with the sheet.**"

"Walk it. Thursday, and the Thursday after, and the one after that, and come back and tell me in a street whether the number's gone up. Not the addresses. Not the dates. **The number.** And when it does go up, you have said a number out loud to a person, on a pavement, in daylight, and I have been there, and I can be asked whether you said it, and that is a record with a name against it, and it is the only one either of us can make."

"That's not much of a record."

"It's four words and a date and two people," said Elias Venn, "**and it is the first instrument I have had in seven weeks that I cannot be got at over, and I have spent seven weeks learning that the ones I can be got at over are the ones that are worth nothing.**"
