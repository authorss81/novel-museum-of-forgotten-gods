# Chapter 0052 — Two Hundred and Ten

The Fisher Lane delivery office is on Nether Kiln Lane and it is a room the size of a supermarket with a counter at the front and a cage of trolleys at the back and about nine people in it on a Wednesday morning, and it opens to the public between eleven and four for the two things it does for the public, which are the collection of a parcel and the redirection of a letter.

He was on the pavement outside it at ten past nine, and it was not open to the public, and he knew that, and he went in anyway at four minutes to ten because a door with a counter behind it is a door.

The woman at the counter was about twenty-seven and she had a headset on and a card reader she was using for something else.

"Can I help you?"

"I want to ask about a delivery round."

"You'd be the fourth person this week."

That is the sentence he wrote down afterwards, because it turned out to be the most important thing anybody said to him on Wednesday, and she said it without any weight at all, the way you read a line off a form, and then she looked at him properly for the first time.

"Round?"

"Fisher Lane and Sillick. About two hundred and ten addresses. One man on it." He put his hands flat on the counter, and then took them off it, because there is a printed line on every counter in this business and he had read the one four miles away that morning in his own head. "I'm not here about anything of mine. I'm here about nine of them."

"Right." She did not put the headset down. "Are you the council, are you the electoral people, or are you a lawyer?"

"None of those."

"Then I'm going to have to say that I can only talk to you about an address if you've got the address and the postcode, and I can only look at a round if you're on it, and if you're on a round you'll have a handheld, and if you've got a handheld you don't need me." She said it in the order she had learned it. "There's a form for a query and it's a one-sided form and it's on that board and the address on the board is the address."

Elias Venn looked at the board. It was a printed A4 with three lines of type and a box of paper slips in a plastic holder, and the slips had *DELIVERY QUERY* at the top and five boxes, and one of the boxes said **RELATIONSHIP TO ADDRESS** and one of them said **REASON FOR QUERY** and neither of them said anything about a person.

"What's the relationship box for."

"It's for if it's not your address."

"Right," he said. "And the reason box."

"Reason for query." She looked at him. "You'd write *missing* or *not received* or *wrong name* and I'd look it up on a different screen and I'd tell you what the sheet says, and if it's a name that's come off, I can tell you it's come off, and I can't tell you who took it off or why, because that's not in my system and I don't have a key to it and I'm not going to pretend to."

Nobody in this business has ever pretended to anything in front of him, not once in six weeks, and it is the single most exhausting fact of his working life, and he said, "That's fine."

"That's not fine, love, that's normal, and there's a difference, and the fact that they're the same is what gets people."

---

**The rounds sheet came off a printer at about ten past ten and the woman on the shift above the counter brought it down without being asked, which he did not expect, and which turned out to be because there were four of him in a week and the whole of the procedure was in a drawer next to the printer.**

It is a single side of A3 on continuous fanfold paper, and it is the working sheet of a delivery round, and every delivery operation in this country that involves a person on foot is keyed off something like it, and it is about two hundred and ten lines, and it has nine columns.

> **FISHER LANE & SILLICK — FLS-2 — WALK SHEET. Revised 02 Dec 24.**
> **SEQ · ADDRESS · POSTCODE · NAME · TYPE · BOX · STATUS · LAST ATT · NOTE**

The **NAME** column is a field. It is a field of a fixed width, it holds a name, it is the field a postman reads off the screen, and it is the field that was empty at number 88 on the sixth of December.

Elias Venn read down it twice. He is not a postman and he is not a manager and he is not an inspector and he has no business with a sheet of continuous paper, and he read down it twice because he could not help it, and he was embarrassed, and he did it anyway.

About two hundred and ten lines. He counted the lines because he could not stop himself, and he was aware of counting the lines, and he wrote in the notebook afterwards that he counted the lines twice and that this is what he is now.

Nine of the two hundred and ten had nothing in the NAME column.

Not wrong names. Not one name where another name ought to be. **Nine empty fields**, and four of the nine in one stretch of the sheet, and the four were 84, 88, 90 and 80 Sillick Lane.

"There," said the woman above the counter. "That's what you've come about."

"How long have those been like that."

"Some of them since the sixth and some since the tenth and one since the eleventh." She put her finger on the sheet without touching it. "That one's Broad Street and it went at the end of November, three days, about nine of them, and they're not on your round so you'd not have seen it."

"And the eighth of December one."

"Sunday the eighth — no, the sixth. The sixth of December." She said it without correcting herself, which he noticed, and which was the first thing in two days that made him feel as though he was in the right street. "That one's ours. That one came off in the overnight."

"What happens to a line when it comes off."

"Nothing happens to the line. The line's the line. The address is still on the walk and you still walk past it." She turned the sheet a degree so he could see the STATUS column. "What happens is the NAME goes. And once the name's gone the handheld stops asking for one, and the item can't be completed, and it comes back, and it comes back because the machine won't let anybody knock on a door with a line in it that says there's nobody there." She put her hand flat on the counter next to the printer. "That's the whole of it. That's not a rule anybody made for anybody. That's a handheld refusing to hand a postman an item it can't account for, and it is the best piece of design in this building and I would fight anybody who took it out."

"Refusing to hand a postman an item it can't account for."

"You'd be amazed what's on it that it can't account for. It won't hand over a birthday card to a house that's gone. It won't do it and it says why in a box." She looked at the sheet. "It doesn't ask whether the house has gone. It asks whether we've got somebody to give it to. Those are not the same and that is the entire problem and it has been the entire problem since about 1994 and I have never once been able to say it to anybody who wanted to hear it."

**Nobody wanted to hear it. That is on the page because it is what happened.**

Elias Venn stood at a counter on Nether Kiln Lane and heard a competent woman in her twenties say the shape of the thing he had come to ask about, and he was the fourth person that week, and the three before him had been a woman whose mother had not come back from a pharmacy, and a man who had come about a redirection, and somebody who had not said.

He wrote it down in his head, because he had not got a pencil out and did not want to have one out, and he had a rule about devices and he had a rule about other people's screens and he had been the reason a device came out of a coat once in six weeks and he was not going to be the reason a screen got looked at twice.

Then he did the arithmetic, and it took him about eleven seconds, and it is the worst of everything he has done since the sixth of October and he knows it while he is doing it.

About two hundred and ten addresses on a round. Nine of them with the name gone, in a fortnight, and nine more on a road in Broad Street three days at the end of November, and he did the division without meaning to, and got about a third, and then he stopped, because he knew what he had just done.

**He had taken a third of a street off a piece of paper, in his head, in about eleven seconds, and written a word for it, and the word was a fraction and the fraction was of households.**

Hattie Bramm had stood in a public gallery on the morning of the twenty-seventh of November and said a number that a foreman had called at a gate in the rain in 1974, and a municipal conciliation officer had ruled on the Thursday evening, in a room, out loud, that **no figure is to be stated in the same breath as another figure from the same source, and that no one is to state a difference, because a difference is an adjudication, and she did not adjudicate, and she said she would rule against it if it was offered.**

And Elias Venn had taken a fraction off a delivery sheet in a delivery office and had not said it out loud and had not written it in the notebook and had put his hand flat on a counter instead.

That was all. That was the whole of it. He had spent six weeks learning that a number in a room is a thing with a name against it and that the counting is what catches the name, and he was on a Wednesday afternoon doing the counting on the wrong side of the paper, for nobody, and there was no room, and no form, and nobody who could rule against it.

"The sheet's right," said the woman above the counter, from about four feet away, in a voice that had gone somewhere he had not been.

"Sorry?"

"The sheet's right. That's what the depot says and the depot's not wrong." She said it without heat, because she had said it before, this week, to three people. "Every address on there exists. Every address on there is a place you can walk to. Nobody has invented an address. If the name's off then either the name's been taken off or the name's been cleared, and there's a difference between those two things and only one of them is a person and the other one is a field." She put her hand on the edge of the sheet. "There's a change log. You can have the change log. I can print the change log and I can read it out and I can tell you the date and the time and the device and the operation, and I cannot tell you a name, because the name is in the field operations system and I have got a window onto it and not a key to it, and I am not going to say a woman's name to a man on a counter for a thing I have not been asked about by her employer."

"Print it."

"That's a lot of paper for nine."

"Print it," said Elias Venn. "And if it's more than nine then it's less than a lot."

> **2291 · 28 NOV · 15:58 · BWD-1 · BROAD ST · 6–9 · NAME · *(populated)* · *(cleared)* · NCO · OP-2201**
> **1402 · 06 DEC · 03:41 · FLS-2 · 88 SILLICK LN · NAME · *(populated)* · *(cleared)* · NCO · *(batch)* — see note**
> **1408 · 10 DEC · 16:04 · FLS-2 · 84 SILLICK LN · NAME · *(populated)* · *(cleared)* · NCO · OP-2201**

The refresh clears the occupier field on any record carrying NCO set in the previous 24 hours. Set on 05/12 at 16:22 by OP-2201, source record not visible to this office. I am not chasing it. R.M.*

And the reason code, in the printed legend at the foot of the log, because codes have legends and this is the one code in the whole of the business that this batch is about:

> **NCO — NO CURRENT OCCUPANT. Set by walk survey only. Consequence: record closed to delivery; occupier field not required; item returned or offered to neighbour.**

**Set by walk survey only.** Four words printed at the foot of a log, and underneath them a set of consequences that nobody wrote down in November and that everybody has obeyed ever since, correctly, in about two hundred and ten addresses a week.

"So it takes a walk survey," Elias Venn said. "Somebody has to have gone."

"It takes a walk survey. Somebody goes to the door, knocks, gets an answer or doesn't, and then either they set it or they don't."

"Who's OP-2201."

"That's an operator."

"That's a number, not a person."

"It's an operator," said the woman above the counter, "and I will tell you what I know about operators, and I know a lot, because I have run a floor with two hundred and thirty of them on it. An operator number is a person, and the person is on the field operations contract, and the field operations contract is not this building's contract. The name is in the field operations system. This office has got a window onto that system with a view of about six fields and one of them is the number, and I have not got a name, and I have asked, and I was told no, and the reason I was told no is that a name is released to the business that employs the operator and not to the public, and that is a data protection thing and it is a real thing and I am not going to have a go at the man who enforces it, because he is right and I would want him to do it to me."

"Then somebody knows."

"Somebody knows," she said. "And it's not a mystery, and I want to say that to you before you go away up that street with a piece of paper, because you look like a man who's going to go and look for somebody to be angry at and there isn't one, and if there is one then he's a person who pressed a key on an ordinary weekday at about four in the afternoon, and there are about two hundred and thirty of them and they press about eleven thousand keys a day, and I could not pick one out of that for you and neither could the field operations system if you asked it nicely."

"Have you got the *times* on it."

"I've got the times on it and you're looking at them and you can see them." She turned the log a degree. "Broad Street was the Tuesday, the Wednesday and the Thursday at about four o'clock in the afternoon. Yours were the Saturday at four o'clock and the Thursday at four o'clock. There's one at about ten to ten on a Friday morning, which is somebody in a hurry, and there's one on the sixth at twenty to four in the morning, which is the file, not a person, and that's my note and I've put my initials on it because if I don't then somebody will."

Elias Venn looked at the log for a long time, and what he was looking at was not the codes.

It was that every single line in it is at about four in the afternoon on an ordinary weekday, and the sixth of December at twenty to four in the morning, and the eleventh of December at about ten to ten in the morning, and that he had spent six weeks learning that the instruments in this business are all the same instruments — a field, a date, a person, a line — and that he had spent six weeks believing that a person who can do an impossible thing to a record is the only kind of person who can do harm to a record, and that the field operations system has a **walk survey** as its only route to the code, and that a walk survey means a person, and a door, and a knock, and an answer.

**And that nobody in this business has ever gone to that door and knocked and asked whether it was still a home, and that includes him, on Sunday, at ten to twelve, when he knocked on number 88 and a man answered and he asked him a question about a job.**

The change log is four lines per event and it is not a document anybody gives out, and he asked for it, and the woman said no, and the reason she said no was given to him in about nine words and was correct.

"You're not on the round, you've not got a postcode in that line, and I've got four forms' worth of reasons on that board and none of them is *a man who came in." She was not unkind about it. "That sheet goes to a business. It doesn't go home with anybody. If I put it in a bin bag in this street then in a fortnight there's a man at a hearing with a printer's copy of an extract of a field operations log and no idea where it came from, and that's not a piece of paper, that's a weapon with no name on it, and I have been in this job nine years and I have watched that happen twice."

"Then I have nothing."

"You have what I've got on the back of a receipt and I'm allowed to do that because that's my own handwriting on my own paper and it is not the sheet, and if anybody asks me where it came from I will say a man in a coat asked me for it and I said it out loud in a room with a printer in it, and I would say it again." She tore a receipt off the roll, turned it over, and wrote on the back of it in the flat hand of a woman who writes down postcodes all day, in about forty seconds, and slid it across.

> **FLS-2 walk sheet extract, 16.12.24, 09:52. NCO = no current occupant, walk survey only.**
> **26/11 16:04 · 27/11 16:11 · 28/11 15:58 — Broad Street, 1–9, OP-2201.**

He read it standing up, twice, and then he said thank you, and she said something that he wrote down afterwards because he could not stop it going round.

"You've been the fourth one this week and you're the first one who's read the code legend. That's the whole of my week. Four people, and one of them read the legend, and it's printed at the bottom of a sheet of paper in a bin in a room nobody goes in." She put her headset back on. "Whoever you are, you're not the first person who's wanted somebody to be angry at, and the truthful thing is there's nobody to be angry at, and the second truthful thing is that that's worse, and if you're the sort of man who can hold both of those at once then you'll be all right and if you're not then don't come back, because I've got a counter."

Elias Venn said, "I'm the sort of man who's all right," which was a lie, and she did not check it, and neither did he.

---

He got the tram to the top of the hill at about eleven and walked down and stood in the entrance of a public museum for a pound, and he did not go in, and he stood in the lobby for about four minutes with a receipt in his hand with writing on the back of it that a stranger had put there out loud in a room with a printer in.

**And then he did the thing he has done for six weeks, and he did it in a lobby, and he counted rooms.**

Not objects. He had not counted objects since the seventh of November. Rooms. How many doors he had been behind, and how many of them had had a form on them, and how many of those forms had a box on them for a person, and whether anybody's name was in the box.

The lobby. One. The Hall of Former Faiths. Two. The four. The nine. The forty-one. The hundred and forty. The two hundred and ten.

He stood in a lobby and counted, and he could not stop, and he was embarrassed, and he did it anyway, and then he wrote the honest version of the fact on the back of nothing, because he had not got a page in front of him, and carried it up the hill in his head until about two in the afternoon, when he wrote it out properly at a table in a rented room and had to stop halfway through and go and stand in the middle of the floor.

> **Wed 16 Dec, 15:20. I counted.**
> **FLS-2, about 210 addresses. Nine with the name gone in twenty-one days, four of them Sillick Lane. Nine more on Broad Street, three on two roads I have never been to. Twenty-one in three weeks. Nobody in any of those three systems has called it anything.**
> **And I worked out, at the counter, in about eleven seconds, that the east side of Sillick Lane has fourteen doors and the sheet has thirteen because there is no 86, and that four of the thirteen have no name on them. About a third.**
> **And the sixth of November ruling is that a difference is an adjudication, and Marsh said she did not adjudicate, and said she would rule against it. There is no room, no form, and nobody who can rule against it, and I did the adjudication anyway, at a counter, in my head, about strangers, on a Wednesday.**
> **That is the whole of what I am on the sixteenth of December. I am a man who takes things off lists.**

And then, underneath it, in the same hand and the same pen and about four minutes later, the second half of the entry, and it is the sentence the whole of Volume 02 is built out of and he had no idea of that when he wrote it:

> **And the only thing anybody in any of the three systems has offered to do about it is a woman on a counter who said the second truthful thing is worse, and she was right, and she is not going to be asked again, and she said so, and she was right about that too.**
> **Nobody has taken anybody off anything. A field that was full has become empty, and every system that reads it has been correct the whole time, and the man on the round was told on Sunday that the sheet's right, and he has believed it his whole life, and he is the only witness anybody has.**
> **A name field is not a place. A name field is a hook, and a hook does not know what is on it, and the only way to find out what is on a hook is to pick it up, and I am the only person in this business who can pick things up, and I put it down in a public gallery on the twenty-seventh of November in front of eighty people and I said the rest of it as well.**
