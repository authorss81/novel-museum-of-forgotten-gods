# Chapter 0087 — Vera Pilling Goes Back

The clinic had her out of the door at about twenty to eleven on the morning of Thursday the fifteenth of April 2025 with a piece of paper in her coat pocket that said somebody was meeting her and where and when she was expected back, and a woman from the clinic walked her to the pavement because the clinic's rule is that somebody walks people to the pavement, and then the woman got back in the car and the car went off up the hill.

**She was eighty-one. She lived at number six on that street and had lived there for about forty-one years. She had been in the clinic since Wednesday the thirteenth of January, which is thirteen weeks and one day, and the day she went in was the day the Hall of Former Faiths on Civic Hill reopened and about a hundred and forty people went through a door, and she was told on Tuesday the twenty-sixth of January, by a woman of thirty-one with a satchel, *you go back to the house, not next year, this spring*, and that was fifty-one days after the morning the field cleared, which is seven weeks and two days.**

She had been standing on that street on the eighth of December for about an hour and a quarter, in the cold, at about four in the afternoon, and somebody had noticed.

Nobody has ever asked her about that. She has not brought it up since January and she is not going to bring it up on Thursday morning, and what everybody in this case knows about it is a date, a street, and the words *about an hour and a quarter*, and the reason everybody knows it is that a person with a duty wrote it down in about four seconds on a form in a corridor.

---

There were three people on that pavement and one of them was not supposed to be.

The one from the clinic was a woman of about thirty with a lanyard and a printed sheet and a duty, and the printed sheet had four lines on it, and one of the four lines was a length of time, and it was twenty-five minutes, and the time started when they stopped walking.

"Right," she said. "**Twenty-five minutes. I'll be at the end of the road. If you want to sit, there's a bench at the top. If you've had enough, say so and that's the whole of it, you don't have to give a reason and nobody's going to write one.**"

"Thank you."

"**And you don't have to speak to anybody.**"

"We know," said Mairin Devlin.

The other two were a registration officer of thirty-one and a man of thirty-two in a coat, and the man of thirty-two was not supposed to be there, and the reason he was not supposed to be there is that nobody had asked him whether he wanted to be.

**He had come because a woman of about thirty-four who lives at number four in a road called Broad Street told him in a library on the seventh of April that Vera Pilling was going back on the fifteenth, and he did not ask her how she knew, and she did not say, and that is the whole of what happened between those two people and neither of them will ever refer to it again.**

The registration officer said the thing that has to be said when a person with a duty is standing on a pavement with a person who does not have one, and she said it to the man in the coat and not to the woman of eighty-one.

"**You are going to stand about nine feet away and you are not going to ask her anything,**" she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"**I know. I am saying it out loud so that there are two of us who have agreed it, because in about four minutes one of us is going to want to.**"

---

## The street is about two hundred yards long and there is no bus down it and it was the fifteenth of April and it was about eleven in the morning

She went slowly. She did not use a stick and she had a coat on and a hat on that she did not need, and she went up the east side and stopped twice, and both times she stopped at a door and neither time she knocked, and both times the stopping was about four seconds long and both times the man in the coat counted them and was embarrassed by the counting.

**About nine flats on the east side. The numbers do not run in order, because this street grew around a mill gate and has never once been renumbered. There is no eighty-six and there never has been. About eleven letter boxes and a row of flats and a road that goes down to the water, and about two hundred yards of it.**

They got to eighty at about ten past eleven.

"**Do you want to go in,**" said Mairin Devlin.

"No."

"**Do you want to stand on the step.**"

"**I want to knock,**" said Vera Pilling.

---

**And she knocked, and she waited, and nobody opened it for about nine seconds, and then the door opened about four inches and there was a woman in it, and the woman of eighty-one did not say anything at all for about four seconds, which the registration officer noticed and the man in the coat noticed and neither of them has ever described to anybody.**

Then she said a name.

It was a man's name and it was not a first name anybody in this case had ever been given and it was a name that has been in a file in a building four miles away since the fourth of February and it was on a sheet of A4 in a plastic sleeve in an inquiry file in a building on a hill, in a registrar's own handwriting, in a column headed OCCUPANT (PRIOR), with a witness's initials at the foot and no accession number, and **the woman of eighty-one said it first, on the fourth of January, at that same door, and the person who opened it said *Who?*, and she was told *Who?*, and nobody has asked her since.**

The door opened a bit further.

"Say again," said the woman in it.

So she said it again.

---

**Nobody asked her anything about the case. That is the finding of the fifteenth of April 2025 and it is not a small thing and it took three people about eleven minutes to not do it, and one of the three was the woman of eighty-one.**

The woman in the doorway asked her in. Vera Pilling said no. The woman in the doorway said something about the weather and shut the door, and stood behind it, and said through it:

"There's a woman living here now. She'll be in at half four."

"Thank you."

"**I'm not saying you were right about him because I don't know anything about him. I'm saying there's a woman here now and that's all I know.**"

"That's all I wanted."

---

**And then the registrar asked her a question, and the registrar asked it properly, and she said no.**

Mara Iles had not been on the pavement. She had been standing in the road at the top of the street for about twenty minutes with a lanyard of her own round her neck, because she had come as a member of the public and had paid nothing and had been on notice and in company, and she had said nothing for twenty minutes, and at about a quarter past eleven she walked down and asked the only question anybody was going to ask that morning.

"Mrs Pilling. I am the registrar of a museum. I am not from the council and I am not from the post office and I have no badge worth the name. There is a record in my building of a man who had that flat, and I put his name on a piece of paper in February because the district's file says he was there, and I have never had a person to support it. You are the only person in this city who has ever said that name at that door. **Would you write it down for me.**"

The pavement was quiet for about nine seconds.

"No," said Vera Pilling.

"**May I say why you are being asked, and it is about four seconds, and then I will not ask you anything else today.**"

"You may say it."

"**A name in a museum is a hook. It is small, and it is printed, and it goes in an envelope, and a hook does not know what is on it. If I put a dead man's name on a public card about number eighty, then in about four years the next person who comes to that door is a person living at an address with a dead man's name on it in a public building, and somebody will pick that hook up inside a department and do a correct thing with it.**" She did not raise her voice. "**And you are eighty-one and you are dead, and it would not be about you. It would be about whoever is in that flat after you and whoever is in it now.**"

"No," said Vera Pilling, for the second time, and then she said why, and it took about eleven seconds and it was the fourth time anybody in this case has refused a thing for a reason that is about what a document does to a person afterwards.

"**I have lived on this street for forty-one years and I watched them empty about nine houses on it in one December and I have seen what it does to a door to have a name on it that somebody has to come and ask about,**" she said. "**There is a woman in that flat and she has been in it since about January and she is going to be in it when I am not. And if your museum puts Walter Naylor on a card about number eighty, then the day somebody comes to that door with a clipboard — and somebody will, in about four years, because that is what a name is for — that woman is going to be asked about a man she has never heard of and she is going to have to say no, and a no about a dead man is a harder thing to say than a no about a bin.**"

"**That is the whole of my reason,**" she said, "**and it is not a moral and I am not being brave. I would do it gladly if there was nobody in that flat. I have said that name out loud at that door twice today and once in January and I have said it to a person, not to a wall.**"

---

**And the registrar said the cost out loud, in about nine seconds, on a pavement, in front of four people, because that is what she does and she has done it in this case in a corridor and in a committee room and in a store and she was not going to stop doing it because the surface had changed.**

"**Then it is not done, and I am going to say the cost of that before I say anything else, because I have said the cost before every decision I have taken since the twelfth of November and I am not going to make an exception on a pavement in April.**"

"Go on."

"**The cost is that a name stays in a district's field and in one sheet of A4 in a plastic sleeve in an inquiry file in a building on a hill, written in my hand, supported by a systems technician's initials, and supported by nothing else, and the district throws that value away every morning and has done since November, and about four years from now the only place in this city where the name of Walter Naylor of number eighty exists is a comparison sheet a member of the public may ask to see.**"

"And it might be wrong."

"**It might be wrong. It is almost certainly incomplete. And I have just been told by the only person who could have corrected it that she will not, and her reason is better than mine, and she is eighty-one, and she is right.**"

"Then what do you do."

"**I write on a card that it is unsupported beyond the district's retained prior value, and that a person who knew the man declined to be recorded, and I do not put the reason on it, because the reason is hers and because a reason on a card is a handle.**" She put the folder under her arm. "**Which means there will be a card about number eighty in a public room with a name on it and a line under it that says nobody would stand behind it, and the hole is the record, and I have been told by a woman in a label room that a hole is the most dangerous thing a card can have on it, because a hole lets whoever comes next fill it in.**"

"Then how does that help her."

"**It does not help her. It is a card that will be wrong on purpose and it is the only one I am able to produce and it is the best I can do with a no.**"

## The clinic's twenty-five minutes ran out at about four minutes to twelve and the woman from the clinic was at the end of the road the whole time

They walked her back. She went the whole length of the street and stopped at about nine feet from the door, and Mairin Devlin did not say anything for the whole two hundred yards, and the man of thirty-two said about nine words in two hundred yards, and they were: *it's a long street for a Thursday.*

At the end of the road the woman from the clinic had the car door open and the printed sheet in her hand and she said:

"**Right. Well done. You were out for about twenty-five minutes and you sat down once and you said you were tired once and I have written both of those on here, and there is no reason written next to either of them, and that is the sheet.**"

"Thank you," said Vera Pilling, and got in, and the car went off up the hill.
