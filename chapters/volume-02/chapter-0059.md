# Chapter 0059 — The Threshold

On Monday the fourth of January 2025, at about ten past three in the afternoon, when it was still just light enough in Sillick Lane to read a number on a door, a woman of eighty-one stood on the step of number 88 and said the name of that house out loud in the street, to whoever came to the door.

That is the whole of it. That is the entire practice, and it took about eleven minutes to explain to Elias Venn on a pavement, and he was one of about nine people on the pavement, and he was the ninth, and he had come at about six on the Friday and it was properly dark and the practice had been off for about an hour and a half.

---

It started on the fourth of January, which was the first ordinary working day of the year, because nobody authorised it and because there was nothing to authorise.

The mechanism is this and it is not a mystery and it is not a god.

A household whose name field is empty cannot be delivered to. A household whose name field is empty does not get a council's letter, a doctor's letter, a school's letter, an electoral return, a bank statement that arrives, a card from a museum, a determination notice, or a registered letter from anybody. A household that cannot be delivered to is not a household that anybody goes and sees, because a person goes and sees a household by writing to it, and the writing comes back, and after the writing comes back twice the whole system stops trying, correctly, because that is what a system is for.

**And then, on the sixteenth of December, a woman on a counter on Nether Kiln Lane wrote four lines of a change log out on the back of a receipt in her own hand because a man in a coat asked her for them, and a systems technician counted four hundred and some dead letters on a Monday because he had nothing else to do, and a registrar found that a school keeps the best name-and-place book in the county on a corridor wall in a hardback, and a woman of eighty-one sat in her own kitchen on the afternoon of New Year's Day and worked out the one thing that none of those four people could do.**

**Somebody has to say it. Not write it. Not key it. Not enter it in a field. Somebody has to stand on a step and say a name out loud in a street, to a person at a door, in front of witnesses, so that a person is a person on a Tuesday and not a field on a wire.**

The rules are three and they were arrived at in about a quarter of an hour on a doorstep and they are on a piece of A4 in a plastic wallet in a hall in Sillick Lane, and they are not minuted anywhere, and they are on the page because they are the whole of the practice and because a practice with three rules in it and no form under it is the shape of the whole of this case.

> **THE MAT. Sillick Lane. In use.**
> **1. *Named.*** We call it standing on the mat. It is not the museum's and it is not a museum's and it has nothing to do with the museum and it is not a practice of this council or of any office and nobody has authorised it and nobody is going to.
> **2. *Timed.*** From three o'clock until you cannot read a number off a door. After that it is off for the day. Nobody goes at night. Nobody goes in the dark. That is the whole of the timing and it is a light rule and not a clock rule, and you can keep it in your head, and in January it is about an hour and a half and in June it is nine.
> **3. *Abandonable.*** Anybody taking part may stop at any point, and stopping is not a failure, and nobody is to be asked afterwards why they stopped or why they did not go, and nobody is to be asked to say they have done it.

And there is a fourth thing, which is not a rule, and which is the only instrument the practice has.

**Whoever says a name writes it down and takes it home and puts it on a sheet on a table in the hall.** Not a form. A sheet. A name, an address, a date, a time and the hand it was written in. On the table, under the post, in the hall, where a person waiting for something can see it.

---

**There is no form for it, and the reason there is no form for it is not that nobody has got round to it. It is that nobody in this city has a form for a person standing in their own doorway, and five institutions were asked, and all five said no, and all five were right, and the reasons are on the page because the reasons are the argument.**

The Museum of Quiet Saints, asked on Thursday the seventh of January by a woman of fifty-four with a plastic wallet, on a telephone, by a registrar of twenty-eight: no, and the reason given by a registrar of twenty-eight on a telephone is that **it is not a practice, it is a relationship, and this institution has a category for the first and no category for the second, and the category it has is *Schedule 12, carried object, no activity observed in the period of inspection*, and putting a person's street into that would be a lie on a public card and we cut one of those in October and it is still lying on a plinth.**

The municipal conciliation service, asked on the seventh: no, and the reason given by a field officer is that **it is not a claim, it is not a dispute, it is not a matter of payments, and I have a form for a person who says they have been underpaid and I have a form for a person who says a record is wrong, and you would be the first person in nine years to bring me a third thing, and the third thing would last about a fortnight and then it would be a thing people say they did in a room in January.**

The district council, whose addressing service supplies the file that the Museum and the electoral roll and the council tax base are all built on, which was rung twice: no comment, and the person who rang back said that the occupancy field is maintained from returns and walk surveys and **is not a function of this authority**, and said it in the tone of a person reading a sentence off a form, and it is true.

The post office: no, and the counter at Nether Kiln Lane said, on the seventh, in a room with a printer in it, that **the round is a delivery round and there is no such thing as a non-delivery visit, and if you want your field put back you want a walk survey and a walk survey is a person going to your door, and the form for that is on the board, and the relationship box on it is where you say who you are to the address, and you are the person to the address, so you can fill that in yourself, and there is no charge and it is about four working days.** And she said that last part twice because she had never said it out loud before and she wanted to be sure he had it.

And the library back room: the woman of fifty-four has the Friday list in pencil, and the Friday list is twenty names, and it is the people who turn up, and it is not kept.

**So there is no form for it, and there is a form for putting a name back in a field, and the two are four working days apart, and nobody on that street has ever been told about either.**

---

**The first person to try it was not Elias Venn and it was not a woman of fifty-four, and it was Vera Pilling of number 6, who is eighty-one years old, and it was on the first Monday of the year, and it cost her her chest.**

She did it at about ten past three on the fourth, on the first afternoon there was, having written the four names out herself on a piece of paper the Friday before.

80. 84. 88. 90. Four doors with no names in them and four names in a woman's handwriting going back about fifty years.

She said it at 84 and somebody answered, and the somebody was seventy-one and had been in that flat since 1988 and had a name, and Vera Pilling said it out loud on his own step and he said "aye" and let her in, and she stayed about nine minutes and had a cup of tea, and she came out and wrote it on the sheet on the table in her own hall with the date and the time and the fact that she had gone in.

She did it at 88 at about half past three on the fourth and Frank Dunning answered and said, "You'll have heard."

"Frank Dunning. Number eighty-eight. Monday. I did it on the Monday and I'm telling you on the Friday because a name in a street is worth nothing on the day it's said."

"You've not said it out loud in about nine years, have you."

"**No," said Vera Pilling. "And I'll tell you the thing about that, because you're the first one on this street that's said anything to me since the spring. I have not been called anything in this street for nine years, not by post and not by mouth, because the post stopped and because the mouths in this street are all the same age as me and we've all got the same thing wrong with our ears.**"

**That is what the practice does. Not a god. A mouth.**

And then she got to 90 and there was nobody in 90 and she said the name anyway, to a shut door, at about ten to four in the afternoon, in a street with a wind coming up it off the water.

And then she got to 80, and that is the one that is on the page.

**There is a man living at 80 who came into that flat in April, and the field at 80 was never updated for him, because a tenancy does not notify an addressing service and nobody has ever thought about why not, and the field held the name of the man who had that flat before him until the eleventh of December, and that man has been dead since about 2019. So for eight months the field at 80 was correct about a dead man, and the post came, and the postman knocked, and the man who lives there now has had his post for eight months because a man of about fifty knocked on a door and asked a question, and nobody has ever written down that any of that is what happened.**

And Vera Pilling said the name that was in the field, and the man opened the door, and the man said: "Who?"

And an old woman of eighty-one stood on a doorstep in January and found out that she had said a dead man's name to a living man, in the street, in front of witnesses, and that the field had held a name for about forty years and had been correct, and that she had put it back on a doorstep anyway without asking whether anybody was there.

"Well, that's that," she said afterwards. "**That's the whole of what's wrong with it and I'll not have it said for me. You can't just say a name. You've to knock first, and then you say the name, and if a body's there you say the name to them, and if there's no body you say it to nobody and you write it down and you go home.**"

Then she went home, and she did her own front step as well, at about ten past four, in the last of the light, and then on the Friday the eighth she went out again and got as far as the wall at the bottom of the lane and had to sit down on it for about four minutes.

She went to the clinic on the Monday the eleventh, and the clinic is on Fisher Lane and the woman who ran it had been coming to the Friday queue at the library for six years and did not know she was going to be in the story.

"Her heart," said the clinic. "Not a collapse. A woman of eighty-one stood on a doorstep in the cold wind for about an hour and a quarter and her heart did what a heart of eighty-one does. She's to have it monitored and she's to stop and she's not to stand about in the cold. **And she'll do what she's told for about eleven weeks and then she'll be back, because she is eighty-one and she has never in her life been told to stop.**"

**And that is the cost, and it is the whole of the cost, and it is a body, and it belongs to the first person who tried it, and she was eighty-one, and nobody had asked her, and the practice has abandonability in it and she used it, and the practice carried on without her on the Friday, which is what a practice with a third rule in it is supposed to do.**

---

**Elias Venn came down the hill at about six on the Friday the eighth of January and it was properly dark and the practice had been off for about an hour and a half, and he was on a pavement in Sillick Lane with about nine people in it, and he was the ninth, and nobody had asked him to do anything.**

He had been asked, in fact. He had been asked on the Thursday morning at a table in a rented room, by telephone, by Mara Iles, in ninety-one seconds, and he had said yes, and he had then spent two days finding out that he had no idea what yes meant.

"Is anybody writing this down anywhere," she had said. "Not for us. I want to be clear. This is not a Museum practice and it is not going in a Museum file and if anybody sends me a form about it I will send it back."

"No."

"And Elias. **If you go, you will need a word.** You have not got one. I have not given you one and I am not going to, and I have thought about that for two days and I have not changed my mind, and I want you to hear the reason on a Thursday in January and not in a room in a crisis in March."

"I know the reason."

"Then say it back."

"Because the practice has to be stoppable by somebody who is not the man doing it, and if I have got a word then the word is mine, and a thing that can be stopped by the man who is doing it has not been stopped, it has been interrupted, and you wrote that on a pad on the twenty-seventh of November and copied it off a man of twenty-five and took the credit for it."

"There," said Mara Iles. "**And it is the reason, and you have never once heard me say it about a man who is not in a room, and there it is.**"

"That's fine. I've not got one anyway."

"You have not got one anyway," she said, and then stopped, and then said the other thing, which is the sentence she telephoned on the seventh of January to say and which is on the page because he has never been told it and nobody else is ever going to be.

"**Elias. Nobody has ever given you a word because there has never been one to give. You did not have one in October and you did not learn one in November and you have not learned one now and nobody is going to, and I am the person who would have to, and I have decided that I will not, and it is not a kindness and it is not a punishment. It is because a word that comes from a registrar in a building four miles away is not a word anybody in Sillick Lane can use at four o'clock in the afternoon when it is getting dark.**"

"Well, they have got one," said Elias Venn.

"What?"

"They've got one. It's not mine and it isn't yours. Somebody on that street wrote it on a piece of A4 in a plastic wallet, and the third rule says anybody taking part can stop, and that means the word is *anybody's*, and it isn't a word, it's a *permission*, and I have not got a permission, I have got a coat and a pound and a notebook with nine pages in it that cost thirty-five pence and I cannot even give the mat back to a man because the room was shut."

---

**So he stood on a pavement in Sillick Lane at about ten past six on the eighth of January 2025 in the dark, with nine people, and he was the ninth, and the practice was off because it was dark and that is the whole of why it was off.**

He did not go to a door. He said, afterwards, that he had wanted to, and that he had stood on a pavement for about forty minutes and worked out that he was the only person in that street who could not do it, and that the reason was not the bar and was not the pound.

**The reason is that the practice is a record with a name against it, made by a person, at a time, in front of a witness. And he cannot be the person who makes it, because a record with his name on it — Venn, E. — is a record about a man who is in a disciplinary file four miles away, and a woman of fifty-four has said out loud in a room of eighty people that a name in a Museum file is a thing that happens to a person, and she was right, and he has been on the wrong end of it for six weeks and knows exactly what it costs.**

So he did the next thing, which is the thing he has been doing since the sixth of October, and he did it at about a quarter to seven on a pavement in the dark with no light and no notebook out because he has not taken a notebook onto a street at night since October and is not going to start, and he stood there and held it in his head for an hour, and it is on the page because he wrote it out the next morning and it is the only entry in a hundred and fifteen pages that he has ever written from memory.

---

**And at about half past six, at number 14 Sillick Lane, in a front room with a fire on, he gave Cliff Sowerby a registered letter.**

He had had it since the thirtieth of October. It was in the inside pocket of his coat with the acknowledgment of delivery counterfoil in his name and the address of the conciliation service in Ferry Bank on it, and it had been in nine coats and four rooms and a bus and a hospital car park and a laundrette, and it had gone to Doreen Sarn's table and come back and gone to Irving Quay and come back, and he had never opened it, and he had never asked what was in it, and nobody had ever asked him for it.

Nobody had ever told Cliff Sowerby that it existed. Cliff Sowerby had been told about a printed card on the twenty-eighth of November by a woman of fifty-four who had carried it in her coat for four weeks, and that was the only notice he had had of anything, and he had asked one question about it, *is it the one off the poster*, and he was right to.

"I'm not going to give you this in a corridor," he said. "I'm going to knock, and I'm going to stand there, and I'm going to hand it to you, and you're going to open it, and I'm going to watch you read it because I have to know that you read it and you have to know that I knew that you hadn't."

Cliff Sowerby read it standing up, in about eleven seconds, and it was four lines from a firm of solicitors at fourteen Lincoln's Lane and it told him that a schedule of interim payments had been opened in the matter of payments for the final trading week of October 1974, that a line had been opened in his name, that the line was public, and that he could come to the conciliation office on any weekday between ten and four and ask to see it.

"That's it?"

"That's it. There's a counterfoil."

Cliff Sowerby gave it back.

"Right," he said. "**I have been on a list since the Thursday before I came to that gallery and nobody has said so, and I have got a stamp on my hand in biro from the thirtieth of October, and I have been carrying it round for two months.**" He looked at the paper. "You could have brought it round in November."

"No."

"No," Cliff Sowerby agreed. "You couldn't have. It wasn't yours and it wasn't a Tuesday." He put it on the sideboard next to the teapot, which is where a document goes in that house, and he did not put it in a drawer, and Elias Venn noticed that he did not put it in a drawer and understood about four years later why.

---

**And the last thing that happened in Sillick Lane on the evening of the eighth of January 2025 was a man of sixty-five standing on his own step at about twenty to seven, in the dark, on his own, because it was his front door and there was nobody at it, saying his own name out loud into a street, to nobody, at about seventy decibels, and it is the last image of the first movement of this book and it is not a god and it is not a haunting and it does not work.**

It does not work. Nothing happens. Nobody hears it. A man of about sixty-five says his own name on his own doorstep in the dark in January and the street hears it and does not remember it and the delivery office does not update and the district feed at four o'clock the next morning has a new row and no new occupancy field, and Frank Dunning's field at eighty-eight Sillick Lane is still empty and will be on Tuesday.

**And it is the right thing to have done, and there is no evidence that it is the right thing to have done, and a man of sixty-five did it about four hundred times a year for the next eleven months and told nobody except two people, one of whom wrote it down, in a notebook, in a rented room in Low Ward, on the ninth of January, at about half past eleven at night, with no light and a pencil that had been at a museum label room in October and had not been used for anything else since.**

Then Frank Dunning said the thing that is the honest floor of the entire first movement of this book, and he said it to a man with a notebook standing on a pavement, and nobody asked him a question.

"You'll have noticed we've all got our doors done and the ones we've not got are the ones that aren't on the sheet," he said. "**The ones on the sheet, we can get to. The ones off it, we've not got a door.**"

"How many are off it."

"Nine on a road called Broad Street, and I don't know the street, and I don't know anybody on it, and there's a man on a round who's told me about it and he's not the only one." He looked up the lane towards the corner. "**And that's the whole of it, and there's no fixing it, and I'll tell you why and it's the last true thing I'll say to you this winter. You can only knock on a door you know is a door. And you know it's a door because it's on a list. And the ones that aren't on the list have had it taken off, and taken off by a machine, and nobody's keeping a copy of the list that was there before.**"

"Somebody kept a copy."

"Somebody kept a copy," said Frank Dunning. "**It is in brown paper bands in a box behind a counter in a building you've been barred out of since the first of December, and it's got four hundred and twelve names on it, and it is the only list in this city with those names on it, and it is in a Museum, and you are the only person I have ever met who has ever read it.**"
