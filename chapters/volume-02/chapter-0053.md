# Chapter 0053 — The Queue

There is a back room at the Sillick Lane branch library with a table in it and six chairs and a kettle that works, and it is booked on Fridays from ten until about half past one by a woman of fifty-four who books it under her own name with her own money and who has been doing that for longer than anybody in the building has been there.

It is not a meeting. There is no agenda and no minutes and no chair at the head of the table, and there is a sheet of A4 on the table with about twenty names down it in pencil, and the pencil gets rubbed out and rewritten every week, and it does not go in a drawer and it does not go in a file and nobody has ever asked to see it.

There were about twenty people in that room at ten past ten on the morning of Friday the eighteenth of December 2024, and about nine of them had come up off Sillick Lane on foot.

Elias Venn was not in the room. He was in the corridor outside it for fifty-one minutes, and the reason he was in the corridor and not in the room is the chapter.

---

He got there at ten past nine, and Hattie Bramm was already at the counter with her coat on, and she saw him across a public library at about twenty past nine on a Friday morning and came straight over, which he had not expected, and said:

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You've got a face on you like the sixth of November." She put her bag on the counter. "You've had a face like that since about half past six on the sixth of November and I have watched you have it through four windows, and whatever it is, it is not for this room."

"What is it, then."

"It's for a room with a form in it and a person behind the table, and there is no such room in this building and there has not been one since the works library closed, and I have been doing this in a back room with a kettle for nine years because it is the only room in Low Ward a person can be in without a form." She took her coat off and hung it on the newel post of the counter, which is not a thing you are supposed to do and which she has done for years. "You'll stand in the corridor."

"Hattie—"

"You'll stand in the corridor," said Hattie Bramm, "and I'll come out every twenty minutes and tell you what I'm saying, and if you put your head round that door once I will stop and turn round and I will be right, and I have been right about you in a room about nine times and you have never once been able to improve on it, and I have a Friday."

And that was the end of it, and she went and unlocked the back room, and Elias Venn stood in a public library corridor for fifty-one minutes on a Friday morning in December with his hands in his pockets and a receipt in his coat with writing on the back of it, and the corridor had a radiator that was not working and a notice about the closure of the local studies room that had been up since the spring.

**It is the whole of where he is, and it is not a temporary condition, and he had known that for seven weeks and had not written it down until that Friday.**

He cannot be in the Museum. He cannot enter the Gallery of Unnamed Hands, which is shut for a condition survey and would be shut anyway. He cannot sign for anything. He cannot take custody of a thing out of a house. He cannot stand in a room in which a decision is being made, because there is no room in this city in which a decision is being made that he is entitled to be in, and the one building in which decisions were made about him for six weeks has a minute in a plastic sleeve in his coat that bars him from the door.

**The first thing he did in Volume 01 was to find out what a thing wanted. The first thing he has done in Volume 02 is to find out that he cannot be in a room, and the second thing he is going to do is to stand in a corridor and be told what a woman of fifty-four is saying on the other side of a door, and the third thing he is going to do is to ask somebody a question, which he has not done eleven times.**

---

**What Hattie Bramm said in that room is on the page because four people wrote it down afterwards, independently, and none of them wrote down the same thing, which is how he knows it was not a speech.**

Field Officer Marsh came at half past ten and sat down at the table with a folder and did not open it, and she is a woman who works in a hired room above a shop in Ferry Bank and who has been the only person in this business who said a thing out loud in advance so that nobody else could be ambushed by it.

Vera Pilling came at about ten past ten with her niece and sat by the radiator and did not speak until she was asked a direct question, and she is eighty-one and she had been in a bus on a Thursday in November with a request in writing that she wanted on the record, and she got it in a recording in a public room on the twenty-seventh, and she has not stopped saying so.

And about eleven other people from Low Ward, of whom four had a debt with a landlord and the rest had come because it is ten past ten on a Friday and there is a kettle.

And the sentence. This is it, and Hattie Bramm gave it four times that morning to four different people and it came out slightly different each time, and the version below is the one she gave to the woman from the conciliation service, because the conciliation service is the only office in this city that has ever put a form in front of her and she wanted it minuted.

"Right. Two things and then we'll do the queue.

"**One: nobody is doing this to anybody.** I have stood in this room nine years and I have never once seen anybody come and take anybody off anything. There's no man. There's no company. There's no letter with a name on it saying your address has changed. What there is, is a form somewhere that stops needing a name, and everything that reads it has carried on being right. And I want that said in a room with a form in it, because the second it's in a room with a form in it it's a thing that happened, and I know what happens to things that have happened, because that's what happened to me in 1974 and I have got the certificate and I have not got the wage book.

"**Two, and this is the one I have been trying to say since the Sunday, and it took Vera Pilling to say it to me in a kitchen.** The post is the register. That's what it is. That's what it's for. Everybody thinks the register is the council and the doctor's and the school's and it is all three of those and all three of them are fed off the same thing, and the same thing is a bag on a man's shoulder every morning, and I have watched that bag go up and down that street for thirty-one years and I have never once thought about it, and neither has anybody, and I have been the gate at a mill and I know exactly what a gate is for, and a gate is for a man to come through it and be counted."

"And nobody's counted them," said Vera Pilling from the radiator.

"No," said Hattie Bramm. "That's the finding. Nobody's counted them. They've stopped being countable and nothing fell over, and that's the whole of it, and it will go on until somebody wants them to be counted again for something."

Field Officer Marsh did not open the folder.

"Two questions," she said. "For the record and I am not minuting this, so nothing said in this room is evidence of anything and I want that said out loud at the start."

"Say it."

"What is said in this room is said in a room. It is not a claim, it is not a statement, it is not a record, and it goes nowhere, and if it is repeated it is repeated as a thing somebody said in a library." She put her hands flat on the folder. "And I have come here on a Friday for two years and I have never once been able to do anything for anybody in it, and I have not pretended otherwise, and about four people a year ask me what I actually do and I tell them that."

"Which is what?"

"I write down what other people have already done," said Field Officer Marsh, "with the name of the person who did it on it. That is the whole of my trade. There is no version of me in this room today."

---

**Doreen Sarn was asked.**

It was asked properly, which is to say it was asked by a woman with a form in it, in a room, at about eleven, in front of two witnesses, and Doreen Sarn was not there and had said she would not be, and was asked anyway, because asking is what a room does.

The refusal came back at about four minutes past eleven in the mouth of a woman of about seventy who had come down from Ferry Bank on a bus to deliver it, and it was four words.

"She says no."

"Will she say why."

"She says she'll write it and she won't send it," said the woman. "And she says to tell you it's the sixth thing she's refused and none of the five before it moved either, so you'll not waste a seventh on her."

That is in the room and nobody argued with it, and the woman from Ferry Bank went back on the bus, and the refusal is the sixth and none of the five before it has moved, and the reason is on the page because she gave it in a doorway in October and it has not changed:

**She will not be a record again, in a room, with a form in it.** She was seventeen in 1974 and she was in a room with a form in it and she said a thing, and the thing was true, and the thing is now a series name in a museum, and she has been a piece of paper for fifty years and has refused five documents since the twenty-ninth of October and would like, in her own words, to be a person who was in a room.

---

**The hundred and forty was in a drawer and was not produced, and the reason was given out loud, twice, at about ten to twelve, and Elias Venn was in the corridor and heard it through the door because the door was open four inches for the kettle.**

"Ask me again in March," said Hattie Bramm.

"It's not a request, it's a list of a hundred and forty people."

"It's a piece of paper in a drawer in a front room in Low Ward with a hundred and forty names on it that I have been keeping since about 2015, and I have got it because I could put a street on about a hundred and twenty of them, and there is not one form in this city that a person can be put on that is safer in my drawer than it is in a file, and the reason is that a list of people is not evidence until it is in a file, and the second it is in a file it can be used." She filled the kettle. "That's why I said it in a gallery in November and got it into a file on purpose with a name of the woman who said it against it, and it took me nine years to work out how to do that and I did it in a room of eighty people because it had to be in a room, and I have not been wrong about it yet."

"And the three typed names."

"Those have gone to Marsh's file, not this room's, and I know that's the wrong way round by every rule there is and I don't care, because if I've got three names in a Museum file with no source on them then in five years there's a document in a museum with three names on it and no source, and the second that happens it gets used. I have been the woman who knows a name in a room for nine years and I know exactly what it costs the room. It costs me most."

"And the ones you can't get."

Hattie Bramm did not answer that for about nine seconds, and she had the kettle in her hand and did not put it down, and Elias Venn stood in a corridor and listened to a woman not answer a question for nine seconds, and it is the only nine seconds of Volume 02 that he has counted.

"You've had that sentence off Ivo Pell," she said, through the door.

"I've had it off a man of twenty-five in a gallery and I gave it away and I've never asked you for it."

"You never asked me for it in eleven weeks and you knocked on a door in my street on Sunday and went past my house, and I watched you go past it, and I said nothing, and I'm saying nothing now either except that the sentence is yours and it came off a boy of twenty-five and it was in a gallery on the fifteenth of November and it has been in a gallery ever since and it is on nobody's door." She put the kettle down. "There is no list of the ones who never had a letter. There never will be. You cannot take a list of a thing nobody kept. And the day somebody comes to me with a form that says *list everybody who has not received post in the last twelve months* I will tear it up, because the only way to fill it in is to have kept it, and nobody kept it, and I did not keep it, and Vera didn't, and the postman didn't, and **it is not a gap in a record, it is the shape of the thing, and the shape of the thing is the only evidence anybody will ever have.**"

---

He asked her one question at twenty past twelve in the corridor, and he waited for the answer, and it took about four seconds, and it is the only question he has asked her in twelve weeks.

"Are you on it?"

"What."

"On the sheet." He got the receipt out of his coat and did not unfold it, because there was no need to and because he has rules. "The post sheet. The round. The one with a name field on it. **Is your name still in the field at number fourteen.**"

Hattie Bramm leaned against the wall of the corridor with her arms folded and looked at him for slightly longer than the question deserved.

"Aye," she said. "It's got my name on it and it's had since about 1974 and I have never once wanted to read it."

"Why not."

"Because I'm the one who writes them." She said it as though he had asked her why she did not want a telephone number. "I've got a hundred and forty in a drawer and a Friday list in pencil and three typed names in a woman's file in Ferry Bank, and I have never in my life wanted to be on a list I can't get off. And you've come into my library on a Friday morning to ask me whether I'm on a postal sheet, and you are the only person in eleven weeks who has asked me one question, and it is about a field."

"Sorry."

"Don't be. It's a good question." She pushed off the wall. "It's a better question than the one you came in with, and I could tell what you came in with, because it's the same face as the sixth of November and it wants a room."

"I wanted—"

"I know what you wanted," said Hattie Bramm. "You wanted to be in here. You've wanted to be in a room since the sixth of October and you've been in about nine of them and you were in all nine because somebody else was in the room first, and there is not one of us in there today who would have let you in, and not one of us is wrong, and **if you had come in here without a face on you I would have given you a chair and put you on the list, and the list is a piece of A4 and a pencil and it goes in a drawer, and I would have done it, and it would have been the best Thursday of my life.**"

---

He was out of the building at ten past one. He stood in the car park behind the library for about four minutes, because he had nowhere to be, and then he wrote the whole of it out longhand at a table in a rented room, which is what he does, and which takes about fifty minutes and which he has done about nine hundred times in six weeks and which nobody has ever asked him for.

> **Fri 18 Dec, 14:10. The queue. Back room, Sillick Lane library, ten to half past one. About twenty people, nine of them off that row on foot.**
> **I was in the corridor for fifty-one minutes. Hattie B. would not have me in and gave a reason that is a rule and the rule is that a room with a form in it makes you a record, and I am not a record, I am a man with a face, and she was right.**
> **Three things I did not have before this morning and cannot unlearn.**
> **1. The post is the register. Not the council's roll, not the doctor's list, not the school's. All three are fed off it. A gate is for a man to come through it and be counted and the gate on that row is a bag on a man's shoulder and nobody has thought about it in thirty-one years, and Hattie B. has, and she has never been asked, and I have not asked her anything for twelve weeks.**
> **2. Nobody is doing this to anybody. There is no man and no company and no letter saying your address has changed. A field stops being needed and everything that reads it carries on being right. That is the worst version and it is the true one and it is why I cannot be angry at anybody and why that is worse than being angry.**
> **3. Doreen Sarn said no for the sixth time on the fourth of December, in a room, through a woman on a bus, and she has been a piece of paper for fifty years, and she has not been wrong once in six refusals, and the sixth refusal cost a bus fare.**
> **And the hundred and forty is in a drawer and there is no list of the ones who never had a letter and there never will be, and the sentence is mine, off a man of twenty-five, and I have now heard it at the back of a door from the woman it came from, and it is better in her mouth than it has ever been in mine, and she made me say where I got it from, and I did.**
> **And I asked her one question. Twelve weeks, ten, and the tenth was a knock on a door in her street and I got nothing, and the eleventh was hers and she answered it in four seconds.**
> **Is your name in the field. Aye. It has been since 1974 and she has never once wanted to read it.**
> **That is the only answer anybody has given me in four days and it is about a name field and it came from the one person in this business who has standing over a record and has refused five things and would not give me the number.**

And then, at about ten to five, the second half of the day, which he wrote at about eleven at night, and which is the part that matters:

> **The only list of that row is a Friday afternoon in a back room with a kettle in it, and the list is a piece of A4 and a pencil, and it is twenty names, and it is not kept, and it is the people who turn up.**
> **On the sixth of December the field at 88 went empty and Frank Dunning did not find out until a bank statement came back with a dash in a box on the eighth, and a bank statement is not the post.**
> **I have spent six weeks learning that the only instrument that has ever caught one in this business is a record with a name against it, and I have got a receipt with a woman's handwriting on the back of it, and a notebook, and a pound, and a cardboard folder with a company's letter in it that I will not put in a Museum file because of clause six.**
> **And on the sixteenth of December a woman on a counter at Nether Kiln Lane wrote out four lines of a change log in her own hand and said out loud in a room with a printer in it where it came from, because a man in a coat asked her for it, and that is the only instrument anybody in three weeks has handed to anybody, and she did it because nobody asked her to and because I asked, and that is the whole of the four inches.**
