# Chapter 0054 — A Door Is Not an Object

The Museum of Quiet Saints is a public museum and it is open from ten until five on a Tuesday in the week before Christmas, and about a hundred and fifty people come through the front door of it on an ordinary day in that week, and there is no charge for any of them except a pound a head for the galleries, which they take or do not take.

Elias Venn was inside the building at twenty past ten on the morning of Tuesday the twenty-second of December 2024, and he was there lawfully, and being there lawfully cost him a printed card with a date and a time and a room on it and the word **WITNESS** in a box, and it had come through the post on Friday inside a Board envelope, and the envelope had a sub-group reference on it, and the sub-group was sitting at eleven on item three and item four.

Line three of a four-line minute permits attendance at any hearing or inquiry at which he is a party or a witness, on notice, in company, not before eight in the morning. He had read it about nine hundred times. He was in company with the Registrar. He had not been inside the building since the eighth of December and he had not been inside a room on the first floor since the second of December and he was not going to be, because his notice said fourth floor, Committee Room, 11:00, and a man does not go where a card does not send him.

He was in the Hall of Former Faiths because the Hall of Former Faiths is on the way, it is a public room, and it is open to anybody for a pound, and nobody in this building has ever asked him to explain why he was standing in a gallery.

---

**Frank Dunning came in at ten past ten and paid a pound and had a mat under his arm.**

He had it rolled. It was rolled the way a thing gets rolled when it has been taken up off a floor and put down again, badly, with the two ends sticking out about four inches each, and there was a rubber backing on it that had come away in one piece and was stuck to the outside of the roll like a hat brim.

The woman at the front desk was Margaret Priest and she has taken messages at a counter for nineteen years and she read the situation in about two seconds and did the thing that a person with nineteen years does, which was to look at the mat and then look at the man and not look at the mat again.

"Frank Dunning," he said. "Eighty-eight Sillick Lane. Hattie Bramm sent me."

"You've come on the bus."

"I've come on the bus." He put the mat down on the floor of the lobby, which is a thing people do and which Margaret Priest noticed and did not comment on. "She said there's a woman at this desk who's been here nineteen years and that you'd see me."

"I've been here nineteen years," said Margaret Priest, "and nobody's sent anybody to me before, and that's a nice thing to be sent for." She opened a drawer. "Now. What is it you've come about."

"The card," said Frank Dunning.

---

**The card was in a box on the shelf behind her, in a bundle of about four hundred, in brown paper bands, in a hand that is not hers, cut in October and printed in October and banded in October, and it took her about ninety seconds to find the right one because they are not in street order, they are in the order of a print run.**

> **MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS — HALL OF FORMER FAITHS — THRESHOLD GROUP.**
> *Our records show this address as part of the Hall of Former Faiths threshold group. If that is wrong, tell us. If you would like to be taken off it, tell us. If you would like a copy of anything we hold about the object at plinth 34, tell us and we will send it.*
> **Annual acknowledgement. Issued 12 December 2024.**
> **DUNNING, F. — 88 SILLICK LANE, LOW WARD.**

She handed it across the counter and he did not take it, and she said, "It's yours, love."

"I know it's mine. That's the bit." He looked at it without touching it. "That came back."

"It came back," said Margaret Priest, and then stopped, because she had said more than she meant to, and because a woman who has taken messages at a counter for nineteen years knows exactly what she has just done, and she did not take it back, which is the correct professional thing and also the humane one. "Yes. It came back in the second week of December, marked undeliverable, all nine of that run together, and they are in the dead letters, and there is another lot in the box behind the boiler on Friday and nobody has opened the box on Friday."

"Nine."

"Nine off that run, and we did not print them, and that is the sentence I have not said to anybody in nineteen years." She closed the drawer, which took a second because the box was heavy. "And I'll tell you what I tell anybody who asks, and then I'll say the other thing, because there are two things and I am not going to give you the comfortable one on its own. The comfortable one is: it's a postal thing, it's got nothing to do with us, there's a form at the post office and it'll come back. That's true and it's the whole of it and it is also not why I'm standing here."

"And the other thing."

"The other thing," said Margaret Priest, "is that we don't make that list. **We print off a file that comes down from the district on the first of the month and we do not make it and we have never made it and we could not make it if we tried, and I have been on this counter for nineteen years and I can tell you that the names on that card came into this building off a computer somewhere and I have never seen the computer and I have never asked, because for nineteen years the only thing anybody has ever wanted from me is the right piece of paper and I have been extremely good at the right piece of paper.**"

---

**The mat went into the Hall of Former Faiths at about twenty to eleven, and it was not accessioned, and it was not put on a plinth, and it was not put behind glass, and it did not go under the rope, and no card was made for it, and nobody read anything.**

The reason it went into a public room at all is that a man of about fifty-three signed a piece of paper on the twenty-fourth of November that says he cannot take custody of a thing he has no title to, cannot indemnify it, and cannot undertake anything about it, and the registrar working under him had worked out, in about four minutes, on the twenty-second, in a conservation office, with a steel rule in her hand, that the only thing this building can do with an object it is not allowed to hold is **write down what it is**, in a room, where a person can see it happen.

"Four minutes," said Mara Iles. "Then it goes back on the floor of a corridor and nobody signs for it, because there is nothing to sign."

"There's no form."

"There is no form," said Mara Iles. "And I have looked, and I want it on the page that I looked, because in about four years somebody is going to say the registrar did not look. There is no form in this building for a thing that is given to us by a member of the public in a lobby. There is a form for a gift, and a form for a bequest, and a form for a loan, and a form for a transfer, and a form for a disclosure, and a disclosure form is about a **document** going into a Museum file, and this is not a document, it is a mat, and a mat is not a document, and if I put it in the disclosure file then a mat is a document and in five years somebody will have built a category on the strength of my Tuesday morning."

Frank Dunning stood on the public side of nine feet of floor and watched.

"Right," he said. "Can somebody write down what it is, then."

"Yes," said Mara Iles. "That is the one thing I am allowed to do and it is the only thing I am allowed to do and I am going to do it now, in front of you, and I am going to do it in about four minutes, and I am going to read it out when I have done it, and you are going to be able to take a copy of it home, and **it will be the only piece of paper in this city that says what that mat is, and it will not say your name, and you are going to have to decide whether that is good or the worst thing you have ever been handed.**"

She put it on the floor by the second plinth and knelt down and had a steel rule out of her coat in about four seconds, in a way that told Elias Venn she had thought about this on the Sunday and had had it in her coat since Monday.

And Elias Venn stood six feet away on the terrazzo with his hands behind his back and did not come closer and did not look away, and this is the whole of what he did in the four minutes and it is the hardest thing he has done since the twenty-seventh of November and there is nobody in this building who will ever know it was hard.

> **DESCRIPTION. No accession number assigned and none proposed. Object not accessioned, not displayed, not labelled, and not to be catalogued.**
> **Item as presented: one door mat. Coir face on a rubber backing. Measured 700 x 400 mm to the nearest 10 mm.**
> **Fibre wear: a band approx. 380 mm wide running the full depth of the mat, its centreline 120 mm from the edge nearest the door. The opposite edge is sound. The wear is therefore on the side of the mat that faces into the dwelling.**
> **Backing: perished at two corners, one corner re-laid. One edge repaired, fibre of a different colour and a different lay, held with two staples.**
> **No inscription, no name, no number, no maker's mark, no trade mark, no date. No evidence of any use other than standing on.**
> **Function: a fixed-size surface of the dimensions required for a person to stand on while a person stands on it, at a doorway, while a person decides whether to open a door. The wear records the position of the standing.**
> **Condition: sound, dirty, and with a documented history of removal and re-laying. Not deteriorating. Not rare. No devotional content observed. No maker identified and no maker determinable.**
> **M. ILES, Senior Conservator and Accession Registrar, 22 Dec 2024, in the Hall of Former Faiths, in the presence of the presenter.**

That is the whole of it and it took four minutes and eleven seconds and it is the only description of any object in the whole of this case, in any volume, that is about a thing's job.

And when she had read it out, she said the thing the whole afternoon is for, and she said it to Frank Dunning and not to Elias Venn, and Elias Venn heard all of it.

"**It is seven hundred by four hundred. That is the size of a doorway with a person standing in it. Nobody chose that size. It is a size because it has to be one. And the wear is where they wait, and the wear is on the side that faces into the house, and that means the person doing the standing is the person being let in — and that is not a mystery and it is not a god and it is the oldest piece of engineering in this building and there is not one word of it on a plinth in this room.**"

She put the steel rule back in her coat.

"And I will not make a card for it. You should know that before I say anything else. **A card is a name. I am not going to put a name on a mat in a public room, and I am not going to put a blank on it either, and both of those are decisions and neither of them is a rule and I am making both of them and I would like them in the minute if anybody is writing one.**"

"Why not a blank?"

"Because a blank on a plinth card in this room is a thing I have seen before," said Mara Iles, "and it is on a card about eleven feet from where we are standing, and I have known about it since the sixth of October, and the reason I am not going to put a blank on a card about a mat in a public room is that I do not yet know what this building does with a card when it cannot be bothered to fill it in, and I have watched this building for five years and the answer is that it fills it in later, and I am not doing that to a door mat while a man is standing here watching."

She picked the mat up off the floor and stood holding it, and did not seem to want to hand it back, and then did.

"Take it home," she said. "No. Wait."

---

The Hall of Former Faiths has thirty-one plinths down the middle of it and a rope line, and plinth eleven is about four feet tall, and on plinth eleven there is a stone.

It is about eleven inches long and eight inches wide and four inches deep and it is a piece of a mill threshold, and somebody in about 1860 took a piece of a door sill out of a working building and cut it to a size that would sit in a case, and in the middle of it there is a hollow.

Not two hollows. **One.** About nine inches long and four wide and a couple of inches deep at the deepest, and it is worn smooth, and the wear is in the middle of a stone, in the position of a foot, and not at an edge and not in a corner.

> **1961/LW/19 — THRESHOLD STONE, sandstone, one sill fragment.**
> *One hollow, worn, foot-shaped, right of centre. Recovered on the demolition of a mill building, Low Ward.*
> *No maker identified.*

> *(Card bears no accession number in the body of the text and no name in the maker's line. The field is empty and has been empty since the card was cut.)*

"It is the same size as a person standing still," said Mara Iles, without being asked, because she had been waiting since Monday for somebody to say it. "It is nine inches by four and it is the size of a foot, and the mat outside is seven hundred by four hundred and it is the size of a person standing still at a door, and **it is a threshold object. It is the oldest thing in this room. It has been in this building since before the collection was catalogued and it has been on an open plinth with a rope in front of it for about a hundred and twenty years, and it has never once been described to anybody in a public room, and there is no maker's name on its card because the label room will not put a name on a card it cannot support.**"

"I have never seen that."

"You have never been on this side of the row at eleven o'clock on a Tuesday, and neither has anybody in this building, and that is not a criticism of anybody in this building, that is a hundred and forty years of a rope line and about four people a year who come to a public gallery and look at the plinths from the middle of the room."

Then she said the sentence that is the last line of that morning and it is the sentence the next eight days are built on, and she said it to a man of about sixty-five holding a rolled mat, and Elias Venn heard it from six feet away and wrote it out in full at a table that evening and did not show it to anybody.

"**Mr Dunning, you have asked me three times in eleven minutes to write down a thing about you. I have written down a thing about a mat. There is no form in this country that will put those two sentences in the same file, and the reason there is not, is that the sentence about you is about a person and the sentence about the mat is about an object, and this building has been very good for a hundred and forty years at the second of those and has never once been asked for the first.**"

---

**It went back to the floor of a service corridor at about half past eleven, and it stayed there.**

Not in a case. Not in a store. Not on the loading-bay desk where a cage stood in October. **On the floor**, on a rubber-backed mat, on a concrete floor, in a corridor that is on the way from the loading bay to the offices, with a folding chair four feet away and a radiator and a mop.

And that is where the rest of the day went, and the rest of the day is the chapter's last movement and it is administrative and it is four sentences long.

Nobody could remove it. Not because they would not. Because the instruments do not reach it.

**Condition one** of the Director's standing conditions of the fifteenth of October says that no Schedule 12 carried object is to be moved, caged or removed from the building except under a written docket, countersigned by the Director, stating the destination and the date, and to remain open on the desk of the post holding the docket until the object is on its pallet and the pallet is sealed. **Condition two** says that no Schedule 12 carried object is to be removed from the building except on a docket countersigned by a person who is not an officer or employee of this Museum, and adds, in the Director's own hand on a card in a corridor in October: *My signature is not sufficient.*

A door mat from a flat in Low Ward is not a Schedule 12 carried object. It has no classification, no number, no location in a systems index, and no owner of record. Both conditions therefore do not apply to it, which means that there is no docket to be raised, because a docket is a document about a Museum object, and there is no document here, and a mat in a corridor is not a Museum object and cannot be made into one by anybody in the building without a form that does not exist.

**And the next day, at about twenty past two in the afternoon, a registrar drafted one anyway, because that is what she is, and it failed, and the way it failed is on the page because the next chapter is built out of it and because it is the first time in this case that the best control in this building stopped a good person doing the right thing.**

She drafted a movement docket for a door mat on the Wednesday morning. She drafted it properly, with an item, a quantity, a method of carriage, a destination, a date and a time out, and a carrier, and a countersignature in the space for **a person who is not an officer or employee of this Museum.**

And then she read condition two again, and the sentence in it, which is in a Director's own hand on a card in a corridor in October: *My signature is not sufficient.*

"**The countersignature has to be somebody who is not an officer or employee. The only person who can sign for a mat is the man it belongs to, and he is a member of the public, and he would sign it this afternoon. And the moment the docket exists, condition two is engaged, and condition two says a Schedule 12 object is not to be removed except on a docket so countersigned — and it is not a Schedule 12 object, and the moment we make it one, in order to get it out of the building, we have built ourselves a rule that says he cannot have it back.**"

Which meant it could not be docketed **out** either. It could not be docketed in. It could not be docketed anywhere. It was in a corridor of a public museum on Civic Hill, in a week when about a hundred and fifty people a day came through the front door of the building, and it was going to be there for as long as nobody could write a piece of paper about it, and nobody in that building was going to be able to write a piece of paper about it for at least three weeks, because the Registry's rota is a paper on a wall in Northglass and the next slot on it was the sixth of February.

Mara Iles told him that at about ten past twelve, on the stairs, in a corridor, on the way to a committee room on the fourth floor where he was due at one o'clock and where he was going to sit as a witness and say what he had seen on a street corner and in a lobby, and never once be asked what he could do.

"There is no way to get it out of here that is not a lie on a form," she said. "And I have spent six weeks telling everybody that the forms are the only thing that holds anything, and I have never once said out loud that a form can hold a thing shut."

"You told me that on the second of December in a corridor."

"I did," said Mara Iles. "And I meant it. And I have not unsaid it and I am not going to. But I have a mat in a corridor and about a hundred and fifty people a day coming through that lobby and I am going to go and sit in front of a Board sub-group this afternoon and be asked about a schedule of collections, and **I want it noted somewhere that the reason a door mat cannot leave a public museum in this city is that there is no form for a person to write a person's name on when they are giving you a thing they own.**"

She went up. He was not going up.

He stood on the fourth-floor landing at ten past one with a card in his hand that said WITNESS and a room number on it, and he was in company with the Registrar, and it was after eight, and it was the only lawful door he had in this city, and in about nine months it would be a room in Northglass with a date on it and an assessor in it and a question he had put there, and Mara Iles had told him in ninety-one seconds on a pavement in Lantern Row on the twelfth of December exactly what that letter would sound like and how long it would take.

And at about four o'clock he wrote the whole of it out, and the last line of the notebook entry for the twenty-second of December 2024 is the one that Volume 02 is built on and it is four words long and he was embarrassed by them and he wrote them anyway.

> **Tue 22 Dec, 18:40. A description is the only thing left.**
