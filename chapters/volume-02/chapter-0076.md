# Chapter 0076 — The Door That Is Not On A List

The pavement in Broad Street is about eleven feet wide and there is a bus stop at the top of it and a chemist's shutter down and a wall with nothing on it, and at ten to ten on the morning of Thursday the eighteenth of February 2025 there were two people standing on it, and one of them had a satchel with eleven forms in it and the other one had nothing in his hands at all, which was the second time in ten weeks that he had stood on a pavement in Low Ward with nothing in his hands and the first time had been on a road in Sillick Lane with a man of about fifty who worked Thursdays.

He had not told anybody he was going. He had told nobody where he was going. He had nine pages in a notebook that cost thirty-five pence and a plastic wallet in his coat with four things in it and he had not taken the notebook out, and there is a rule at the top of the first page in his own hand in three lines, and the second of the three lines is the reason he was stood on that pavement with his hands in his coat at ten to ten in the morning on a Thursday in February.

Mairin Devlin came round the corner at ten past ten and recognised him in about a second and a half, which is what a doorstep person does for a living.

"You're the coat from the last time."

"Yes."

"You're not on my round."

"**No.**"

"Right." She did not ask him in and she did not tell him to go away, and she said the thing that has been sitting in the middle of this case since the twenty-eighth of January before she said anything else at all, and she said it in about six words.

"**I said that thing in January and I'm not going to say it again, and I'm not going to have it said at me, and if you've come to hear it you can turn round.**"

"I've come because there are nine houses on your pad and I've been told about five of them."

"**You've been told about five of them by somebody who has not been round this street since 1994.**" She shifted the satchel. "I've got nine. I have always had nine. The pad is not the list and the list was never the pad. And there is one on that pad I want to talk to you about, and it is not the last one we did. It's number four, and it is the one I have never stood in front of."

---

**They did five of them over about an hour and ten minutes. The first three were nothing. The fourth was something. The fifth was the thing.**

**The first house got no answer, and she wrote it on the back of her own form and put the date on it in ink and put the form back in the satchel, and said the sentence about it that she has said before and that Elias Venn has written down twice and did not write down on the day.**

"That's it. That's the instrument. A bit of paper with a date and a house on it in a bag a person carries. And in about four years that bag's a skip and the bit of paper's a bit of paper, and there is no code in this county that says *I did not get an answer on the eighteenth of February at Broad Street*, because there is a code for it on my form, and I read it about nine times a day, and nobody has ever asked me what it means."

**The second house got no answer, the same, and she said nothing about it, and that was correct.**

**The third house got an answer from a man of about seventy who has lived there for thirty-one years and who has never been on a roll and never wanted to be, and who came to the door in a dressing gown at eleven o'clock on a Thursday morning, and who told them both to go away in a few seconds, and who was on nothing at all, and who did not know a soul in the case had ever said his name.**

**The house before number four got an answer from a woman of about sixty who has been in it since November and who has two jobs and no car and who wants to know, once, and only once, whether being on the roll costs anything.**

"Nothing," said Mairin Devlin. "**Nothing at all. No penalty, no fee, no letter about your bins. It's a list of who is allowed to vote at that address and you can vote without being on it if you turn up with a photograph and a bill, and being on it is the shorter door into the same room. The only people it costs anything to are people who have moved and don't tell us, and they get a letter, and that is the whole of the letter.**"

"I thought it was a list of people who were problems."

"**It is a list of people who have told us where they live,**" she said. "**And I know what you have heard and I know where you have heard it and I have said the sentence I said in January to about four people since and I am not saying it again, and you are not obliged to be on anything.**"

---

Number four was the whole of the day, and the going was the whole of it.

"**Number four is the one I've never stood in front of,**" said Mairin Devlin. "It's got a number on my pad because it's got a number on the street and I write the number down when I go past, and I have been on this street about six times since November and I have not been to that door once, and I have been not going to it for a reason and the reason is that I have got a justification box on a special-visit form and I have never in nine years been able to write a true sentence in it about any of these nine."

"What's the box."

"**It says *reason for special visit*.** And it wants about two lines. And for eight of my nine for nine years I have written *concern for vulnerable resident* and it is a lie, and it is a lie that everybody in that office knows is a lie, and it is the phrase we use, and I have used it about a hundred times and I have never once been able to put it in front of a person and say *this is what we think of you*."

"And number four."

"**Number four is a household where nobody is vulnerable.**" She said it flatly. "**They are about thirty-four. They work. They pay. They moved in at the end of November and they have not been on a roll since, and there is no form in this country for them, and the reason there is no form for them is on a piece of paper in a building four miles from here and it takes four minutes to read and it is called a change of occupancy.**"

"How does a move-in get missed."

"**It doesn't get missed. It gets declined.** That's what nobody has understood about me for nine years. I am not failing to find them. **I find every one of them. I knock at every one of them, and about a third of them open, and I take a name and a date of birth and a qualifying date in their own hand, and on the form I do not have a single box that asks me whether a household exists.** The register is made of people and the people come to me and the address is the thing I write down while they tell me their name. And when there's nobody at number four, then the line goes off the roll, and then the line is gone, and then somebody moves in, and then I have to go and find them again, and to go and find them again I need a special visit, and to get a special visit I have to write two lines in a box."

"And the two lines."

"**And the two lines are the whole of the case and they are about half an inch of ink,**" said Mairin Devlin, "**and I have been putting them off since the fourteenth of February.**"

---

She said the sentence then, and it took her about four seconds, and she did not say it kindly and she did not say it to a room.

"I'm going to write *address not in roll following recorded change of occupancy, possible omission, date of change not in local copy.*"

"That's a form of words."

"That is the truth, that is the whole of the truth, and there is not one word of it about a person. **The address is not in the roll. The change of occupancy is recorded — recorded by somebody else, four miles away, on a Sunday, applied without anybody knocking. I have not got the date of it in my copy. There may be an omission. All three of those are things about documents.** And it will go up and it will come back approved, because it is not a lie and it is not a hardship application and it does not require anybody to be vulnerable, and my supervisor will not read it twice, and in nine years nobody will know why this ward has a better roll than the ward next door."

Elias Venn said: "I would like to give you something back and I have got nothing, and I am going to do it anyway, and it is going to sound like nothing."

"Go on."

"You asked me in a corridor in January how a house gets into a system and I could not answer you. I have not got a form and I have not got a satchel and I have not got a form for you, and I am not going to tell you there is one, because there is not, and I have been told there is not by two people in the last four weeks and I have believed both of them."

"Then what are you doing on this pavement."

"**Knocking,**" he said.

---

They walked about ninety yards and he knocked on a door in Broad Street at about eleven twenty on the morning of Thursday the eighteenth of February 2025 and it was opened by a woman of about thirty-four who had a coat on over her arm and who was about to go to work, and who had been in the house since the twenty-ninth of November 2024 and who had never had a letter that was not returned.

"**I am not from the council,**" he said, before she said anything, because he had thought about the order of the sentences for about a mile.

"Alright."

"**I am not from anywhere. I have got no form, I have got no badge, I have got nothing at all, and I am going to tell you three things and then I am going to go, and you can shut the door at any point and it will be correct to do so.**"

She did not shut the door.

"**One. There is no form in this country for a person at a house that no system has got a name for.**" He said it exactly as she had said it in a hall in Sillick Lane on the twenty-eighth of January, in the same twenty-one words, because it was not his sentence and he was not going to improve it. "**I am not saying it to you as a fact about you. I am saying it because you have been here since the twenty-ninth of November and I have got a reason to think it applies to you and I have been told by the woman on this pavement that she cannot put it in a box.**"

"What is the second thing."

"**There is a woman here with a satchel and she is the only person in this county whose job it is to go and stand in front of a house and write down your name in your own hand. She is going to have to ask her office for permission to come back to this door, and she is going to have to write two lines in a box to get it, and she has just told me what she is going to write and it is not about you. Her name is Mairin Devlin and she works out of an office in the district and she will be able to tell you the address of it and I am not going to write it on anything.**"

"And the third thing."

And this is the one he came for, and it is the whole of what ten weeks has been for, and it took him a few seconds and he did not have it written down anywhere and he has still not written it down.

"**I am not going to write your name down.**"

She looked at him.

"**Not today, not in a book, not on a form, not in a message, not anywhere. I have got nine pages in a pocket notebook and there is a rule at the top of the first page in my own handwriting that says a fact a person gives me in a street is theirs until it is in a file, and I wrote that on the twenty-fifth of January after a postman on a pavement told me the truth about something, and I have not broken it once since and I am not going to start on your doorstep.** And I want you to know that it costs me something, because it means that in about a month, when somebody asks me what I did in February, I will not be able to say that I came here. **I will be able to say that there is a street called Broad Street. I will not be able to say there is a person in it. And that is the correct arrangement and it is the correct arrangement because of you and not because of me.**"

"You don't even know my name."

"I know it. You are going to tell her in about a minute and I am going to stand here and hear it and I am not going to hold it." He put his hands behind his back, which is what a person does at a door. "**And I have never said that out loud to a stranger before and I have wanted to for about ten weeks.**"

---

The woman told Mairin Devlin her name, and Mairin Devlin wrote it down in the woman's own hand on a form, on a doorstep, in about a quarter of an hour, because a form wants a name and a name is what a person gives a stranger at a door, and that is the entire mechanism and it has not changed in four hundred years.

The qualifying date was the twenty-ninth of November 2024 and Mairin Devlin had to ask for the month and then she had to ask for the year and the woman said twenty twenty-four and Mairin Devlin wrote it and did not comment on it and the comment was in a file four miles away in a binder on the twentieth of January and could not be got.

"Right," she said, when the form was done. "**That's it in. It goes in on Monday and it comes down in about nine days, and that is the third of March, and after that anybody can look you up, including you, including me, and you are on a list, and you did not ask to be on a list and I did not tell you it was free of consequences, because it is not.**"

"What are the consequences."

"**Somebody will know.** That's it. That's the whole of the consequences of every form I have ever filled in and it is why I have been not going to that door for nine years. In about nine years somebody will write to you about a bin or a precept or a boundary and they will use a name that is correct, and that is what a list is for."

"Fine."

"**And I am going to be honest with you about what I cannot do,**" said Mairin Devlin, "**because you have had two men in a fortnight and one of them was decent about it. I can get you on the roll. I cannot get a name into any other system in this city and I have no idea which ones have lost you and neither does anybody else and I asked, in January, in writing, and the answer I got was four pages and the last sentence of it was that the file is not a register of persons.** And that is the last time anybody in this city is going to say a true thing to you for about a month, so I am saying it now on a doorstep."

"And you will come back."

"**I will come back and I am going to say the date out loud so that you can hold me to it and not me to it. Wednesday the twenty-fourth of March, in the afternoon, and if I am not here by four o'clock I have been put on a round and I will be at the office on the number on the card and you can ring and ask for me by name, which is the only thing anybody in this building can actually do.**"

---

The last thing that happened on that pavement was at about ten to twelve on the eighteenth of February 2025. The woman asked the man in the coat whether there was anything else, and he said no, and then said one more thing, and the thing he said was about a mat.

"They've got a mat down by the wall at about number forty in Sillick Lane. Two pounds, off a shelf in a shop on Fisher Lane. **It's not a church and it isn't anything and there are no rules you have to keep and nobody's job is to know. You stand on it and you knock and you say a name. That's the whole of it and it started in January and it's about nine people and it's on a sheet of A4 in a plastic wallet on a table in a hall and it dies with the man who keeps it.**"

"And what does it do?"

"**Nothing,**" said Elias Venn. "**It does absolutely nothing, and I have been in this case for ten weeks and I have been the only person in every room where anything has happened and I am telling you on a doorstep that it does nothing. It is a man of about sixty-five putting his name out loud at a door where somebody lives. That's all. If it does nothing then it is the only instrument in this city that does nothing and that is why it works and that is why nobody has stopped it.**"

She looked at him for a second and a half.

"You're the first person who's said that about anything," she said.

"I am," he said, "**and I have been waiting ten weeks to say it to somebody who is not in a room.**"
