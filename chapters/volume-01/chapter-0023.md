# Chapter 0023 — The List

They were in a room on the first floor of a police station in Low Ward at twenty past two on Friday afternoon, and it was not an interview room, it was a room with four tables in it and a noticeboard and a bicycle helmet on the floor, and there were two men in it who were not police and one of them was there because she had been sent for by the man who had read the caution.

Elias Venn had been in a police station twice. Once at nineteen, for a fight outside a pub on the tram route, which had been his fault and had ended in a caution and a fifty-pound fine and a set of letters to his mother. Once in the last fortnight, for a fall.

The caution was the event. Not the statement, and not the questions, and not the man with the clipboard, and not the fact that he was forty feet from a door he could have walked out of.

The caution was the event because it was the first time in twelve days that a person with a statutory duty had sat down in front of him and said the words *you may be prosecuted*, and had then written it on a form, and had then given him a copy, and the copy was a document and the document was a record and the record had his name on it and it was going to be in a building on the fourth floor of somebody else's establishment for the rest of time.

The previous document with his name on it had said *unable to act within the terms of his restriction*. This one said * cautioned, caution accepted 14:34, interview commenced 14:40*.

"You've been cautioned on this before," said Sergeant Ashby, reading the top of the file. "Twenty eleven."

"Yes."

"Good." He turned the file round so it was facing him and squared the top sheet against the table. "So you know what this is, and I'll not do the speech. Do you want the solicitor's room, or the table?"

"The table," said VPC Ainsley.

She was about forty-five and she had a cardigan on over a blouse and a satchel with a legal pad in it and a telephone of her own on the table, which she had not switched on. She had come from a room downstairs and she had a piece of paper with the custody record on it in front of her, and Elias Venn found that he trusted her within about four seconds, for a reason he could not afterwards have explained and which was that she had asked for the table and not for the room, and had not made a speech.

"Sit where you can see the door," she said. "Do not answer anything you do not understand. If he asks the same thing twice, that is because he is doing it on purpose. If you want me to stop it you say *I want to stop* and we stop and you can go home and telephone somebody. That is the whole of it. I am not going to tell you the rest because I have not read the file and I am not going to read the file, and if I have not read the file then nobody can say I told you what to say."

That was the sentence that made him trust her, and she had said it in a voice of complete indifference.

---

The questions took an hour and ten minutes and they were good questions, and they were not about a bell.

They were about a gate. About a fence with thirty inches of gap in it. About a man of seventy-four who had come through it. About a rope out of a first aid box on a nail. About a coil of blue polypropylene with a splice eye, eighteen metres, and whether he had known it was there before he went in, and the answer was that he had not known it was there before he went in and he had known it was there about ninety seconds after he had been in the water, which is a sentence that took him eleven seconds to say and which he had not planned.

They were about the water depth, and the temperature, and the time of day, and how long the man had been in, and they were about the loch of it, and about the gap in the fence, and about whether he had put one stone back.

Sergeant Ashby wrote it all down. He was not fast and he was not slow and he did not invent anything, and twice he read a passage back and twice he changed a word at Elias Venn's request, and both times he did it without comment and both times the change was correct.

Then he got to the middle of it, and stopped writing, and looked up.

"You say the reason you were on that site is that you knew a seventy-four-year-old man was going to be hurt."

"Yes."

"How did you know that?"

"Because a man of seventy-four came and asked me a question about a date in a document on Wednesday," Elias said, "and I did not go and ask him, and I did not tell anybody he was coming, and I did not pass on what he told me. And on Saturday I went to a site I was not allowed on because I was frightened of what I was going to find out about a gate count."

"That's your answer to why you were there."

"That's my answer to why I was there."

"Fine." Ashby wrote for about ten seconds. "And the reason you were frightened about a gate count — I'm going to ask this the way the Act wants me to and I'm going to tell you why I'm asking it, which is that there's an insurance file with a counter-attack on it and a claim that is not yours, and I need to know whether the thing you are frightened about has a document in it, because if it does then that document is a real fact and not a story, and if it doesn't then you are a man who trespassed and got a bad result and I have to write it up that way."

Elias Venn looked at the table.

"There is a record," he said.

"Whose record?"

"The Museum's. It's a 1974 receipt for eleven items off a mill on the last working day. It's in a drawer in a gallery on Civic Hill and I have a longhand copy of it in a notebook because I am not allowed in the room. It is in the Museum's own file in the Museum's own hand. It is a document and it does not have my name anywhere on it and it does not have a count on it either."

"And the count is the thing you're frightened about."

"The count was in a reading."

Sergeant Ashby's pen stopped.

"Explain that to me."

"No."

That is the whole of what Elias Venn said about a reading, ever, to anybody outside that room, and it is one word long, and it was the first time he had ever heard himself say it out loud to a person with a statutory duty.

Ashby looked at him for about three seconds.

"I'm going to ask you a question," he said, "and I want you to think about the answer before you give it, because I am going to type this up exactly as you say it and you are going to sign the typing. Is *reading* a word you use for looking at a document, or is it a word you use for something else?"

"It's a word I use for something else."

"Right."

"And I'm not going to tell you what."

"Also right." Ashby wrote one line and turned the pad so it faced down, which is a thing a man does when he is being fair, and not one of the two hundred things a man does when he is being clever. "I'll tell you what I am going to write, and you can object to any of it. I am going to write: *the person states that a figure was obtained by a means he calls a reading; he has declined to explain the term and the writer makes no finding as to its nature.*"

"That's fair."

"It's more than fair. Most men would have made a joke by now."

---

They had a break at ten past four, and Elias Venn was taken down to a room with a table and a jug of water in it and told not to leave the building, which he could not have done anyway.

He was sitting there with his hands round a paper cup when Ivo Pell came in, and Ivo was carrying a toolbag and had been carrying it for two hours and had been in the front waiting room since half past one on a Friday systems day, and had told the front desk he was waiting for Mr Venn and had not told the front desk anything else.

"I've got something," Ivo said.

"Which something."

"The drawer." He put his bag down and did not sit. "Six B. The flat files in the Gallery. In March I did the condition survey for the insurance schedule, all six units, drawer by drawer, because the units are nineteen years old and the schedule said *surveyed annually* and nobody had surveyed them since the year before, so I did them, and it took me two days, and it went in the systems index on the sixth of March under *schedule of collections, condition, Gallery flat files*."

"You photographed drawer 6B."

"I photographed every drawer in the room. Two hundred and forty-six exposures, four to a drawer, and I did them on a tripod with a scale rule in the frame because the schedule says *scale rule in frame where the item is a document*." He took a folded sheet out of the top pocket of his work jacket and put it on the table. "And 6B is open in all four, with the card in it, and the card says *see note on drawer 6B*, and the drawer is in the frame, and there is a date on the sheet of the fourteenth of March, and the sheet has got my name on it because the sheet is the index entry."

Elias Venn looked at the four small prints. There was a flat file, an open drawer, a card, and a rule. It was a worse photograph than his. It was in focus. It was in a Museum file with a Museum reference and a Museum date and a Museum employee's name on the front of it, and it had been taken by a person who had a legitimate reason to be standing in that room with a camera, which was the only reason in the world that a photograph of collections material is not a disciplinary matter.

"That's yours," Elias said.

"Four of them, and they're in the system index, and I'm handing them to you and you're handing them to him, and I want it said out loud in this room by somebody, so that nobody can say later that a junior guard passed them over on his own initiative out of friendship with a suspended officer." Ivo stood up very straight. "I'm handing them over in my own working capacity as the technician who took them, on a scheduled condition survey, in the ordinary way. Nobody asked me to. Nobody will ever have asked me to. That's the whole of it and I'd like it written down that way."

"Who is going to write it down?"

"You write it down." He was already at the door. "And then you give them to him, and if he types them into a statement then they're in a police record and the Museum can produce the index entry and say the drawer was open, and there's not one thing anybody in that building can do about it, and it will not be because you were clever. It'll be because I did a survey I was supposed to do in March and didn't throw away."

"Ivo—"

"Friday," said Ivo Pell, and went out.

Elias Venn sat in a room on the ground floor of a police station in Low Ward for about a minute and a half, holding four prints of a drawer that had been open since March, that were in a system index that a man's name was attached to, and then he went back up and asked to continue.

---

They typed the statement in the room with the four tables in it and he read it and corrected it and initialled every page and the last page, and the caution was recorded as accepted at 14:34 and the interview was recorded as commencing at 14:40 and concluding at 17:58, and he was given a copy.

He read the copy in the waiting room with the vending machine going on the other side of the glass. Eleven paragraphs. Paragraph six was:

> *The person states that a figure connected with a closing-day count at the Sillick Mill was obtained by a means he calls "a reading." The person has declined to explain what he means by that term. The person has declined to say whose the figure was or to whom it was communicated. The writer makes no finding as to the nature of the means or as to the figure.*

Three days, said the woman from Redgate & Kell, and he had said *a reading*, and it was in a police record in a building in Low Ward in four sentences, and that it was the first thing he had ever said out loud to a stranger that he could not take back with a decision, and he had said it to a man with a clipboard who was not going to be able to do anything with it and who was going to keep it anyway.

That was what a record was. He had spent twelve days learning it from other people's documents and he had learned it in ninety seconds from his own.

---

VPC Ainsley came back at about ten past six and put a plastic wallet on the table and did not sit down.

"Two things and then you can go, and the second one is bigger than the first and I have thought about whether to give it to you at all, and I am giving it to you, and I want it on record that I have thought about it."

"Go on."

"One. Do not telephone me about this." She said it without any weight at all. "Not because I don't want to help. Because the moment you telephone me I am your lawyer and everything you say to me is privileged and therefore unavailable to the investigation, and it also means that from that point I am not a witness to anything and I am a person you have told things to, and in nine years' time, if this goes wrong, the thing that will be in the record is that the officer's caution was handled by somebody who was being fed by the man who was cautioned. I have seen it happen. I would rather you had the advice and not the relationship."

"That is very kind."

"It is not kind, it is a rule." She opened the wallet. "Two. You are going to be asked, in about a fortnight, in a room in Northglass, whether you will give evidence about a site and a company and a date. I have read the letter you were served, because the officer showed it to me, and it is from a claims manager and it is courteous and it is asking you not to be there."

"Yes."

"Take the advice and be somewhere else. And then, when it is over, do this." She put a single sheet of paper on the table. It was typed, and it was a list, and it was a list of about twenty lines and it had been produced by a woman who had spent an hour with a telephone and a directory in a police station in Low Ward on a Friday afternoon, unpaid, for a man she had met once.

> **AINSLEY, V. (voluntary police caller) — list compiled Fri 23 Oct, from the material produced by Mr Venn and from the two public registers. Not legal advice. Not evidence of anything. A list of people who may be able to help a solicitor establish facts.**
>
> In the order I have put them, which is most recent document first, because that is how I think and it is the wrong order for a lawyer and I am not a lawyer:
>
> 1. **I. CASE**, Sillick Lane, Low Ward. Daughter of W. Case. Wrote to the Museum in about April. Has a copy of the letter.
> 2. **H. BRAMM**, Sillick Lane, Low Ward. Ran the pay queue at the Sillick Lane branch library. *Not to be approached.*
> 3. ~~A. POTTINGER, Ferry Bank, Low Ward~~ *(struck through, in her own hand, at the second column)*.
> 4. ~~THE RIVER WORKS DISTRICT CREW, South Sluice~~ *(struck through, in her own hand, at the second column)*.
> 5. **H. LOACH**, Museum night cleaning, via the Museum. (services contract — see note)
> 6. **D. SARN**, Ferry Bank. Retired pay clerk, Sillick Mill. Holds a hardback cash book with a rubber band on it. **Has stated she will not surrender it and has stated why.**
> 7. **CLOUGH, R.** and **DEAKIN, R.**, municipal payroll bureau, cost centre M.Q.S. 4. Neighbours of W. Case on a current remittance statement. Can speak to the bureau, not to Case.
> 8. **M. REID**, Grade hourly, this Museum, till September. Can speak to the time book and the CLEANING — 0 contract returns.
> 9. **QUILL & FRAYLE, 14 LINCOLN'S LANE.** Mr Rhys. Has a file for a 1974 disposal and has stated in writing that there is a clearance schedule he is not permitted to hand over. A solicitor may be able to compel the list.
> 10. **THE REGISTRY**, Northglass. Public register. Any member of the public may request an inspection. Six forms used in six years.
> 11. **The Museum's own records.** *1961/LW/44.* A drawer in a public gallery. *1961/LW/51.* A plinth. Locker 81, staff basement, **not** a Museum record, **not** Museum property.
> 12. **A police log, a consignment note, and a custody record.** All three have a number. All three are copies.
>
> **NOTE (mine, not his):** two names on this list are struck through in my own hand and I cannot tell you why I did it and neither of them was struck when I typed the list, which I typed on Thursday. Somebody with access to my typewriter has been through a document that was lying on my own desk for a whole day and has taken two people off it and has not told me and has not signed anything, and I have decided that the correct response is to hand the sheet over with both lines still visible, because a list with the reasons written on it is worth more than a list with the names taken off it. **You may take a name off this list yourself. You do not have to give a reason and nobody in this building will check. Do it in your own hand.**
>
> **AND:** a solicitor is not the only person who can use a list. If anybody tells you this afternoon that a list is evidence, that is wrong. A list is an invitation. An invitation is worth what the room on the other side of it is worth, and the room on the other side of it in November is a room you are not going to be allowed into.

Elias Venn read the whole of it twice.

Then he took out his pen and struck two names off, in his own hand, without a reason, because she had told him he did not have to give one.

He struck off number two.

**H. Bramm**, Sillick Lane, Low Ward. Ran the pay queue at the Sillick Lane branch library.

He looked at it for a long time, and then he struck off number five as well, and his hand was slower the second time, and VPC Ainsley watched him do it and did not say a word about either of the two, and when he had finished she took the sheet back and folded it once and put it in the plastic wallet and gave it to him.

"That was yours to do," she said. "I have written down the time at which this list was given and returned and I have written down the times of the two amendments and I have put a copy of the sheet with the two amendments on it in a file here that does not go to anybody, and if anybody ever asks me whether you were shown a list I will say yes and I will produce the unamended one, and I will not produce the amended one, and that is not a trick, it is the reason I said out loud that I have not read your file."

He stood up.

"You said the second thing was bigger than the first."

"It is, though you will not see it for a fortnight and you will not believe it for a month." She put the wallet under her arm. "Mr Venn. You have been doing this on your own for twelve days and you have been extremely good at it, and I have read the file, or I have read the four pages somebody let me read, and there is not one other person in it who has done anything except hold a door. In about a month you are going to be in a building with a bar on it and a list like that one, and you are going to be tempted to do the whole of it in one night by yourself, and there will be nobody there to stop you, and it will be the best night of your life and it will be the end of everything you have."

"Who stops me?"

"You have just written down two names you will not have anything to do with," said VPC Ainsley, "and one of them is the only person in this city who has said a thing out loud at you. Keep her name off and keep the other one, and you will not be able to do that on your own, and that is the arrangement. It isn't sentiment. It is a control."

---

He was in the street at twenty past seven and telephoned the claims manager's office in Northglass and got a voicemail and left a message with four words on it, because she had asked him not to say anything and he was going to obey her for as long as he could stand up.

> *Venn, 19:22. You were right about all of it. Not to me. To the file.*

And then, because he had been in a police station for five hours and because the day had been the day the four photographs had been entered into a record with a number on it, and because a claims manager in Northglass had read his four hundred words twice on the nineteenth of October and had put a reference on it in ninety seconds because it came in registered post to her and not to the building, and had said *it is the only document anybody has sent me this year that was any use at all*, and had told him not to be in a room on the twenty-eighth —

He stood in the street outside a police station in Low Ward for about two minutes, and did the arithmetic he had done every day for twelve days, and it came out the way it had come out every night since a laundrette on Sunday morning at ten o'clock, and it was shorter than it had been, and that was the whole of what had happened that day.

Three weeks. The thing he had built in a coat pocket had a reference number, a date, a set of initials, four photographs from a system index with a man's name on the front, four from a drawer in a room, and a paragraph six in a typed record in a building in Low Ward that said *he has declined to say whose*.

And a woman had not forgiven him, and had sent a piece of paper four miles away, and had not told him what was in it, and a duty solicitor had said *that isn't a trick, it's a control*, and a night cleaner had not been asked anything, and two names were struck out of a list in a plastic wallet in his coat, and a man's initials were in a corridor on a wall in a door nobody had opened.

Elias Venn got the tram up the hill at half past seven and did the dead letters, because it was Friday and there were two hundred and forty-eight of them and a clerk's is a slow job, and it is the only thing he has ever been able to do in this building that has not turned out to be somebody else's decision.
