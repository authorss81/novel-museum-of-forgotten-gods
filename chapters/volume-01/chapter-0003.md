# Chapter 0003 — A Number That Does Not Add

At ten past ten on Wednesday the treatment rooms upstairs were finishing, and the last of the lights went off in the metals room one floor above them, and Mara Iles let him into the Hall of Former Faiths with a master key and told him not to touch anything before the sentence had finished.

She had a cloth over her forearm. She had a small torch with a red end. She had the manner of a woman who had been interrupted in something careful and had decided to be extremely fair about it.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "I'm not being kind to you. I'm being kind to the object. If you're going to stand in front of it at two in the morning anyway, you may as well look at it properly, because a man who has looked at a thing properly is very much harder to frighten with it later."

"That was not what I—"

"I know it wasn't. Come on, then."

The conservation lights in the Hall were on, low, and the room looked like a different animal. Under proper light the bronze on plinth thirty-four was the colour of a wet coin, and the case top threw its own reflection back at them, and Mara went down the row with her eyes on the labels until she found the one she wanted and stood in front of it with her head on one side.

"Right. First: this label is not written by me. I know the type. I have made four hundred of them." She read it out with no inflection at all. "Tally bell. Cast 1902, bronze. Recovered on the closure of a textile works, Low Ward. Acq. 4471 over B. Unremarkable industrial object, retained for completeness of the Low Ward series. That's the whole text. That's a label somebody sat down and chose."

"What's wrong with it?"

"Two dates and a gap." She put her finger under the second line. "Cast 1902. Acq. 4471 over B. And then the next line says it was recovered on the closure of a works, which is a second date the label will not give you, and there is no textile works in Low Ward that closed in 1902, Elias, there is no textile works in Low Ward that was *casting* in 1902. The Low Ward series is the shallowest thing we hold. It was opened in 1961, it is not a deep series, and I have read every box of it." She took her finger off the glass. "So the label has cast it in 1902, acquired it in an unknown year, and put it in a series that did not exist for fifty-nine years after the casting. One of those three is a lie and the one lying is the middle one, because it is the only one of the three that anybody typed."

"That's the number."

"That is the number." She looked at him. "And I will tell you the shape of it, because you are going to go and look. A Museum accession is a year, then letters, then a number. Nineteen sixty-one, slash L-W, slash forty-four. That is a Low Ward series item and you could read it out in a telephone call. And the year on the front of a Low Ward reference is the year the *series* was opened, not the year the object came in — district holdings are catalogued by district, not by date, which is why nobody in this building has ever been able to date a Low Ward box off its own label, and which is why that box of Sillick papers will tell you it is item forty-four and nothing else. Four-four-seven-one over B is not that shape at all. It has no year and it has one letter where the form wants two, and there is no series in this building that has ever been written like it, and there is no year I can put to it that is not a lie."

"Which one do you think it is?"

"I think the number is the lie. I think somebody made the number." She looked at him. "That is not an accusation. That is a preference, and I would need a file to make it an accusation, and there is no file."

"Then make the file."

"From what? From you?"

"You just told me the two honest options—"

"I told you the two *plausible* options. I did not tell you a third one, and I am not going to, because the third one is that somebody in this building wrote a card by hand in a room and put a number on it that means nothing, and if I say that out loud then somebody upstairs will come and ask me why I did not act on it, and I will have to say that a night guard asked me a question in a corridor." She turned to the case. "Look at the casting seam, and do not touch the case, and tell me what you see."

Elias looked. "Seam's proud. They left it."

"They left it because the founder's mould had a cold joint and they were in a hurry and the bell was going to be rung that same week." She said it with a certainty that made him look again. "That's not an opinion. That's the only reason anybody leaves a seam like that. You do not leave a seam proud if you're putting the piece away. You leave it proud if it's a working bell and you have a shift to call." She moved along the case a little. "Now the lip. Look at the lip, and look at the wear, and tell me which side it's on."

He put his face close to the glass without touching it. The inside of the lip was not worn the way a rim wears, evenly, all the way round. It was hollowed in a curve, and the curve was deeper on the left-hand side, and the metal had been pushed up and outward there, the way metal moves when a soft thing is gripped hard in the same place for years.

"Left," Elias said.

"Left." She came round to look at where he was looking. "Somebody held this bell in their left hand for years. At the gate, probably, or on a bench. Not on a rope — you don't get this from a rope, you get a polish, you get even, you get the sound of it. This is a grip. This is a thumb and a fist and a man tired of holding the same thing." She straightened up. "And nothing else is done to it. No grease, no oil, no cloth, no maintenance of any kind, for as long as it was in use. Somebody rang it and carried it and never once looked after it, and they rang it hard, because there's a strike wear on the shoulder here you can see under raking light, and that's thousands of hits, and nobody was counting them."

The treatment rooms above went quiet. A door shut. Boots went across a floor overhead and stopped, and then came back and went down a different way, and the building was left with the two of them in it.

Elias had been in this building two years. In two years nobody had ever taken him anywhere in it like this.

"Now the staple," Mara said.

She got the red-ended torch and put it flat against the side of the case and raked the light down through it, and the staple came up out of the bronze like something newly born. It was bright, it was a machine staple, it was a rectangle of steel about the width of a grain of rice, and it crossed the shoulder of the bell where a crack ran.

"You can see the crack."

"I can see the crack."

"Through the staple," she said. "That's what I want you to understand. Somebody stapled this. They didn't braze it, they didn't silver-solder it, they didn't do any of the four things I would have done. They drove a staple through a crack in a working bell because the crack was letting the sound out and they needed the sound in the case by a quarter to six." She put the torch down. "And the crack is still there. You can see both halves of it either side of the staple. It has never healed. A staple is not a repair. A staple is a decision somebody made about a Tuesday morning."

"Who decided it?"

"That's a beautiful question and I have no way of answering it." She straightened up and looked at him. "It's the same hand of work as the rest of this building in the nineteen hundreds, if you want the truth. The old staff did this sort of thing. The old staff also filed things wrongly, and labeled things generously, and let a great deal go that should not have been let go. The bell is not suspicious to me, Elias. The bell is *ordinary*. It's the most ordinary object on this floor and it has been hammered and stapled and never once looked after by somebody in a hurry, and there are nine hundred of them in the store on B."

"Then why does it sound?"

"Because you say it sounds."

"You heard the case for a good quarter of an hour on Wednesday."

"No," Mara said. "You watched a case for a quarter of an hour on Wednesday. You watched it in the dark, on a monitor at half brightness, in a hall with fifty other objects in it, and a bell that makes a sound in a hall that is not making any other sound is a thing that every old building in this city has a perfectly good explanation for, and you have one, and it is on the wall, and it is written on official paper by men whose job it is to know." She picked up her cloth. "I'm not saying it isn't making a noise. I'm saying that a noise is not yet a fact, and if you take a noise to a room full of people who have to answer for the room, you had better have something under it besides your word and my inability to find a file."

"I have my word."

"You have your word and a Tuesday-night entry in a building log, which is not the same thing at all, and the difference between them is the whole of my profession."

She went out through the service door with her key and her torch and her cloth, and left Elias standing in a lit hall with a bell on plinth thirty-four and two hours of the night still in front of him.

She came back at ten to midnight with a grey archival tray, the kind you carry paper in, and said she had left it on the bench by the door for the tape repair, and went out again.

---

The hall door had swung a few degrees on its return, because the strike plate he had re-set on Tuesday had begun to ride again, which is what a plate does when its screw is in soft timber, and he had put a request in the queue about it on Wednesday morning and had not expected it answered before Thursday. He went to put his shoulder against it and hold it while he reached for the bolt with his left hand, which meant he was standing at the case when it happened, and he had both hands on the glass.

The note came out of the case and it was the loudest of the three.

It was not loud in the way a sound is loud. It was loud the way a thing is loud when it has been waiting to be at a certain volume and has finally been asked. It filled the hall from the case and it did not decay and it did not come from the metal, it came from the metal and the metal was saying it, and under it — under it, in it, in the middle of it — a man said a number.

A voice. Not in the room. A man's voice, middle-aged or older, flat with cold and tiredness, outdoors, and he was calling integers out loud, one after another, up a line, the way a man reads a line of figures off a board, and he was doing it a thousand times in a second, or the same second over and over, so that all that came through was the shape of it and not the content: a voice used to being heard over machinery, a gate in the rain, water on stone, a smell of wet wool, and figures, figures, figures, and then at the end of the run a total, and the total was there and he did not have it, it went past the place in him where numbers went, the way a name goes when somebody else shouts over it.

Four seconds. He knew it was four seconds afterwards because his own watch, when he came back, had a hand across eight of the small marks, and he had not been anywhere near it.

His hands were on the case glass, both of them, spread, and he did not remember deciding to put them there.

He was on his knees on the terrazzo when the door came back and Mara's voice said his name once, sharply, and he got up off one knee like a man coming out of cold water.

The nose was going. He put his hand under it and looked at his fingers and the blood was bright and ordinary, and he was aware in the way you are aware of your own house in the dark, of exactly where everything was, and the awareness took a great deal of concentration, as if the concentration were the price of the address.

Mara put the tray down. She came across, took his chin, turned his face to the light, and looked at him for a long second without saying anything at all, which was the single most frightening thing that had happened to him that week.

"Right," she said. "Sit down. You're concussed, you've hit your head on something, and you are not going to clean that up with your own handkerchief because you'll get it on the terrazzo and I'll have to log it."

"I opened the case."

"You did not open the case. The seal is intact and I have been looking at it for twenty minutes." She took a paper towel from her coat and gave it to him. "Now. From the top. What happened?"

"A sound."

"What else."

"Nothing else."

She waited. She was extremely good at it. She stood with her hands at her sides and gave him nothing to work against, no silence to fill, no sympathy to duck into, and after about eight seconds Elias discovered that he had a great deal of urgency in him about a subject on which he had decided to say nothing, which is itself a kind of tell, and he heard himself say:

"The lock. The hall door, it came open, I was shoving it, the sound came while I was shoving it. I think it knocked me. Or I had a — I've had a thing, a head thing, since the summer, and Dael knows about it, it's on my medical."

"You have a diagnosed condition you haven't reported to me in any of the paperwork I've asked you to sign." She said it lightly. It was not light at all. "The head thing."

"Occasional. It's not the same—"

"All right." She wrote something on the top sheet of her tray, and the pen went the way Mara's pen always went, quick and level and unhesitating, and he watched her write and understood with a cold drop that she was writing *sec notes* and not a note, and that he would find out what she had written in about nine months, if then, from a form with a number on it, in a building where he could not reach the forms.

"Do you want me to call the duty manager?"

"No."

"I have to write down what I write down, Elias. I don't have a version where I write *guard appeared to lose balance in an empty gallery* and leave it at that, because in about four years somebody will ask me to describe this evening and I will want to have been careful."

"Then write it careful."

She wrote for another few seconds. "Right," she said. "That's all. Go and sit on the bench for ten minutes with your tea and then go back to your round, and if it happens again I want to know before I find out."

She picked up the tray and went out, and he heard her heels go away across the terrazzo and stop by the door and start again, and then the service door shut, and he was alone in the Hall of Former Faiths with a bell he had not touched and a nose he had not cut and a headache that came in under the ear and settled.

It did not break. That was the part he wrote down later, in the notebook, in the small hours: that it stayed in the same place at the same level and did not shift once, and that it was still there on Thursday morning when he got off the bed, and that by then it had stopped being a thing that was happening to him and become a thing he was carrying.

He did not write down the gate. He did not write down the rain, because there was no rain in that hall. He did not write down that somewhere under a voice reading figures there had been a total he could not catch, because a man who writes that down has to say what a total is for, and he had nothing to put it against.

He wrote:

> Wed 23:40 — FoF 34. Third. Loudest by far, heard at the case, in room. Four sec. Bleeding (left nostril) on coming round. Balance off on standing. Told Ms Iles: door push, knock to head, reported head condition. Actual: none of it.

> I was on my knees. I don't remember going down.

> The bell did not make a noise. A man said numbers. I could not get the total.

> Head. Left ear. Continuous since 23:41, now 00:20, no change. Clock from here, not from when it stops.

He read the last line twice, and it was true, and he thought — as he closed the notebook, as he had thought on Tuesday about a note in a log — that if he was careful, if he was this careful, for long enough, then nobody would ever be in a position to tell him what he was allowed to know.

He put the notebook in the inside pocket, and he went out of the Hall, and he locked it, and he did the last hour of the round with a headache he could not shift and a door he kept checking behind him.
