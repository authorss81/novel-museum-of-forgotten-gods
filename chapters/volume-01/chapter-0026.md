# Chapter 0026 — What He Wants Asked and What He Does Not

The school telephoned on Friday afternoon at half past four and the woman who telephoned had a good voice and was not flustered, and the reason she gave was one sentence long and it was completely legitimate, and it was the reason the tour came forward by six days and became the event the whole of the last month had been built out of.

"I was going to leave it until the Friday," she said. "But the school is closed on the thirteenth for a curriculum review and the hall booking was made in September, and I found out in September that the thirteenth was a review day, and I did not think to ring you, and I have been the one who did not think to ring you, and I would like to move it to Saturday the seventh at the same time, if you can take us."

Nobody in the building knew about the call until Monday morning, because it was taken at the front desk by a woman on a Saturday, and a Saturday front desk writes it on a sheet and the sheet goes into a tray, and the tray goes to the systems office, and the systems office does not circulate it, because there is nothing in the culture of this building that says a rescheduling of a school booking is an event, and there has never before been one that was.

On Monday morning at a quarter past nine, Mara Iles had the sheet on her desk and had the green booking book open in front of her, and had a rubber band round her wrist, and did not go to the Director and did not go to the press office and did not go to the education office, and sat down and wrote a minute instead, in her own hand, on conservation department paper, and put a time on it.

> **M. ILES, 26 October, 09:20.** *School of per-term booking SK/BK/0912, Hall of Former Faiths, 38 children and 2 teachers, has asked to move from Friday 13 November to Saturday 7 November, same hour, for a reason given in one sentence and accepted. The booking is in the ledger. I have amended the ledger in the second column in ink and initialled it and dated it, and the original entry has not been erased, because a booking book is a record and a record that has been tidied is a record that has been read by nobody. Copy to the education office. Copy to the Director.*
>
> **And I am writing this minute before I telephone anybody, because the moment I telephone the Director this becomes a Director's decision, and it is not one. It is a Registrar's decision, it is within my own authority, and the Director's condition four is satisfied because the object has not been caged or moved and is in its last certified place under a certificate I re-issued in one sentence. I am copying her because she must be able to defend it. I am not asking her, because she would say yes and then it would be hers, and it is mine, and there is a difference and this is the difference.*

She telephoned the Director at eleven o'clock on the Monday and got her office, and the whole of the conversation was ninety seconds, and it happened standing up in a corridor outside the label room, and Anja Ward was on the receiving end of a fact that had been in the building since Friday afternoon and was a member of the Board sub-committee and had four conditions of her own issuing and had been in Northglass three weeks before and was going again on the Wednesday.

The education office was rung on the Monday afternoon and the press office was not rung at all, and the booking was confirmed to the school in writing on the Monday with a certificate, and the school had a governor, Mrs Appleton, who was told to expect a telephone call on the Friday before the visit about a matter relating to the case, and the woman on the telephone had written *I'll tell you on Friday* and had then written it out of the letter before she sent it, because she read it back, which she had not done for the woman at the front desk.

On Tuesday morning at half past ten, Elias Venn was in the director's office because he had been sent for, and Anja Ward had a certificate in her hand, and she said the thing first.

"Saturday the seventh at eleven."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Go on then. You have been in this building sixteen days and you have learned to be careful and I have not got time."

Elias Venn stood in the director's office of the Morrowgate Museum of Quiet Saints and had a choice, and the choice was about four seconds long, and he made it and it was the correct one and it cost him nothing at all, which after three weeks was so unusual that he had to stop and check it in the corridor afterwards.

"The first public event in the Hall since the object came back off the plinth is on Saturday the seventh of November at eleven, in a room with a press officer in it and a donor's office in it and thirty-eight children in it," he said, "and the bell will be on plinth thirty-four, sealed, certified, and displayed, and Annexe D is permanent and it is displayed and I have not asked for it to move and it is not going to move. And there was a telephone call on Friday afternoon at half past four that nobody told me about for two days, and a booking book with a line through it and a second column in ink and initials, and a minute in Mara Iles's hand dated Monday morning, and I did not know about any of it until I was in this room, and I am telling you that now, before you find out that I did not know, because you have told me twice this month that there is no more private channel and I am not going to start one on a Tuesday morning either."

Anja Ward put the certificate down on her desk.

"Mara told me on Monday morning," she said. "In ninety seconds, in a corridor, standing up, at about eleven o'clock, and she told me because she is a person who does not keep a director outside a piece of evidence, and because she is better than I am and she has been better than I am since about the fourteenth of October and I have not told her that either." She squared the certificate. "And she told me because she is right that the Director has to be able to defend a decision, and a Director who has not been told cannot defend it, and a Director who has been told late has to invent a reason, and I have never in nineteen years invented a reason in front of anybody and I am not starting."

"You weren't told until Monday."

"I was told on Monday morning, in a corridor, standing up, in ninety seconds. You told me four seconds ago, in this room, in a doorway, in your own words, having worked out that you were going to have to." She looked at him for a moment. "That is different. Do you understand that it is different? Mara did it to me and you did it to me and one of those is a colleague and one of those is a man who has been suspended for a fortnight and has just handed the single most useful card in the building to a school on a telephone without a plan. It is not the same and it is not meant to be the same and I want the difference on the record before I say anything else, because I have spent three weeks being the woman in the corridor who is not told, and that was a choice I made, and it was a good choice for about nine days and then it was a habit."

Elias Venn stood in the doorway and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that would not have been better said by somebody else.

"The Hall re-opens at ten o'clock this morning," said Anja Ward, "on a re-issue of the certificate of condition in one sentence, by the registrar, in her own hand, and I am not the Director for it and I am not going to be quoted about it. And the education office rang me yesterday afternoon and I was pleasant at her, and Mr Ordway had been pleasant at her on Monday, and between the two of you this building has not lost a single school booking in three weeks, which is more than anybody expected and less than anybody wants." She picked the certificate up. "Now go and be a guide."

---

The Hall opened at ten o'clock on Tuesday the twenty-seventh of October and the certificate was one sentence and Mara Iles read it out in the doorway of the room to a man from the press office and a woman from the local paper who had asked to come and look at the floor, and it was the same sentence, in the same form, with the same initials, as it had been on the twentieth:

> **ANNEXE C — CERTIFICATE OF CONDITION.** *No undue deterioration has occurred since the last certification.* **M. ILES, Senior Conservator and Accession Registrar. 27 October 2024.**

"It is a lie of omission of a size I have signed about four hundred times," she said, "and this time it is a lie of omission of a size I have signed about four hundred times *and it is also true*, because the plinth has been reset and the seal is new and the anchor plate is where it was and I have measured it myself, and a certificate is not a statement about the past. It is a statement that nobody has damaged anything since I last looked. And I have looked."

"And the public?"

"The public is from ten o'clock." She closed the file. "It has been from ten o'clock for a month. That is the thing nobody in this building understands about Annexe D. It is a classification that says *displayed*, and the cheapest way to display an object that nobody can touch is to open the room to the public, and then the classification is satisfied and the risk is carried by whoever is standing nearest the case, which is a school group, and the classification is doing its job perfectly and the job is *keep it in a public room*."

---

At half past eleven on Tuesday morning, in the loading-bay office, with the cover still standing against the wall where it had stood since Saturday night because nothing had needed it, Mara Iles put a piece of paper on the desk and took her hand off it.

"That is finished," she said. "It went to Quarry Bank on Monday afternoon. They dated it the twenty-first and gave me a receipt and it is in a safe in Irving Quay and I have a copy, and you are going to read the copy and then you are going to do nothing to it."

Elias Venn read the copy. It was the same nine lines he had read on Wednesday, in the rain, on a pavement on Civic Hill, and the last line was the one with the box round it, and the box said *MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS — LOADING BAY OFFICE — Second floor, service lane. Ask for the duty manager. Morrowgate 4 — Temple Street 0118 — 01r*, and the whole of the design of that box was that a man who has never been inside a museum and is frightened of a museum has somewhere to begin that is not a form of words.

"It has to go in a claim file," he said. "Doesn't it."

"Yes. It is the only instrument in this business that takes a man's own account, and in about a year there is going to be a hearing, and a hearing has a bundle, and the bundle has an index, and the index is going to say *occupation form* and it is going to point at a piece of paper." She put her finger on the blank part of it, which was the middle, which was the space between the two headings where the account went. "And it will go in the bundle with that space empty. And the index will say *occupation form, uncompleted*, and it will be in the bundle for the whole of the hearing, and at some point somebody will stand up and say *why is it blank*, and the answer is going to be written down and dated and initialled, and the answer is going to be *because nobody has yet found a way of asking him that does not put him in a room in which he has to answer a policeman's question in a registrar's document in a claim he did not start*."

"And you can't."

"I can. I could ring him tonight and ask him to come in and fill it in and I could countersign it as his supervisor and it would be in the file by Thursday and it would be the only document in the entire claim that anybody could put in front of a tribunal and it would be a man's own account of the only thing that ever happened to him in this building." She took her hand off the paper. "And he would have told me whatever he told me because I asked him, on a telephone, on a Sunday, and he is a man who answers a direct question, and I have had a woman's letter in a file for a fortnight that says *we know about the money* and I have never been so tempted by a piece of paper in my life. And that is the whole of it. That is why the form says *may be left blank*. It is not in the form to protect him. It is in the form to protect me from me."

Elias Venn looked at the signature line at the bottom of the typed side.

"I can't fill it in either."

"No. You cannot, and if you tried, the thing you produced would be a guard's account of a cleaner's afternoon, and it would be true, and it would be worth exactly nothing, and it would be in a bundle for thirty years with a man's name on the front of it and no words of his own anywhere in it." She picked the copy up and squared it. "You are not allowed to write a word of it. You were told that on the eighteenth of October and you agreed and you have not asked me about it since, and I have noticed that you have not asked me about it since, and I have written the date of the day you did not ask me about in my own book, because in about four years it will be the only evidence that you kept to it."

He read it again. The line that was the only line in the whole of it.

> *Do you want anybody in the room with you while you fill this in? Yes / No / I do not know yet. You may change this answer at any time, including after you have handed it in.*

"That is the line," he said.

"That is the line."

"Nobody in the history of the world has ever asked a man that."

"No," said Mara Iles. "And I did not invent it. I want to say that, because you will assume I did, and I did not. I read nine forms in three weeks, in four different buildings, in two of them on the same afternoon, and every one of them wanted a name and a date and a signature and a grade, and not one of them in any of the four buildings asked the person whether they wanted to be in the room. And I have been doing this for five years and I have watched about four hundred people fill in about four hundred forms in this building, and there is a line in about half of them that says *the person making this return may be contacted at the address below*, and not one of the four hundred asked whether they wanted to be contacted."

---

He telephoned Perry Loach at twenty past two on the Tuesday afternoon from the loading-bay desk, and it took nine minutes, and he wrote down nothing on the notebook while it was happening, which was the first time in three weeks that he had made a telephone call and not written the time of it, and the reason was that he understood, in the middle of the call, that a man who takes notes on a telephone call to another man in overalls has not made a call, he has made an interview, and that the difference between the two is the whole of the last month.

"Mr Loach. It's Elias Venn at the Museum. I rang you once, on Sunday the eighteenth, through Mara Iles, and you asked her whether Saturdays were still on. I've not forgotten that and I want to say so before I ask you anything."

"That was a good question," said Perry Loach. "Nobody's asked me that since the first of the month."

"Right. She's built a form. It's one side, printed, and it's voluntary, and any part of it can be left blank and you can hand it in blank and it doesn't touch your job or your hours or your contract, and it has your name on it and your own words on it and your own signature at the bottom, and it's countersigned by your supervisor, and it will not be filled in from a memory. And she has asked me not to write a word of it and I am not going to, and I am not going to ask you to say anything to me either, and if you want the conversation recorded, she'll come and record it herself and I'll not be in the room."

There was a pause of about four seconds, and Elias Venn stood at a loading-bay desk and understood that the pause was a man in his early sixties deciding whether a stranger on a telephone was safe, and that the man was entitled to take the time, and that the correct thing to do was to wait and say nothing.

"Go on," said Perry Loach.

"It asks one question. It's the only question. It asks whether you want anybody in the room with you while you fill it in. Yes, no, or I don't know yet, and you can change your mind after you hand it in."

Perry Loach laughed, once, without any humour in it at all.

"Is that all it is."

"That's all it is."

"Yeh. I've been trying to work out for three weeks what a man in a museum wants off me," he said, "and I've come up with money, and a statement, and somebody to be angry at, and I could not get my teeth into any of it. And it's *do you want somebody in the room*." He was quiet for a second. "Eleven years I've done Saturdays on them floors. There's not one person in that building who's ever asked me a question where the answer could be no and the no would be all right. They ask you *can you take the bin out at eleven* and you can say yes or you can say no, and if you say no then there's a conversation, so you say yes. That's it. That's forty years of floors, that is, and I've never once been asked a question that I've been allowed to leave."

Elias Venn did not say anything.

"There's a hearing," said Perry Loach. "On the twenty-sixth of November."

"There is. Six o'clock. It's a room with microphones and a fee and a form, and it's not a court and nobody's got a lawyer, and I've said you can be in it and you can say nothing and you'll be paid the fee anyway."

"Where'd you get the date from?"

"Marsh. The conciliation service. It's on a form with my name nowhere on it."

"Right." He took his time. "Right. Two things, and then I'm going to clean a corridor."

"Go on."

"**One.** Ask it in front of the people. Whatever it is, ask it in a room with the people that was in it in it. Not in an office, not through a form, not one of you in a corridor at two in the morning with a card. I was in a dye house for three years and I never heard the count called out loud once — not in forty years, not on one shift — and I'm not saying that's the reason, I'm saying I never heard it, and if a room's got people in it that was on that gate then they can hear it said."

"Yes."

"**Two.**" And here he took so long that Elias Venn checked the loading-bay clock. "**And this is the one, so write it down. I don't want anybody asking me if I've remembered it right.**"

Elias Venn stood at a loading-bay desk with a telephone in his hand and had a week of his own work collapse into about two seconds.

"Say that again."

"Don't make me say it twice, love."

"I've written it down. I want to make sure I've got it right, and I want to tell you why I'm asking, and the reason is that I have spent a month trying to get a man to stand up in a room and say a number he is certain of, and I have wanted very badly for you to be that man, and I have not asked you, because I have been afraid that asking you to be certain of it is asking you to be responsible for it."

Perry Loach did not say anything for about two seconds.

"Well," he said. "You've not asked me. And I'm telling you what I don't want, and it's a favour if you write it down, and it's the only thing I've got to give anybody this week. And the thing about it is — you do the checks, don't you. You do your checks, and I'm not the check. I've got a folder and a queue and nine years and I'm not a check. I was a fitter. I can tell you what a spanner's doing, and I could not tell you what a number's doing after thirty-one years, and the difference is not that one of us is lying."

"That's fair."

"So write down that I said it and then go and do your checks." The line went dead, or nearly, in the way of a telephone in a corridor, and Elias Venn stood at a desk in a loading bay and did not put the receiver down for about four seconds because he had a piece of paper in his hand and it needed a weight.

Then he put the telephone down and got the notebook out and wrote the time and the two things, in order, in the block hand, because a fact with no time on it is a fact with no end on it.

> *Tue 27 Oct, 14:31. P.L., by telephone, 9 mins, I took no notes on the call and the reason is mine and is in the notebook.*
> *He wants it asked in front of the people that was in it. He does not want anybody to ask him if he has remembered it right. He said the second one once and would not say it twice.*
> *He also said, and I am putting it here because it is the sentence of the whole business: he was in a dye house for three years and never heard the count called out loud once, in forty years, on any shift, and he is not saying that is the reason and he is not saying it is not the reason, and I am not going to write a paragraph underneath it.*

---

At four o'clock on Tuesday afternoon Elias Venn signed the visitors' book at the front desk as a member of the public, which he had not done since the twenty-sixth and which nobody stopped him doing, and went up to the Hall of Former Faiths to do the thing he had been putting off since Saturday, and Ivo Pell was on a systems day and came up with him at his own suggestion and then said, on the stairs, "This is not a systems thing and I am not pretending it is, and I am coming because I did the board and I know what a card is for."

There were four people in the Hall and nobody was looking at anything and the plinths went down the middle of the room, thirty-one of them, and the object was on the far one at the end, in a case, under a seal, with the fracture still visible in the shoulder under a bright machine staple.

"Right," said Elias Venn. "This is the seventh of November in eleven days' time and I am going to say the thing. I have never said it out loud in a room that was going to be in a recording, and I am not going to find out on the seventh, in front of thirty-eight children, whether I can say it."

"Go on, then."

And he tried.

It took four attempts and it was bad, and Ivo Pell, who is twenty-five and has never in his life had to do anything like it, listened to all four and did not once make a joke, which is the single most alarming thing that happened to Elias Venn in the month of October.

The first one was a briefing. He did the Hall and the object and the classification and the condition and it was four minutes long and it was the four minutes he had given himself in the corridor at seven o'clock on Sunday morning and it was a man talking at a room.

The second one he got the object right and the classification wrong — he said *it is not a devotional object*, which is a sentence about a category, and Ivo said, "That's a sentence about a box in a filing system. What's on the table?"

The third one was the one that nearly worked, and it went wrong at the fourth sentence, because he said *and I am not going to put anybody at risk*, and Ivo said, "You've just told them the risk is theirs to decide. Say what the risk is."

The fourth one he got all the way to the end and it was two minutes and eleven seconds long and it had no jargon in it at all and it said, in the end:

> "There is a small bronze bell in that case and it is a bell that was rung at a mill gate to call the shift in, and the last time it was rung anybody heard was the thirtieth of October in 1974, and it is not a religious object and it is not a saint and there is nothing in it that anybody has to believe in, and I am not a medium and I am not a priest and I have no idea whether I am doing this the way it is supposed to be done, because I do not think there is a way it is supposed to be done.
>
> "What I am going to do at eleven o'clock is put my hand on that case and ask it one question, out loud, once, and it will answer in its own words, and the answer will not be a message for you and it will not be a warning and it will not be for me. I do not know what it will do and neither do you, and that is the honest position and I would rather say it than have you find out.
>
> "Here is what it costs me and I would like you to know it before it happens rather than after. If it goes wrong I will have a nosebleed and a headache for three days. If it goes *very* wrong I will lose my name for four seconds, which I did on Saturday night, which is four seconds in which I could not produce the name of anybody, and I do not know which name, and that happened once already and I do not want it to happen in front of you.
>
> "And here is the last of it and it is the important one. If it reaches one of you — if this thing does what it did to me, and to a man with a mop on a Saturday night in October, and it puts something into somebody who did not ask to be in this room — then that is on me, and not on you, and I will say so in this room at the time, and I will say your name nowhere, and I will not do it again.
>
> "There is a woman in this room who is going to be able to stop it, and it is her word and not mine, and if she says it, it stops, and I am not going to argue with her, and I am not going to be the man who says *are you sure*.
>
> "That is all of it. Does anybody want to ask me anything about any of that before we start, and if you would rather stand further back from the case, that is the right thing to do and I would rather you did it now than at eleven."

The Hall of Former Faiths was silent for about a second and a half.

"That's it, then," said Ivo Pell.

"That is the whole of it."

"You said the woman's name is going to stop it."

"I said the woman in this room."

"You've not told me who that is, and I've been in this building for two years." Ivo put his hands in his pockets. "Elias. You have just stood in an empty room and rehearsed a consent statement for thirty-eight children and eleven adults, and it is the best thing I have ever heard anybody say in this building, and you said *the woman in this room* to a man who works here, and you will say that to a school group on the seventh of November with a tape recorder in the room and a documentary in it, and in about two years' time there will be a woman on a tape who is the reason a bell in a museum was allowed to keep ringing, and there will not be one single document in this building that says what her name is."

"That is correct."

"And you can't put it in."

"I can't put it in. If I put it in it stops being hers. It is the only thing in the whole of this business that belongs to somebody else."

Ivo Pell stood in a public gallery on a Tuesday afternoon and looked at the case at the end of the row, and then said: "Then you'd better ask her. Properly. And not in a corridor at two in the morning, and not through Mara, and not in ninety seconds. You've had four minutes with a bell in front of you and you have not had four minutes with her, and that is the actual bug in it, and it is not in the object."

He went down to the systems office at five, and Elias Venn stood in the Hall of Former Faiths for about ten minutes by himself, and then went and did the dead letters, because it was Tuesday and there were two hundred and fifty-five of them and a clerk's is a slow job, and a clerk's is the only thing in this building that has never once needed him to be brave.
