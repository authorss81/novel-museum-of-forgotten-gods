# Chapter 0032 — The Notice and the Quiet

The Registry's morning post comes up on the Tuesday and the Wednesday and the Thursday, in a grey wallet, and it goes into a tray on the front desk and somebody signs the tray, and the tray is a public document in the sense that anybody who comes through the door can read the top of it while they are waiting to pay a pound a head.

Elias Venn was on the desk at 07:00 on Wednesday the eleventh of November because he was on the desk at 07:00 most days, and because the post had been on the desk at 07:00 on the Saturday when there was a press officer and a camera in the building, and the desk is the one place in this museum that nobody has ever restricted.

The wallet came at twenty to nine. There were four things in it: a printed sheet with a rota on it, a circular about a fire door, a return envelope, and a single sheet with **QA/N/xx** in the top right corner in the Registry's typeface, which is the typeface on every statutory instrument in this country and is a typeface that has never once been used to be pleasant to anybody.

He read it standing up. It was two thirds of a page. He read it twice and then a third time with his finger on the bottom third, and then he took it to the loading-bay office, because the loading-bay office has a table, and because the front desk is public.

> **REGISTRY MAINTENANCE NOTICE — QA/N/xx**
> **Object:** 1961/LW/44/09 — hand bell, Hall of Former Faiths, plinth 34.
> **Observation:** The object's certified condition is not capable of being maintained in its present location. The Hall's environment is within tolerance. The object is not.
> **Recommendation:** That the object be transferred to a controlled store, on the ground that no public-room certification can be sustained for a carried pattern in an unaltered condition indefinitely.
> **Action required:** None.
> **Countersigned:** [ ] Registry assessor (Schedule 12) [ ] Museum officer (not the Director)
> **NO ACTION REQUIRED — TICK BOX NOT TO BE COMPLETED BY THE ADDRESSEE.**

And at the foot of it, where the Registry's notices put the thing that makes them notices and not instructions:

> *Issued under maintenance procedure 4(b). A maintenance notice records a recommendation. It is not an order. It confers no authority to move any object.*

The countersignature blocks were empty. One of them was for a Registry assessor and the other for a Museum officer who was not the Director, and neither of them had been filled in, and there was a date against the second one in faint pencil, and Elias Venn, who reads these, worked out inside about four seconds that a date in faint pencil in a countersignature box on a maintenance notice is either an assessor who came in October and filled it in and nobody countersigned, or nobody at all.

He got the file. He had asked for it on Monday and it had been sent, because a request for a file is a public procedure and a request for a room is not, and this was the fourth time in five weeks that the difference had done the work.

**Annexe D.** Aurelia Roth's classification of 19 October, attached not heard, in her own words on the certificate:

> **Quiet Accord classification: Annexe D (permanent). To be displayed. Under no circumstances to be removed from public view by a museum acting alone.**

**Annexe E.** Read into the record at 13:10 on Saturday 7 November by Ottilie Grange, under procedure 11, on a disturbance report, in about four minutes.

> *A Schedule 12 carried object which has been reported as having reached, or as being likely to reach, a person who has not consented to it, in a public room, is classified as a disturbance, and is **stayed** from all handling, use, cleaning, movement and public access until a material-change inspection has been completed and a determination made.*
> *This is not a closure. The object remains where it is. The room remains open. Nothing has been withdrawn from the public. The lights are on and nobody may touch it.*
> *Dispute route: the requester, in the form of a request.*

He put the two sheets side by side on the table in the loading-bay office and looked at them for a long time, and then he did the thing he was frightened of doing, which was to write it down, because a man who writes a thing down has to look at it.

> **Wed 11 Nov, 09:24.**
> **The Registry has recommended the fix.**
> **The fix is blocked by two documents this building produced.**
> **Annexe D is the Registry's own assessor's work. The assessor who wrote the thing that stops the notice is on the same rota as the assessor who wrote the notice.**
> **Annexe E is this building's own procedure, applied correctly, by a Registry officer, on a report this building's own front desk took, on a form this building's own front desk accepted from me, because I asked for an inspection in a corridor on 19 October to get a sentence into a file that would embarrass a Director.**
> **So the document that recommends the fix is the document that blocks the fix. Nobody has to be wicked. Two competent people in Northglass did their jobs on the same object eight days apart.**

He sent it to the Director at 09:31 with a covering line of one sentence, and the sentence was: **Registry maintenance notice QA/N/xx, received 08:40, recommending transfer to controlled store; not actioned; Annexe D and Annexe E; for your signature or your refusal.**

Anja Ward came down at 10:05 and read it standing, and she did not sit, and she read it twice.

"No," she said.

"That is what is in the blank at the foot of it."

"You are not entitled to write in that box, and it is fortunate for you that the box says so as well." She handed it back. "Counter-sign it. Leave the box alone. Under procedure 4(b) a maintenance notice that is not actioned is not a failure; it is a notice, and the file records that the addressee was advised and declined to act, and that is a complete file, and the next assessor who opens it in ten years will read three lines and think nothing of it." She turned to go and stopped in the doorway. "Mr Venn. It is the correct decision. It is also the reason that Registry put *no action required* in capital letters at the bottom of the sheet, and they did not put it there to be kind to me."

The Gallery of Unnamed Hands was quieter.

That is not a thing a person can say about a room in a way that anybody else can check, and Elias Venn had known that for a moment before he said it to himself, and said it to himself anyway, standing in a corridor at half past ten on a Wednesday morning, and then spent a quarter of an hour proving it.

He could not go into the Gallery. He had a key card that would not open the door and a restriction that would not let him through the door with a key card if the card worked, and the door has a counter on it, a little mechanical wheel, that is reset by a systems technician on a Tuesday.

The counter said one hundred and twenty-six.

He had read it on the seventh of November at nine in the morning, on his way up, because he reads them, and it had said five hundred and eighty-eight, and it had said four hundred and ninety-one at half past ten on Wednesday the fourth, which is what a Wednesday at half past ten is supposed to say in a building that does about two thousand people a day.

One hundred and twenty-six, on the eleventh of November, at half past ten on a Wednesday, with the Hall open and the Gallery open and a term running, is a quarter of a Wednesday. He stood and looked at it for a while and then went up one floor and did the same thing to a corridor instead of a room.

The level-two archive corridor. There is no door on it and no counter, so he stood in the middle of it with his back to a wall, in the way you stand in a corridor when you are not doing anything, for about six minutes, and he had a notebook, and the notebook was no use at all, because you cannot time a corridor by looking at it.

What he could do was this. In October the level-two corridor had a sound in it, and the sound was the far end of a building, and the building has a heating system that is older than he is and makes a noise all day like a held note, and there is a lift that comes and goes, and there is a fire door on the east that is on a closer and bangs about four times an hour, and there is a person on the desk at the front who is seventy-one and does the schools' letters and a schools' letter is about nine pages.

At half past ten on the Wednesday of the fourth of November, in a corridor, for six minutes, he had counted eleven things: the plant, the lift twice, the fire door once, the schools' letters once, a trolley going past on the other side of the glass, and a man's voice from the office of the quiet room on level two, which is a room for a school group to be quiet in, and the man's voice was the only human sound in the whole of the six minutes.

At twenty to eleven on the Wednesday of the eleventh of November, in the same corridor, for six minutes, he counted four.

He was embarrassed by the counting. He did it again at ten past eleven and got five, and he wrote down the word *four (and five)* on the back of a dead letter envelope, and the word *or* in front of it, and the word *or* was the honest part.

Then he did the one that was hardest to do, and the hardest one was the shortest walk.

The Quiet Wing corridor is on the top floor and it is barred to him and there is no counter on its door because his key card does not open it at all, and the thing he did instead was stand at the bottom of the stair that serves it, on the half landing. It is a public stair. Anybody in this building may use it. And he listened.

He had done that on about six occasions in five weeks, always at the same time, always for the same length of time, and he had never told anybody he was doing it, and it was the only thing he had ever done in this building that was pure waste.

On Wednesday the fourth of November, half past ten, six minutes: he counted the same list again and got to eleven, and then he went back over his own list twice and took one off, because the civic repeater at the far end of that corridor is dead since the fifteenth of October and a bracket with no bell on it is not a sound, and he had put it in the list out of habit, the way you count a light that is off. Ten, then, and one he had not heard: a door forty feet along on the far side, which he could not hear from a half landing and would not have heard if he had gone up.

On Wednesday the eleventh of November, twenty past eleven, six minutes: four things. Five at the second run.

---

He had been in the loading bay since about a quarter to twelve, doing the one job he has, and at seven minutes to twelve it happened.

It came up the stairwell.

It was one stroke of a small hard bell, and it was a service bell and not a ward bell and not the hall bell, and it had a pitch, and the pitch was about half a tone under where a pressed-brass service bell of that size sits, and the sound came from above him and behind him and it arrived in the loading bay with everything else that arrives in a loading bay, which is the flue from the boiler, and a door two floors up that is on a closer, and the wind in a duct.

It was there for about a second and a quarter.

And Elias Venn, who is a man who thinks in doors and clocks and exits and stains on floors, stood absolutely still in a loading bay with a clipboard in his hand and worked out where it was from, and got it right, and it took him about four seconds.

The only pressed-brass service bell on a corridor wall in this building that has been connected since 1908 is the one beside the unlisted door on the Quiet Wing corridor. It is on a line to the porters' board. The porters' board is a dead repeater bracket halfway down the same corridor, with no bell on it and no bell on it for years, and the line to the security room died on the fifteenth of October when the Registry notice declared the third ward bell out of service for the quarter, and the third ward bell is on the same riser.

So the bell rang in the corridor. It did not ring in the security room. It rang about sixty feet from a bracket with no bell on it, in a corridor he is not permitted to enter, and it did not carry, because the wire to the only place in this building that would have turned a ring into a person is dead, and nobody came, and nobody was going to come, and there had been nobody on that corridor at eleven o'clock on a Wednesday morning in November since about 1979.

A service bell of that kind does not ring by itself. It has a plunger on the wall beside it and a wire to a sounder and somebody has to press it.

He looked at his watch, and then he read the same time off the clock over the security room door, because a man who is going to put a time in a notebook has to be sure of the time, and because those two are not always the same answer.

11:52.

He stood in the loading bay and listened for it to happen again, and it did not happen again, and he waited until four minutes past twelve with his eyes on the stairwell door, and nothing came down it.

Then, at about a quarter past twelve, he went upstairs as far as he was allowed to go, which is the second floor, and stood at the top of the Quiet Wing stair on the half landing with his heart going, and listened for four minutes, and nothing.

He got a pencil out on that half landing and put it in the notebook on the page with two lines on it from yesterday, and he wrote:

> **Wed 11 Nov, 11:52. Quiet Wing corridor. One stroke, pressed-brass service bell beside the unlisted door. About a second and a quarter. Half a tone flat — a semitone, near enough, under where that bell sits.**
> **Dead since 15 Oct. It rang in the corridor and it did not come to the security room and the line to the board is dead. Nobody came. Nobody was going to come.**
> **Nothing on that corridor at 11:52 on a Wednesday.**

And then he stood on that half landing for a good deal longer than four minutes, and the thing he was doing while he stood there was not listening any more, it was arithmetic, and he was embarrassed by the arithmetic, and he did it anyway.

If the corridor outside the unlisted room is quieter than it has been in eighteen years, and the repeater line died on the fifteenth of October, and it was quiet before the line died, and the room is quieter than it has been in eighteen years, then the quiet is not the wire. It is not a fault. It is not a dead bell. It is the room, and it has been the room since 2006, and the wire going in October is a coincidence of dates and nothing else.

And if a corridor is quiet, and a gallery is quiet, and an archive corridor on level two is quiet — and he put the word down — if all three of them are quiet on the same morning, and they are all in the same building, and the one thing in the building that is under a stay from all handling, use, cleaning, movement and public access is on plinth thirty-four in a public gallery, then it is a small bronze bell about the size of a fist with a crack in its shoulder under a staple, and it is not a god and it is not a saint and there is nothing devotional about it, and it is carrying something.

Or holding something.

Or standing in the way of something.

Elias Venn had got as far as that on a half landing at twenty past twelve on the eleventh of November, and then he did the thing he does with a thing he wants, which is to go looking for the thing that would make it false, and he found one inside two minutes.

The notice in the Director's office at ten past five the day before does nothing. It is countersigned and it cannot be executed, and nobody has acted on it, and nothing in this building has changed since the seventh of November except a drawer, a cupboard, four photographs in a police file and a set of keys that now have a person to hand them to. A thing that does nothing is not the reason three corridors are quiet. And if it were the staying of the object that had made the Hall quiet, then the Hall had been quiet since the seventh, and the counter on that door said five hundred and eighty-eight at nine o'clock on the Saturday morning, and a gallery that reads five hundred and eighty-eight at nine in the morning is not a gallery that has gone quiet.

Which left him with three quiet things and no cause for any of them, and with the additional difficulty that he had measured all three on a Wednesday in November, and a Wednesday in November is the day of the week on which a building like this one is at its least, and he had no other Wednesday to set it against. He wrote that down as well, on the same page, in the same pencil, because a man who keeps the bad line as well as the good one is a man who can be argued with.

And he stopped there, deliberately, with the pencil still in his hand, because of the sentence he had said out loud at the end of a row in a public gallery on a Saturday morning at ten to eleven and had meant.

*If it reaches one of you, then that is on me and not on you, and I will say so in this room at the time.*

If that was a fuse, then the people behind it were in a public gallery with thirty-eight children in it, and the people behind it were in a library queue in a room built for school groups, and the people behind it were a woman of eighty-one who had come on a bus and a man of seventy-eight with a plastic carrier bag, and the people behind it were in a loading bay at ten at night and in a corridor at one in the morning, and they were all in rooms he had been in all month.

He counted them, once, and stopped, and that was the second time in two days that he had started and stopped.

Then he went downstairs and did not tell anybody.

He did not tell Ivo, who was in the systems office with a mug and would have believed him and would have written it down. He did not tell Mara, who has said to him four times that there is no more private channel and who is right. He did not tell Dael Ordway, who does not want to know things. He did not ring Anja Ward, who would have gone and looked, and the moment she went and looked somebody would know that a night guard had asked.

He did not tell anybody for four days.

He told himself that this was the protocol, and it was not, and he knew it was not while he was saying it, because the protocol is a card on a plinth with five lines on it and one of the five is a witness in the corridor who can say a word, and the protocol is for the object, and this was not the object. This was him, on a half landing, at twenty past twelve, with a time in a book.

He did it because of what the sentence would do. If he said it, it would be a thing that had been measured, and a thing that has been measured in a building like this one is a thing that goes in a systems index, and a systems index is on a screen in a room with a kettle in it, and Ivo was right about that on Tuesday morning, and Ivo is right about it about twice a week.

He did it because he was not sure, and a man who is not sure should not start a mechanism.

He did it because of the twenty-sixth of November.

And that was the real one, and he worked it out at about half past four in the loading bay, and it took him the whole afternoon to get to it and he got there by going round it eleven times.

On the twenty-sixth of November, at six in the evening, in a room, with a fee of a hundred and forty pounds payable in advance by the people who come, there are going to be six families. Six people who were on a closing shift at the Sillick Mill on the thirtieth of October 1974, or a woman of eighty-one who was on the looms, or a man of seventy-four with a frame, and they are going to be taken through everything that this Museum knows, and every word of it is going to be read out and kept.

And he is going to have to stand up in that room and explain what a bell is.

Not what this bell is. What a bell is. He has had that coming since the tenth of October and he had it written on the back of his hand at ten to eleven on a Saturday, and the sentence he had written was *what is this bell for*, and it came out of a man's mouth in the rain, and eleven, and then a room.

And if it is a fuse, and the people behind it are in rooms with people in them, and he says so in a room on the twenty-sixth, then what he has said is: *this object is making this building quiet, and I have measured it, and the people it is quiet for are in your dining rooms.* And every person in that room who is seventy-eight or eighty-one is going to hear a young man from a museum tell them that the thing that has been in their chest since 1974 is in a building in Civic Hill and is doing something to the heating.

He put the notebook away at half past four and did the dead letters, and there were two hundred and eighty-one of them, and a clerk's is a slow job.

At about eight he went up the hill and it was still not raining and he got the tram and sat on the wrong side of it.

He had worked out, over four hours, with a pencil and the back of a dead letter envelope, four things, and he had them in order.

One: the object is stayed, and a stay is from movement, and a stay is a decision this building cannot undo, and it was caused by a form he lodged at a front desk on the nineteenth of October, and the dispute route for the stay is his own form, and on Thursday the nineteenth of November at eleven o'clock there is a material-change inspection in a room he may enter as a member of the public and nowhere else in this building.

Two: the notice that came this morning cannot be executed, and it is blocked by the Registry's own assessor and by this Museum's own procedure, and if it cannot be executed then the object stays exactly where it is for an unknown period, and the people who are hurt by that are the people in the rooms, and the number of people in the rooms has gone down by something and he has three figures that are four, five and maybe four, and he cannot prove any of them, and the word *or* that he put in front of them on a dead letter envelope at half past eleven is the honest part of the page.

Three: he is the only person in this building who can tell that this has happened, because there is no instrument for it, and an instrument is the only thing he has ever been able to argue with.

And four, and this was the one he did not like: **the good news and the bad news are the same document.**

For five weeks he has been trying to get a bell out of a public gallery by a lawful door, and the lawful door is a form, and the form has worked, and the object is on plinth thirty-four with a clamp and a staple and a number on a card, and nobody may touch it, and the Hall is open and the lights are on, and the count stops when the room is full and starts when the room is full and there is no procedure on earth for stopping that, and every time he has got a little further with a document the count has got a little further with a room.

And on the eleventh of November the building went quiet at about half past ten in the morning and a bell in a corridor nobody is in rang once and did not come down the wire and nobody came.

He got off the tram at Ferry Bank with the rain starting and walked the long way, past the shuttered shop, and he was at the laundrette at ten to eleven because the laundrette shuts at ten and there is a bench by the door and his mother is behind the counter doing the one thing she does between four and five on a Sunday, and it was a Wednesday and she was counting coins and looking at the machines.

She said, "You've come at a funny time."

"I've come at the normal time," Elias said. "I always come at the normal time."

"You've come at the normal time eleven times in five weeks," Helen Venn said, "and you haven't asked me a single question in any of them, and I'd like you to know that I have noticed, and I'd like you to know that I'm not going to ask you about it either, because you're my son and you'd only tell me the version you've rehearsed."

He sat down on the bench by the door. Eleven machines ran. The boiler ticked.

"I'm not going to be able to come on Sunday," he said.

"No," said his mother. "You never can."

That was the whole of it. He had come to tell her something, and she had not asked him for it, and he had not said it, and he was going to walk out in about ten minutes having said nothing, and he had told her the weather for next Sunday by telling her he was not going to be there, and she had said *you never can*, which was true, and which was also the sentence about a man who has been asked four times to produce the reading he has never produced and has not once said yes or no to anybody in this building.

He got up at half past eleven and she let him go, and on the way out he stopped, and he said:

"Have you ever had one of these days where you have worked out something in the morning and then you have spent all day not being able to do it, and then in the evening you have worked out that the not being able to do it is the thing, and it is the only thing, and you cannot put it in a box?"

His mother put a coin in a box.

"Yes," she said. "That's the worst of it, that. The finding of it. Everybody can get through a day on not knowing. It's the knowing you've got to get through the day with, and there's not one single thing you can do with it."
