# Chapter 0019 — Standing Instruction 14

It came down onto the systems terminal at ten past nine on Monday morning with a number in the top right corner and no covering note, and it was four pages, and it was signed by three people, and it was called **STANDING INSTRUCTION 14 (2024) — OBJECTS IN TRANSIT: SUSPENDED QUARANTINE**, and Elias Venn read the whole of it standing up at the loading-bay desk because a man on a restriction has nothing else to do with a Monday.

Clause one: no Schedule 12 carried object to be removed from the building except on a docket countersigned by a person who is not an officer or employee of the Museum.

Clause two: public access to a Schedule 12 carried object is suspended for the duration of a proposed disposition, and the suspension is lifted only on a written notice in the form at Appendix B, which is one sentence long.

Clause three, and he read it three times:

> *Unaddressed and returned correspondence held in the post room shall be retained for twelve months from receipt and then destroyed. No member of staff shall open, copy, act upon or discuss a returned item of correspondence without the written authority of the Director. The existence of a returned item of correspondence is not of itself evidence that the addressee is or was a member of staff, employee, contractor or other person.*

Clause four:

> *Staff lockers in the basement are reviewed at the annual staff audit. Any locker whose plate does not correspond to a current member of staff shall be recorded, and its contents held pending enquiry for twelve months, after which the Director may authorise disposal. A locker is not a Museum record and its contents are not Museum property.*

Clause five: any photograph of collections material, labels or records taken by a member of staff is a disciplinary matter and may be referred to the Board.

Clause six: **no document, image, record or copy of any Museum material may be lodged in any Museum file, Board file or departmental file which the Museum has not itself requested, without the written authority of the Director.**

And then, at the bottom of page two, in a different hand, added last, initialled, and dated the eighteenth of October at a quarter to four in the afternoon, in the margin, in ballpoint:

> *7. The Museum will not destroy, or permit the destruction of, any record of an individual employee held by the municipal payroll bureau without first obtaining the bureau's own account of that record in writing. The Museum's records are not the only records of a person who has worked here.*

Elias Venn stood at a loading-bay desk and read clause seven until he could have said it out loud, and had to put his hand on the edge of the desk, because it was Mara Iles's, and because she had written it into a document that three people had signed and an institution would be held to, and because she had done it on a Sunday afternoon in forty minutes while he was on a stair landing being told that he was not going to be allowed near any of it.

And clause six was two lines above it.

---

He worked out clause six in about four minutes, standing at the desk, the way he worked everything out.

On Tuesday the thirteenth of October, at ten past six in the evening, he had written four hundred words on a sheet of A4, in a loading bay, in his own hand, saying what he had done and when and with what, and he had sealed four photographs in a Museum envelope, addressed it in block capitals to the Board Secretary, and handed it to a duty manager who had written **VENN, E. — 18:24** in the day book and initialled it, and it had gone into the Monday bag, and it had gone out of the building in registered post that morning with a receipt, and he had stood on a step in the rain and been, for about four minutes, a man whose evidence was in the world and could not be reached by anybody who worked here.

The Board Secretary's office is on the fourth floor of the building the post went to.

A registered-post item is delivered to a building and signed for at a desk and carried up four floors, and if it is a *record* of the Museum's business, and it arrives at the Board Secretary's office, then it is in a Museum file, and the Board Secretary is the Board's employee and not a member of the Board, and the envelope is a document that the Museum has not itself requested, and clause six requires the written authority of the Director for it to be lodged in any Museum or Board file.

Clause six did not say *destroy it*. Clause six was, on its own terms, entirely reasonable. There had been a review of the schedule of contents. There were four hundred items in the Low Ward series. If anybody in this city could put a document into a Museum file, then a Museum's records are a bin, and a bin is what a museum's records become, and every archivist in the country would write clause six and every one of them would be right.

And it had taken him one week to learn that a Museum's most dangerous record is the true one, and that a Museum's best defence is a piece of paper that tells you what it is not allowed to hold.

The two men from Redgate & Kell arrived at half past ten and were let in at the service entrance, because a press office at a museum in a crisis does not use the public doors, and they were extremely quick.

The older one was named and the younger one was not, and Elias was not going to be told either name, and both of them were polite, and both of them had read the *Chronicle* and the *Chronicle*'s picture of the weighbridge.

"We're not here about the fall."

"Then what—"

"We're here about the object." The older one had a single sheet. "The Bell. Hall of Former Faiths. On Wednesday we asked a routine question about the condition of a Schedule 12 object and the press office said it would come back to us today, and today it has come back with a form on it, and the form says *the object is subject to an internal review of documentation and is not available for comment*, and that is a *phrase*, mate, and somebody in that building wrote that phrase this morning and we would like to know who and why and on what authority, because we have a photograph from Sunday of a site being cleared and a piece in Monday's paper about a gate and a telephone number in this building."

"I'm security. I can't—"

"Sure." The younger one had not said anything. Now he said: "Is it still in the Hall?"

"It's not in the Hall."

"Where is it?"

Elias Venn stood at a loading-bay desk in a building in which he was not permitted on the collections floors and thought about clause six, and about clause two, and about a woman with a stopwatch on a Saturday night, and about a woman in a chair at a back table in a library.

"It's in a cage in a corridor on the ground floor," he said. "It's in a cage by the lavatories, it's got a red disc on the cage with the Director's initials, and the corridor's four feet wide and the cage's on castors and two of the castors don't turn, and the door to the lavatories swings into it. That's not a secret. That's what happened this morning, and a transfer clerk wrote *noted* on a plan and did not do anything about it, because it isn't his job."

Two men looked at each other.

"Thank you," the older one said.

"That's the second time this week a man has said that to me and I have not got a single thing out of it."

Vail & Thwaite rang the security room at noon and it was the claims manager, and she rang the security room, and not the Director's office, which told Elias that a firm of claims managers had worked out on Monday morning that a suspended night guard is a fact-finding resource.

"Mr Venn? Cheryl Vosper. I understand you're not on duty."

"That's correct."

"Right." She was brisk and she was not unkind and she was, he understood inside about forty seconds, a person doing a job that she was good at. "I'm handling the claim arising from the fault report of the tenth of October and I'm also handling the one arising from Saturday. I've got a file on the fault report with your name on a line at the bottom of a supervisor's summary, and I want to say two things to you and then I'll let you go."

"Go on."

"One. The summary's fine. It's a correct summary and the only wrong sentence in it is one you put your name to, and if the Board never looks at anything else in that file, they will be all right." A pause. "I have been doing this nineteen years and I have never once said that to anybody, and I would like you to understand that I am saying it because if I do not say it to you, somebody above me will, and they will not be as straight about it."

"Thank you."

"Two. I've had a letter from a firm called Quill & Frayle about a site at Low Ward, and there is going to be a hearing, and it is on the twenty-eighth, and it is in Northglass, and the claim is not yours and I am not your solicitor and I am going to have to ask you not to be in that room."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair, it's my job, and I'd like to be straight with you about the difference, because in about four years somebody is going to say that the insurance company kept a man out of a hearing." She let a second go by. "Mr Venn, there's a second page in the file."

"What page."

"A photograph," said Cheryl Vosper, "of a card, and four small prints of the same card, and a covering note of four hundred words in your own handwriting, and a sentence at the bottom of it that says *if this is evidence it is evidence against me first, and I have written it so that it is.*"

Elias put his hand flat on the desk.

"I've read it," she said. "I've read it twice and I have put a reference on it and a date, and it is now in a file with a number, and I did that in about ninety seconds and it took nobody's permission, and it is the only document anybody has sent me this year that was any use at all."

"Then it isn't under clause six."

"It's nowhere near clause six, because it came in registered post to me and not to the building, and because I am not the Museum and the Museum has no authority over my post." She sounded briefly pleased with herself. "It came in an envelope with your name on it in block capitals, and it came to a claims address that is printed at the foot of a fault report form in your own post room, and I want to say that you got that right, and I have no idea whether you got it right on purpose."

"It was on purpose."

There had been two envelopes. One of them was on the fourth floor of the building Elias Venn was standing in, in a Board file that nobody had asked for it, under a clause an archivist had written for the express purpose of stopping exactly that, and it would stay there for ever and it would be worth nothing to anybody for ever. The other one was in Northglass, in a plastic wallet, with a reference and a date on it, ninety seconds after a woman had opened it, and it had taken nobody's permission, and neither had the first one, and that was the part he had not understood at twenty past nine on Tuesday evening when he had walked down to a branch post office in the rain with two envelopes in his coat and one of them addressed to a building four miles away.

He had wanted two. He had wanted it the way a man wants a second signature. It had taken him four days to understand that he had not wanted a second copy of a document at all, and that what he actually wanted was a fact, and that a fact is the only thing in this building that does not need anybody's permission to be true.

"Then you are a better shot than anybody on that docket." A pause. "The hearing is on the twenty-eighth. You are not in the room. And if somebody gets a witness statement out of you about that site before then, I will know, and I will be cross, and it will make my job harder and it will not make yours better."

Ivo's systems inventory was presented at seven that morning in the systems office and Elias was not in it and was not asked and would not have been allowed.

He had told Elias on the Tuesday of the previous week that it was the best work he had ever done and nobody in that room cared about one line of it, and he was almost right, and the almost was the part that mattered.

The outcome came down on the terminal at two o'clock, in a minute, in the systems office's own format, and Elias read it at the desk.

> **Systems index 6B — schedule of collections.** Restored. Twelve months of the Museum's own locations, insured values and custody entries, reconciled from the paper series against the Registry's register on 14 October by S. Spence (metals) and R. Priest (post). Discrepancies: **eleven**, of which nine are location errors of 1980s origin and **two** are of record. Two are referred to Audit and Assurance. All items located except **one**, accessioned as *unaccessioned, carried, on deposit* (ref. QA/CR/1187), location recorded as *in situ, QA/A/2, level 2* and physically found in a cage in a service corridor, ground floor. **Location corrected.**
>
> **Fire compartmentation, third compartment, west range.** 4-hour rated, in date to March. Note added 12 Oct: *Schedule 12 carried objects in the west range are within a rated compartment; in date to 31 March next year.* **Renewal date for this Museum: 8 April.**
>
> **Loading bay, cage 2 (metal, mesh, four castors, two seized), current position blocks the swing of a door on a designated route of escape.**

Elias Venn read the last line and sat down on a sack of frames.

Ivo Pell had done in six minutes on a Monday morning the thing that a suspended man, a conservator, a night supervisor, an assessor and a claims manager had not managed in two weeks: he had written down, in a document that nobody had to pass through the Director to see, in the most boring format in the world, that the cage in the corridor is a fire hazard, and he had done it in the third section of a system inventory about bin numbers and drain falls, in a numbered line, in a form, on a Tuesday morning in a systems office with four people in it.

Which meant it was not an allegation. It was a schedule note. And a schedule note in a systems index gets a reference and gets an action and gets chased by whoever has the action, and it did not need anybody to be brave about it or to have lost anybody.

Elias found him at the workshop door at ten to three.

"I've read the minute."

"Then you've read the best line I've ever written." Ivo had his sleeves rolled up and a mug of machine coffee and he had not lost weight since the summer, which was the first good piece of news in a fortnight. "It's a fire thing. It's a block on a route of escape. It's got a number. Nobody has to be a hero about a fire thing, mate, they just have to move a cage."

"You didn't ask me."

"No."

"You've still not asked me."

Ivo put the mug down and looked at him for a second and a half.

"Elias," he said. "I have been not-asking you for two weeks and it has been the only thing keeping you out of a police file, and I have got four hours to do a presentation I have already done, and if I ask you what I am supposed to say when a man tells me about a locked door at ten at night in a gallery, then I have to say something, and I do not have anything, and you will go away and do it on your own again and I will be an accessory and I am twenty-five." He picked the mug up. "You do not need a system. You need somebody to hold the door. Those are different and I have spent two years trying to work out how to be the second one of those in a building that is full of the first."

Dael Ordway came in at half past five on his day off, which he had not done in eleven years, and stood at the loading-bay desk in his coat with his hat in his hand and did not take it off.

"I'm not on till seven."

"I know that. That's why I came." Dael put a sheet on the desk and squared it, and it was a working note, and it was written in pencil, and it was the fourth such note Elias had seen in two years and Dael had never once let one of them leave that desk. "I am not allowed to do the docket. That's in the standing instruction. Clause one. It's mine and it isn't mine any more, and I have signed every one of them, the disc, the docket and the book, and I have been signing things for eleven years, and on Monday morning a woman in Northglass put a form on it that says a director's signature is not sufficient, and she's right, and she knew it before I did, and I have been signing things for eleven years."

"Who's doing the docket?"

"A transfer clerk called Nunn. Foyston's. He's got a van on the lane at eight tonight and he's got a key to the cage and he's got a docket, and the docket is a Saturday-dated one that I wrote, and it's still got my initials on it, and it is the only piece of paper in this building that the Museum is *doing* something with." He tapped it once. "And I have spent a day working out how a cage in a corridor by a lavatory gets to a van, and I have got two answers, and the first one is that nobody moves it, and the second one is that a transfer clerk does."

"Which is it?"

"It's the second one, because I can't do the first one and say I've done it." Dael's mouth moved. "Elias, do you know what a cage is? It's not a decision. It's furniture. It came out of the loading bay for people with bad backs and it's been in the bay since before you and it's got a padlock on it now with a Registry tag on the padlock. And a cage in a corridor by a lavatory is a *door*, and a door in a fire is a thing that kills people, and I am not having my name on that. So it's got to move, and it has got to move because of a systems index, and that is the only way out of it I have been able to find in two days."

"Then it moves on Saturday," Elias said, "on the docket, with the chain."

"It moves on Saturday if the second signature is on it, and the second signature has to be somebody who is not an officer or employee of this Museum, and I do not know who that is going to be, and neither does the Director, and it is half past five on a Monday and I am off nights and I have to be standing in this bay at eight tonight with my own name on a docket that says something that isn't true."

He picked the note up and put it in his pocket.

"Right," he said. "I've said it out loud in a room, and you've repeated it back to me, and if anybody comes looking for me at six in the morning, you'll know where I am and you didn't hear it here."

"Ivo," Elias said. "It's Ivo's systems note. It has a number in it. It says *location corrected* and it says *block on a route of escape* and it went to a systems office with four people in it on a Monday morning and it is in a document that anybody can walk in and read."

Dael Ordway stood in a loading bay and did not say anything for about four seconds.

"You told him," he said. "When."

"Tonight. Twenty past five. He was in the workshop and he was doing a presentation he'd already done and I told him that a cage in a corridor by a lavatory is a fire thing and it wants a number."

"You told him." Dael put his hat on. "Right. You've had it since eight weeks and you've not asked him once, and you have got exactly until six in the morning to notice that." He got to the door. "You know that's the first useful thing you've done all fortnight."

Then he stopped, and turned round.

"Oh, and one more thing, and it's the reason I came in on my day off." He was not looking at him. "The minute of the staff audit came out on the same form as the minute of the sub-committee. Item 5 on Tuesday is the schedule of contents. And the director's minute says — and I'm reading it off the paper, so I'm not editorialising — *it is noted that a records clerk has been replaced by a systems technician*."

Elias Venn looked up.

"They've taken the records clerk's post," Dael said. "They've had a records clerk in this building for twenty-six years and she retires in March and the post has gone to systems, and a systems index is a bin count with a date on it, and in a couple of years somebody upstairs is going to ask me where the Museum's *records* are, and I am going to have to tell them that the records are on a shelf on level two with a fire plan in front of it, and that the woman who knew every one of them is gone, and that the only person who has looked at that shelf in four years is a man who is currently not allowed to be on the floor."

He went out into the rain.

Nunn came at nine with a clipboard and a man and a cage on a sack truck, and the cage had two castors that turned and two that did not, and they had brought a **witness form**, because a transfer clerk's standard form has a witness line on it, and the line said:

> *Present at removal: name, grade, time, and signature.*

Elias Venn read the whole of the page twice, and then he read clause one of the standing instruction, and then he read the witness line again, and then he understood, standing in a loading bay at nine o'clock on a Monday night, that for the second time in eight days he had been handed a piece of paper with a box on it.

It wanted a name and it wanted a grade and it wanted a time and it wanted his signature, and it was a *witness* and not a *record*, and it was not asking him what he had seen or what he thought or what he knew.

And it could be filled in by a man with no authority whatsoever, because a witness is anybody, and a member of staff is anybody, and the words *in his own hand* are nowhere on it.

He wrote:

> *Present at removal: cage 2, metal, mesh, four castors, two seized, from the west range service corridor (ground floor) to the loading bay, secured pending a disposal decision. Contents: one bronze bell, accessioned as 1961/LW/44 (see tag), which is **not** the object described in this docket, and which is not removed by this movement and is not part of it.*
>
> *21:05, 19 Oct. I have seen what has been moved and I have seen what has not. I have not been told what is in the cage and I have not asked and I have not opened it. I am a member of staff and I am under restriction and I am not a conservator and I am not an inspector. I am the only person in this building on a Monday night and I have signed this because somebody has to, and if you want a witness for what is inside it, you want a person whose job that is, and she works on the first floor and her name is on the other side of this paper.*
>
> *E. VENN, security, grade 1 — signed, in my own hand, in the room, at the time above.*

Then he did not hand it straight back, and he stood for a second with it in his hand, because he had learned that from Mara Iles on a bench in a corner of a locked gallery, and because it is the whole of what he had learned in eight days, and he was going to learn it or he was not going to learn anything.

"Mr Nunn."

"Sorry?"

"You've filled in the location as *cage 2, west range*." He held the form up. "It was in a corridor off the west range. It's a service corridor. It doesn't have a room number, it has a lavatory. And in about four months somebody's going to write a schedule with a location on it and this is the document the location will come from, and if the location is wrong then in four months it is wrong in a fire register and nobody will ever be able to tell whether the cage was on a route of escape or in a store."

Nunn looked at his own handwriting for a moment and said, "That's fair," and put a line through it and rewrote it, and signed his own initials in the margin, and gave it back.

"Thanks," he said. "Nobody's ever corrected one of those before."

"They're not used to being wrong," Elias said, and did not know that it was not true, and it was not.

He was in the building until a quarter to eleven and there were two hundred and six dead letters and a cage standing in the middle of a loading bay with a sheet of polythene over it and a padlock and a tag, and he did the letters, because the letters are a slow job and a clerk's is the only thing he had ever been good at that nobody can turn into a mistake.

At half past ten the pay clerk's clipboard came in on the Friday round and he copied the list onto a sheet because that is the job, and there were nine names on it and two of the surnames went on the sheet, and then he turned the sheet over and there was a new page clipped to the back of it, which the pay clerk had left on it because she is a pay clerk and she had been told something and she had nowhere to put it.

> **NOTICE TO ALL — PERSONNEL RECORDS AND TIME BOOKING**
> With effect from **1 November** the time book and the punch-card system at this building are abolished.
> From 1 November, hours are recorded at a terminal, by badge, in the security room.
> The **payroll bureau** is not affected. The bureau continues to administer grade hourly staff (cost centre M.Q.S. 4) and requires no change.
> Queries: payroll, Thursdays.

Elias read it, and read it again, and had to sit down on a sack of frames.

Because in six weeks this building was going to stop having a book with a name and a time and a signature in it, and the people the book had been keeping were not the ones on the terminal. The book was for the cleaners and the porters and the men who came in at nine and went out at three on a Saturday. The terminal was for badge. And what the notice was actually saying, four lines down, in a sentence about a payroll bureau that somebody in an office had added to a notice about a time book because they were being careful:

**The bureau is not affected.**

The cleaners were not on M.Q.S. 4. The cleaners were not on the bureau. The cleaners were on a **services contract**, and a services contract is written in *establishment, shift and hours*, and a services contract does not have a person's name in it, because a person's name is a data matter and an establishment is a service, and there is a schedule in that contract that says, in its own words, that contractor personnel are not employees of the client and are not to be recorded on the client's staff roll.

Elias Venn sat in a loading bay at half past ten on the Monday of the second week of October and read a piece of paper about a time book and understood the whole of the volume in one sentence.

There is no document in the city of Morrowgate in which the name of the man who said ninety-four on a plinth at ten o'clock on a Saturday night appears. Not a payroll roll, because he is a service and not a person on a bureau. Not a personnel binder, because the binders stop at 1974 and there is no W. Case in any of them and there is no leaver's record. Not a rota, because the rota was right. Not a post-room letter, because the letters in the post room are addressed to a letting agent in a building four miles from where he sleeps, and eleven years of them went out of a door in a blue bale every Friday morning and were never opened by anybody.

And the only form in this building that wants his own words has a box for a name and a countersignature and a grade, and it is sitting in a file on the first floor of this building with the box **blank**, and the woman who wrote it has told him to his face that she is going to build him a form he is not allowed to write a word of, and that it is not for him.

He got the clipboard and turned it over and looked at the bottom of the pay clerk's list, and there was a third page, which was the weekly contract returns for the cleaning service, and it was a printed grid with cells in it, and the cells were filled in by hand, and the cells said:

> **CLEANING — 0**
>
> HALL OF FORMER FAITHS — 8h — 3 days
> GALLERY — 2h — 2 days
> WEST RANGE / CORRIDOR — 1h — 7 days
> LOADING BAY — 30m — 7 days
> QU. WING CORRIDOR — 1h — 7 days

No name. A zero. A service, with hours against it, split by *room*, and one of the rooms was a corridor with a cage in it, and one of the rooms was a corridor outside an unlisted room, and one of the rooms was the loading bay, and the numbers in the grid were hours, and the sum of the hours was a quantity of work and the sum of the work was a sum of money, and somewhere in the arithmetic of it was the man with the mop and the tin of tea in his bag and the hearing aid in his pocket that he did not turn on, and there was no column in the whole of the printed grid in which a person could be written.

He put the clipboard down.

*Nine objects and forty-one drawers in a room with a paper on the wall saying nine. And a corridor with a cage in it and a contract line for a corridor with an hour a day against it, and no name. And nine. Nine was the number the suppression had been using for thirty-six years, and it is the number this city is still using tonight, and it is not a lie, and it is not an accident, and it is not going to be a mistake that somebody made.*

He got the notebook out at the loading-bay desk and wrote the time against it, because he had been taught that by a woman with a stopwatch and had never once been able to explain to himself why it worked, and then he wrote one line, and then he shut it and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, where it had been every night since the sixth of October, and which was the one thing in this building that no standing instruction had yet found a clause about.
