# Chapter 0021 — The Form

The form was on the loading-bay desk at nine o'clock on Wednesday morning and it was one sheet of A4, printed on one side and typed on the other, and it had a number in the top right corner that was not a Museum number, and Elias Venn read all of it standing up in about ninety seconds and then sat down on a sack of frames and read it again.

There were no boxes on it. That was the first thing. There was no box for a name and a grade and a countersignature, and there was no box headed *account of any person present during result*, and there was nothing on it that wanted anybody's version of anybody.

> **OCCASIONAL ACCOUNT — MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS: CONTRACT CLEANING SERVICE**
> **This form is voluntary. Any part of it may be left blank and the paper may be handed in blank. Leaving it blank has no effect on your work, your hours, your pay, your contract, or your immigration status.**
> **Write in your own words. Do not ask anybody to write it for you and do not let anybody write it for you. If you cannot write, say it out loud to the person who takes it and they will write down what you said and read it back to you and you will say whether it is right.**
> **This form is not filled in from a memory. Nobody is to help you remember. If you do not know, write *I do not know* and that is the correct answer and it is worth more than a guess.**
> **It will be countersigned by your supervisor.**
> **Do you want anybody in the room with you while you fill this in? Yes / No / I do not know yet. You may change this answer at any time, including after you have handed it in.**

And at the bottom of the typed side, in a box with a rule round it:

> **MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS — LOADING BAY OFFICE**
> **Second floor, service lane. Ask for the duty manager.**
> **Morrowgate 4 — Temple Street 0118 — 01r**

He read the whole of it again.

That was the form. That was ten days of a registrar not sleeping on Sundays, and it was the only document in the whole of this business that anybody could put their name to and be proud of, and there was a telephone number in a box at the bottom of it with a room in it, and the room was a room he was permitted to be in.

Elias Venn put his hand flat on the desk and looked at it for a while, and then he understood what he was about to do, and he did not do it.

There was a signature line. It was at the foot of the typed side, and it said *signature of the person making this account* and next to it *date*. He did not sign it. He did not sign it because it was not his, and because a man who signs a form on behalf of a person who has not been asked has done the exact thing the form exists to prevent, and because he had signed a device-book entry with no destination once and a fault report with four false words in it once and he knew precisely what his signature was for and on this piece of paper it was for nothing at all.

He wrote one line in the day book, in pencil, against the Wednesday, and put the form in an internal envelope and wrote *M. ILES — FORM 1/1 — DO NOT FILE* on the outside of it in his own capitals, and did not put it in a Museum file, because clause six of Standing Instruction 14 forbade him and forbade her and forbade everybody, and because this was not a Museum document yet. It was a piece of paper.

Quarry Bank Solicitors was four rooms above a shop in Irving Quay and it had been there since before either of their births, and Mara Iles had chosen it on the Tuesday night for four reasons, all of which she gave out loud on the pavement of Civic Hill at ten past ten in the morning while the rain came and went.

"One. They are not on the Board's panel and they will never be on the Board's panel, because the Board's panel is four firms and three of them have acted for the Hallowgate people at some point and one of them has acted for us. If this goes to a panel firm it becomes an institutional document in about a fortnight and an institutional document gets a reference and a reference gets a file."

"Two. They do unpaid accounts. That is all they do. It is not a thankless field. It is a field with about nine hundred households in it in this city and every one of those households has been told by somebody that the road is closed."

"Three. They are a two-partner firm and one of the partners is a woman of about fifty called Nan Quill who was a union shop steward at a hospital laundry for eleven years, and I have telephoned her and I have not met her, and I told her on the telephone exactly what the form is and she asked me one question, which was *can it be left blank*, and when I said yes she said *then it is the only form anybody in this city has ever given me that I could hand to a man in a corridor without insulting him*, and that is the person who is going to hold it."

"And four?"

"Four is that they have a document schedule and a date of receipt and a place of safekeeping, and they will tell you the day it arrived and they will not lose it, and if I die in a stairwell tomorrow then it is in a firm in Irving Quay and not in a Museum file, and I would rather it were in a firm in Irving Quay."

Elias Venn stood on a pavement on Civic Hill in a coat that had not been dry since Tuesday and listened to a woman he had argued with for ten days explain, in the order she had decided to explain it, why she was taking the only good document in this whole business four miles away from the building it was about.

"You've told them what it's for," he said.

"I have told them what it *is*."

"What is it."

"A piece of paper." She put her bag on the wall and took a folded sheet out of it and gave it to him, and it was a copy, and it had a Quarry Bank letterhead on it and a number in the corner and a date, the twenty-first of October, and a sentence in the middle.

> *We are asked to advise on a matter concerning an employee of the cleaning contractor engaged at the above premises. We are not instructed and do not wish to be instructed upon the merits. We note that the document enclosed is voluntary in its terms and confers no obligation upon any person to complete it. We will hold it, date its receipt, and return a copy of this note to the instructing party. We are not able to assist with enquiries made by third parties in the absence of that party's written authority.*

"And that is it," Mara Iles said. "That is the whole of what I have sent. One page. No claim, no name, no number, no word about a bell and no word about a mill. There is no document in the city tonight that could be used against anybody, and if it is stolen out of their safe tomorrow it is a piece of paper with a box in it and a telephone number in the box."

"You'll want something to go with it eventually."

"I will want about nine things to go with it eventually," she said, "and every one of them will need a man's hand, and I have not got one, and I am not going to have one before I have got this one. That is the order the steps go in and I have thought about it for ten days and I am not going to be told the order by anybody else this week."

Elias Venn put the copy back in her hand.

"Perry Loach said yes."

"He said yes to being asked whether he would be asked. That is not the same as saying yes to filling it in, and if anybody puts those two things next to each other in a document I will take it apart." She looked at him. "I have not been in a room with him. I have spoken to him on the telephone once, on Sunday, and he did most of the talking and none of it was about the form. He asked me whether Saturdays were still on. That was the whole of the call. He wanted to know whether his Saturdays were still on."

"Are they?"

"I don't know," said Mara Iles. "There is a cleaning contract and I have never read the cleaning contract, and I am going to read the cleaning contract this week, and I would rather you did not help me do it."

Redgate & Kell came down the service lane at half past ten on Wednesday morning and were let in, because a press office at a museum in a crisis does not use the public doors, and they were extremely quick, and the older one was named and the younger one was not and neither of them had been told anything they had not read.

"We asked a routine question about the condition of a Schedule 12 object on Wednesday," the older one said, "and we were told it would come back to us on Wednesday, and it came back on Wednesday with a form on it, and the form said *the object is subject to an internal review of documentation and is not available for comment*, and that is a phrase, and somebody in that building wrote that phrase."

"I didn't write it."

"No, mate." He had a card out. "Third question, then, and this is the one that goes up."

"Goes up where?"

"Wherever it goes. That's not our line of country, that's yours." He turned the card over. "**Is the object in the Hall of Former Faiths at this moment?**"

Elias Venn looked at the man for about a second and a half.

The object was on plinth thirty-four. The object had been on plinth thirty-four since ten to eleven on Tuesday morning, sealed, re-anchored, classified Annexe D, permanent, with the crack in its shoulder under a bright machine staple that was never going to heal. The Hall was open. It was ten o'clock on a Wednesday and there were nine people in it and a school group due at eleven on Friday the thirteenth of November.

The answer was yes, and it was true, and he said it.

And then he stood at a loading-bay desk and waited for it to be a lie, because it had been a lie every other time he had been asked anything, and it was not, and the not-being was the part that put a weight on him that he could not name for about a week afterwards. **There was no man in the case, no corridor, nothing on the floor of the building that was not where it was supposed to be, and every part of the answer was a true sentence.** He said, "It's on plinth thirty-four. It's been on plinth thirty-four since Tuesday morning. It's sealed and it's certified and it's open to anybody who comes through the door this morning."

"That's the Director's," said the older one, "or it will be, by about two o'clock, and I'll take that as a referral."

"You can take it as a referral."

"There's always a referral," said the older one, and was not unkind about it, and wrote *third question, referred, 10:40* on the card, and went up.

The press office built a holding statement by twenty past twelve and it was four lines long and every word of it was true:

> **MORROWGATE MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS — STATEMENT 3**
> *The Museum's collections are displayed in accordance with the Quiet Accord's Schedule 12 classifications. An object classified at Annexe D is displayed in the Hall of Former Faiths and is available to view at all times that the Hall is open. The Museum does not comment on internal documentation reviews. Media enquiries: the office of the Director.*

And it went out at half past one to a woman in a copy of the *Chronicle* who had a picture of a weighbridge on page four and had asked three questions and had been given a form and a phrase and now a statement, and who read it, and who wrote *available to view at all times that the Hall is open* in her own notebook, in a hand that had been trained at some point and had gone rusty, and who did not know anything about the sentence and only knew that it was a sentence.

And here is the thing Elias Venn did not know and could not have known, standing at a loading-bay desk at ten past two on Wednesday afternoon in a building he was not permitted above the ground floor of.

On the Saturday of that week, at four o'clock in the afternoon, a registrar who is not an officer would sign five clauses and a note saying that the Hall was closed to the public on Saturday and Sunday for protective floor treatment, that a sealed case could not be coated with an object standing on it, that the case would be staged in a ground-floor room with a cover on it and not on the case, and that the public view would resume on the Tuesday on a re-issued certificate in one sentence. She would write the whole of the risk on the same sheet, in her own hand, and sign it, and go up.

Which meant that on the Sunday morning of that same week the object would not be in the Hall, and Statement 3 would be a lie, and the building would have put it on the wire three days before it became untrue, at the speed at which buildings issue true sentences, by people who were not on the floor and would not be on the floor when it stopped being true.

He could not have prevented that. He could not have known it. And the sentence on the wire would be *available to view at all times that the Hall is open*, and the Hall would be shut, and the *times that the Hall is open* would be none.

That is what a well-meant institution is, and it took him ten days and a room with eleven people in it to work out: a set of true sentences issued at speed by people who are not on the floor, and the floor is a different department, and nobody in the building is required to know that the two can come apart.

The poster went up in Lantern Row on the Wednesday morning and it was a paste-up on the hoarding by the second-hand bookseller, at about the height of a grown man's shoulder, and it was A3 and it had a photograph on it.

The photograph was of a weighbridge. A cast-iron weighbridge set into brick, with a hump over it and a small hut beside it and a gate behind, taken in about 1961 with a square-format camera on a slow shutter, and the light in it was flat and northern and the sky was the colour of a wet road.

Underneath the photograph there was one line of type in the Museum's own house style.

> **Sillick Mill weighbridge, Low Ward. Demolished 1998.**
> *Morrowgate Museum of Quiet Saints — Our City in Objects. Civic Hill.*

That was all of it. There was no credit beyond the museum's name and there was no caption that said anything at all, and there was no collection number, because the press officer who had done the lantern row paste-ups in the third week of October had gone into the picture library's Low Ward folder on a Friday and had taken the first Low Ward photograph in the folder that was not a person, and had put a caption line under it that took up the space a caption line takes up, and had not looked at the number of the thing in the photograph because the number was in a drawer in a gallery four miles up the hill on a plinth with a felt top and a card that said *see note on drawer 6B*, and the drawer was open, and the object had been taken off it in the dark, with a torch, on a Wednesday night in the second week of October, by a suspended night guard who was not permitted in the room, and by a conservator who was, and neither of them wrote anything down.

Nobody in Lantern Row looked at it twice. It was a picture of a weighbridge, and there are nine hundred weighbridges and about forty of them are still in use, and it was a hoarding with posters on it, and one of the posters was a school play and one was a locksmith and one was a picture of a bridge that had been built in 1888 and had a name on it.

Elias Venn stood on the pavement on Lantern Row at ten past two in the afternoon and looked at a photograph of the front of Sillick Mill, and there was a gate in it, and a hut beside the gate, and the gate was the gate, and seventeen days later there was going to be a poster of it on the wall of the lobby of the building on the hill, facing the door, at four foot six, which is the height of a nine-year-old's eye.

He wrote it in the notebook in the flat block hand, with the time against it, and then he read the line back and did not cross it out, because a fact with no time on it is a fact with no end on it, and because he had learned ten days ago that a man who writes down the fact that his own institution is doing the thing has already stopped being the person who can stop it.

At three o'clock on Wednesday afternoon Elias Venn came round the west range for a bin and found that the west range corridor had a new handrail.

It ran the length of the corridor from the fire door to the end, at the right height, in galvanised tube, on nine stand-off brackets, and it was screwed to the wall and not to the mesh of the cage, which was two feet away and which the corridor had been fouling since the twelfth of October, and it was good work, and whoever had done it had not been in a hurry and had capped the ends and had painted over the two bracket heads that had got in the way of a tray.

The cage was still in the corridor. It was going out on Friday. It was a piece of furniture in a corridor of escape and it was going to be a piece of furniture somewhere else by Friday morning, and the handrail was not the point of anything. The handrail was there because Ivo Pell had a systems day and had nothing else to do with it, and because a corridor with a cage in it and no handrail is a corridor that a person with a trolley cannot get down, and because Ivo was the kind of man who fixes the thing that is in front of him.

Elias followed the handrail to the end of the corridor and found the new board.

It was a proper board, not a notice: a plywood panel, four hundred by three hundred, routed at the edges, with a Vitrex front and a lock, and it had been screwed to the wall beside the fire assembly point, and it had not been there on Tuesday, and the old noticeboard was eleven feet away in the staff room with three curling sheets and a fire drill rota from 2019 on it.

Ivo was on his knees in a high-visibility vest with a driver bit.

"Don't," he said, without looking round.

"I'm not going to say anything about it. I want to read it."

"You can read it when I'm not holding the last screw. Ninety seconds."

Elias Venn stood in a corridor on a Wednesday afternoon and read a notice board through a sheet of glass with a man on his knees in front of it, and it was four lines, and it was headed:

> **ASSISTANCE AT EVACUATION — WHO MAY DO WHAT**
> *A person is assessed at Levels 1 to 4 on the Museum's assistance register. Level 4 means the person cannot move through the building without being moved by another person.*
> *An evacuation party shall never include, in its tasking, any person assessed at Level 4 or above. If a party is found to include a person at Level 4, the party does the job. If a party is required to do a job and cannot, the party goes out. The party goes out first and the building empties around it.*
> *A party is a party. A pair of people is a party. A person on their own is a party.*
> *If you are not sure whether you are a party, you are a party.*

Elias read it four times.

"Which is yours," he said.

"Mine is the third one. That took me about a fortnight." Ivo got the last screw in and stood up and put the driver bit in his pocket. "And the first one is the good one and the second one is the important one, and I want to explain the second one to you because in about a week I am going to be asked to explain it and I would rather have said it out loud to you first."

"Go on."

"The old one said parties assist persons at Level 3 and above. Level 3 is somebody who can get about with a hand on a shoulder. Level 4 is somebody who cannot get about at all." He took his gloves off. "Which is fine on paper. But it means the job — the actual job, moving a person who cannot move — falls on a party, and a party is a rota of four people who were all at lunch at the same time, and on the day the rota is the whole story, and there is no level of competence in the sentence at all. So the new one doesn't say parties can't do it. It says the party does the job and then it goes out. The party empties the room and then the party leaves. And the building is left with a person at Level 4 in it and a fire appliance, and that's correct, because a fire appliance is a thing with a crew and a training and a statutory duty to come, and four people off a lunch rota is not."

Elias Venn looked at the board.

"That's a good rule."

"It's a *correct* rule," Ivo said. "That's different and it's worse, and I want you to have both words, because in about three days somebody is going to work out what it does."

They did it at half past three on Wednesday afternoon, in the systems office, on Ivo's systems day, and it took about forty minutes, and nobody was a villain, which was the part that made it hard.

The Museum's evacuation parties are a printed list. They are on the wall behind the old board in the staff room in a plastic wallet, and they are revised — the last revision was stamped on the top sheet in November, and before that in the year before, and before that nobody could remember, and the whole thing is revised on a Friday afternoon in October by whoever is going to be in the building over Christmas, and it is a thing everybody in a building like this has learned to treat as wallpaper.

There are nine parties on the list. Ivo read them out loud with a finger, with the assistance register open beside him on the same screen, because the assistance register is a live document and the party list is a piece of paper from a previous year, and nobody had ever put the two side by side because nobody had ever had a reason to.

"Ground, west. Loach, P., Porter, C., Nunn, R." The first party had a man at Level 4 on it.

"Ground, central. Priest, R., Spence, S., two from the ticket office."

"Second, east. Callaghan R., Crowther M." Neither of them had been a porter and Callaghan was at Level 4 in the assistance register, with a date of assessment from 2016, and the party had been carrying his name for eight years, and it worked every year because he was never the person who needed carrying, and the rule is not about the day it works.

"Second, west, Quiet Wing. Six names, and one of them is at Level 4, and I am not going to read the level out, it's in the register, it's his business."

"Sub-level. All five parties, and three of the five have somebody at Level 3 and one has somebody at Level 4, and the parties overlap, and the overlap is the whole reason the list is short, because the same four people are in the west party and the sub-level party and if you stand all of them down to get the Level 4 person out then the building has no party on the second floor at all."

Nine parties and nine failures.

"It's not a list of failures, though," Ivo said, when Elias looked at him. "I want to be exact. It's a list of *parties* and every one of them is non-compliant, and it is non-compliant for one of three reasons, and only one of the three is anybody's fault. One of them is that the party has a person at Level 4 on it. One of them is that two parties share members and standing both down empties a floor. And one of them is that the party does not exist on the day, because the person who is supposed to be in it is on a different shift, and the party list was made in October by whoever is here over Christmas and the other shifts were not consulted."

"And the fourth reason."

Ivo turned the screen round.

"The fourth reason is standing arrangements," he said. "And they're not parties, they're not on the list, and they're in a separate file, and the file is called *fixed positions*, and it's got about nine entries in it, and the entries are things like *the cage at the west range is static and is not to be moved during an evacuation without the Director's written instruction*, and *the west range corridor is a designated route of escape and is to be kept clear at all times*, and *level B, bay 9, is a rated store and its contents are not to be moved in an evacuation*."

"Which of those is a person."

"None of them." He scrolled down. "The last one. It's dated the sixteenth of September, and it's the day the last whole-building review was done, and it says: *west range service corridor, ground floor — one observer, fixed position, minimum distance three metres, no contact, at all times when the corridor is in use.*"

Elias Venn stood in a systems office on a Wednesday afternoon and read a line that somebody had written in a fixed-positions file on the sixteenth of September about a cage on two seized castors and a man who was not allowed in the room at the end of the corridor.

"That's about the cage," he said.

"That's about the cage."

"It's got nothing to do with me."

"It is not about the cage," said Ivo Pell. "It is about *one observer*. One. Fixed position. Minimum distance three metres. No contact. At all times when the corridor is in use. And the observer is not named, because nobody writes a name in a fixed-positions file, they write a post." He shut the screen. "And the file has not been opened since the sixteenth of September, which is five weeks, and the whole of the party list in the other wallet has not been opened since November, which is longer, and I opened both of them this afternoon because I was putting a board on a wall and I am the person who books the Registry and I had a half hour."

"Take it down."

"No."

"Mr Pell—"

"Not on my own." Ivo did not look at him. "The board is mine. The board is a notice board and a notice is a person's job and I have got a sister's chest to pay for and a Thursday in this building that I would like to keep. But the list is not mine. The list is a fire document and it goes to a safety officer and it goes through the Director, and if I hand it in on a Wednesday afternoon off my own bat, then somebody is going to look at this and work out inside about nine seconds that the entry that breaks it hardest belongs to the one person in this building who is currently suspended, and they are going to do the sensible thing, which is to leave the list alone and take my notice down and give me a week's holiday and think about it in the new year."

"So it sits there."

"It sits there until somebody with a letterhead puts it in front of somebody who has to answer for it." He picked his gloves up. "That's the same as your request form and you know it, because you put yours in on a Monday morning and it has taken four days to get me a meeting."

The consequence landed at twenty past four on Wednesday afternoon and it landed on Mara Iles first, and then on Dael Ordway, and then, at about ten past five, on a man in a telephone in Northglass who had a school on Hall Lane on his rota and a group of thirty-eight in a ledger on a desk.

She came down to the loading bay with a telephone in her hand and a piece of A4 in the other hand and she was not walking quickly, which was how he knew.

"Read that."

It was a printed extract of the Museum's assistance register and the Museum's party list, side by side, with a column on the right headed *non-compliant* and nine ticks in it, and underneath, in a different hand:

> **Hall of Former Faiths is opened to school groups as a constituted party. The Hall's party is not constituted. Under the new instruction the Hall cannot be opened to a party while any party for the Hall is non-compliant.**
> **Therefore the Hall cannot be opened to a school group.**
> **This applies to all bookings, term and single, including those made before this date.**

"Term and single," Elias said.

"Term and single."

"That is thirty-eight children on the thirteenth of November."

"That is every school group in this city between now and Christmas," said Mara Iles, "and the Hall has been shut to parties since three o'clock this afternoon, and I have had a woman from the education office on the telephone already, and she was extremely reasonable, and she said *when can you certify it*, and I said *I don't know*, and she said *I'll ring back this afternoon*." She put the sheet down on the desk. "And I want you to understand that nobody in this decision did anything wrong. The rule is correct. Ivo's rule is correct and he is right that it is a good rule, and it has been his afternoon and he is going to be pleased with himself for about a week, and I am going to have to spend the rest of the week on the telephone with a woman in an education office, and at no point in any of it is there a person I can be angry at."

"Who signs it off?"

"The safety officer. It is her document; I have never seen her and she has never seen me; she came in from outside on Tuesday and has been here two days and she did a fire risk assessment of the kitchen because that is what she was engaged for and it took a photograph of a combi-oven." Mara squared the sheet. "She is going to be delighted. Every one of those nine parties is a fire document that has been wrong for between one and eight years, and she has been in the building two days. This is the best week she is going to have in this city for a decade."

"And the bell."

"The bell is on plinth thirty-four in a room that is now shut to parties, and a room that is shut to parties can still be open to the public on a general admission ticket, and the general public can walk in and stand in front of a case all day and touch nothing." She said it without any emphasis at all. "Which is exactly what a room with the lights on that nobody may touch looks like, and I want you to notice that nobody in this building arrived at it by a decision. It arrived by a rule, correctly applied, on a Wednesday afternoon, in a corridor, by a man putting a board on a wall."

Dael Ordway came on at about ten past five and did not take his coat off, and Elias Venn had not seen him since Tuesday afternoon and had not expected him.

"Have you heard about the Hall?"

"I've heard about the Hall."

"Then you know that I put the whole of Movement Three on a booking in a green ledger on a security desk and that a man of twenty-five has closed the Hall to parties with a piece of plywood." Dael put his hat on the desk and left his hand on it. "That's the tour. That is the only card on the table and it has been turned over, and I have been in this building eleven years and I have never had a card turned over that fast."

"Can it be re-certified?"

"There is nothing to re-certify. I asked the question in the only way anybody can ask it, which is by telephoning a woman in an education office and being pleasant at her, and she said that a school group is not a party, it is thirty-eight people in a gallery with two adults, and it is the adults who are the party." He took his hat off again. "And the adults would have to be constituted and assessed, and that is a thing you can do, and it is a thing you can do in about a fortnight if you start today and do nothing else, and every one of those forty adults is a member of staff of a school and not an employee of this Museum, and the assistance register is an employee register."

"Which means you cannot assess them."

"Which means I cannot assess them, and there is no version of this building in which thirty-eight children from a school on Hall Lane can be on our assistance register, and while that is true the Hall is a room with the lights on that nobody may touch." He said it entirely levelly. "I have been thinking about that phrase since about half past four and I have decided I like it and I am not going to pretend to you that I know what to do about it."

Elias Venn looked at the loading bay, at the corridor, at the cage, at the corridor beyond the cage where the handrail was new and bright and capped at the ends.

"Mr Ordway. In your capacity as the man who read paragraph nine and the schedule of costs."

"In no capacity at all. I am a night supervisor on my day off."

"You are a man who has been in this building for eleven years and has read every clause on that card and every clause on the standing instruction and every clause on the docket, and you read a booking book on a Tuesday that nobody had read in two years, and you have done those things and I have watched you do them, and so you know what I am about to ask and you can answer it before I do." Elias put his hands flat on the desk. "Is there another book on a desk in this building?"

Dael Ordway did not answer for a while.

"There is a booking book for the Hall and there is a diary in the front office and there is a letting diary at the porter's desk that the porter's desk has not had since the letting office closed," he said. "And there is nothing else that I have read and nothing else I have not."

"Mr Ordway—"

"I have not read the green book again since the Tuesday," Dael said, "and I read it once, and I read one line, and I have thought about that line every day for eight days, and I have not been able to find a single reason why anybody has ever wanted a school to come and look at a bell." He picked his hat up. "Ring your solicitor. Ring the claims manager in Northglass. And do not tell me what either of them says, because I am going to be a witness on Wednesday and I am not going to be a man who has been told things by the man in the middle of it."

He went out into the lane.

Elias Venn worked until nine and did the dead letters, because the dead letters are a slow job and a clerk's is the only thing he had ever been good at that nobody can turn into a mistake. There were two hundred and thirty-four of them and it was a Wednesday.

At twenty to eight he got the form out of the internal envelope and read the second side of it again, and the last line was the one he had read four times and understood four times and not once been able to put down:

> **Do you want anybody in the room with you while you fill this in?**

Nobody in the city had ever asked a man that. Not the Museum, which asked for a grade. Not the contractor, which asked for an establishment, a shift and hours. Not the payroll bureau, which asked for four lines and gave a cost centre. Not Hattie Bramm's queue, which asked for a name and a street and never once asked whether the person wanted to be in the room.

Somebody in this city had sat down and gone through every form a person can be asked to sign in a building like this one, and had gone through it again, and had noticed that the single thing missing from all of them was the only thing that was actually a question, and had printed it in a box.

He put it back in the envelope and wrote on the outside, under DO NOT FILE, in pencil, in his own hand, with the time against it:

> *Wed 21 Oct, 19:52. It is the only line in it. There is a place to begin at the bottom and a telephone number and a room I am allowed into, and there is one question in the whole thing and it is not about money. I have not signed it and I am not going to sign it, and if I sign it then in four months there will be a piece of paper in a Museum file with my name on a line that says a man spoke, and he will not have spoken, and I will have made him a document.*

Then he counted the dead letters again, because a fact with no time on it is a fact with no end on it, and it was Wednesday, and it was only eight o'clock.
