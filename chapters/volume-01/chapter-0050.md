# Chapter 0050 — Sillick Lane

He was on the pavement outside the Museum of Quiet Saints from about half past nine on the morning of Sunday the thirteenth of December 2024, and he was not going in, and the reason was a piece of paper in a plastic sleeve in his coat, dated the twenty-fifth of November, that said he was barred from the building as from the first of December as a security matter, and that his appointment ended on the eighth, and that there was no finding and nothing to appeal.

He had a pound. He had had two since the eighth of December, when he had spent one of them to walk the length of the ground floor of his own building, and he was not going to spend this one, and he could not have told anybody at the time why not.

Nobody had told him the Hall was reopening. He had found the date on the eleventh in a printed card that somebody had put through a letter box in Sillick Lane, and a date is a thing a man can act on, and there is no form in the world for a man who is barred from a building turning up outside it on the morning a room he had been inside thirty-nine days opens to the public, and so he had come.

The lobby was open and the doors were propped and the heating was on and there was a card in a plastic sleeve on a stand by the turnstiles, and from the pavement, at about twenty to ten, he could read all of it, because he had read it four times in the *Chronicle* and twice in a *Gazette* and once in a leaflet that somebody had put through a letter box in Sillick Lane, which was a leaflet, and the leaflet was wrong about one thing and right about nine.

> **HALL OF FORMER FAITHS — REOPENS TODAY, 09:50.**
> *The object at plinth 34 is displayed under a permanent classification of the Quiet Accord. It has not been handled, moved, opened or approached. The Rope line is the Museum's provision and the provision is unchanged.*
> *The panel on the case was made by the requester of 20 November 2024 and is reproduced without amendment. The Museum's own card is in place beneath it and has been since 27 November 2024.*

**There was no Museum staff on the door.**

That is the sentence and it is true and nobody arranged it. The Hall was open, and the Hall has a door, and the door on a Sunday morning in a public museum is manned by a member of staff, and on the thirteenth of December 2024 at ten o'clock in the morning the door of the Hall of Former Faiths was not manned by anybody, because the front-desk rota had been cut to a skeleton for the term and the Museum's staffing notice had gone up on the eleventh and had a line in it about the Gallery of Unnamed Hands being closed for a condition survey, and nobody had read the notice except the Board Secretary, and the Board Secretary had read it on the Friday and had gone home.

He could see the Hall from the pavement. Not well — it is up a broad stair and behind a doorway and along a run of terrazzo — but he could see the far end of the plinth row, and he could see the rope, and he could see the case at the east end on plinth 34, and he could see, from about ninety feet away, that there was something on the front of the plinth that was not the plinth card.

There were about forty people in that room at ten o'clock on a Sunday morning in December, and among them, at about half past ten, sitting on a bench against the west wall with a folded newspaper on his knee and a frame against the wall beside him, was Cliff Sowerby, who had come on the tram with a woman of fifty-four and who had paid a pound each at a counter and who was not going to be told what a bell was by anybody.

At four minutes past eleven, in a hall he could see and could not touch, and about ninety feet from a man standing on a pavement in his coat, the bell gave one note.

It was the same note. That is the only thing anybody in that room could say about it afterwards, and about thirty of the about forty people in there were there for a wage and not for a bell, and eleven of them had been in that room on the twenty-seventh and had heard it, and one of those eleven is a woman of eighty-one who had stood up at the side of a table in a library front room on the thirtieth of October and told a room that this had been about the letter. Not one of the forty wrote anything down and none of them knew who to write it to, and the Museum's record of the thirteenth of December 2024 is a sheet of notepaper on a stand by the turnstiles and about two hundred people through a door, and the only document anywhere about that note is a line in a notebook of a man who could not see the case.

The crack in its shoulder is still open under the staple. It was open at four minutes past eleven on the thirteenth of December and it is open now.

At ten minutes to ten, four floors above him, on a level he had not been allowed on since the nineteenth of October, a door that had been shut and latched for eighteen years was opened by a director, with a registrar and a metals conservator and one person who is not Museum staff and is not a member of the public.

There was nothing about it on the pavement. He did not know it had happened until the Monday, and what he was told on the Monday is not anybody else's to write down, and it is the first thing anybody in this business has ever done to him that he was not told about in advance, and it was done to him by post, on a sheet of Museum notepaper with no number on it, and it was the only gift in eleven weeks.

Elias Venn did not go in.

He stood on the pavement until about a quarter past eleven with a pound in his pocket and his hands in his coat and about two hundred people going past him into and out of a building he had been paid to stand in at night for three years, and then he got the tram down the hill, because the question had moved to where the people were, and a man who can only guard buildings has been told, in writing, in a plastic sleeve, that he is not a building.

---

There were about four people on the pavement at the bottom of Sillick Lane at about half past eleven on a Sunday morning in December, and it was not raining and it was about four degrees and there was a wind coming up the street from the water, and one of the four was a postman of about fifty with a bundle of letters in a sack on his shoulder and a handheld in his hand, standing at the corner outside number 88, and he had been standing there for about four minutes, and the corner is four hundred yards from the tram stop and the tram takes nineteen minutes from the top of the hill.

"There's no 86," he said, before Elias Venn got within ten feet of him. "I've got 84, I've got 88, and I've got a 90 that hasn't been there since about 2019, and the job sheet's got 86 on it. I know the job sheet's got 86 on it because I've had it eleven times and I've walked this street since the old roadworks and 86 is not a thing on this street."

"There's no 86?"

"There's no 86 and there's never been a 86." He held the handheld out and turned it round, which is not something a person does, and which is exactly what a person does. "Look. 84, 88, 90. And I've got 14 and 6 and 40 and 3 Ferry Bank's not on my round. And the 88 is a problem anyway because the 88's got no *name* on it. Not no name like a wrong one. No name. It's a four-character field and the field is empty and it's been empty since about the sixth of December, and before that it had a name on it and I've delivered to it for about nine years."

Elias Venn looked at the screen. He did not touch it, because it was a person's hand and a handheld, and because a man who has been the reason a device came out of a coat once in six weeks is not going to be the reason a screen gets looked at twice, and the printed line on the day book four miles away says **NO PHOTOGRAPHY OF COLLECTIONS MATERIAL, LABELS, OR RECORDS** and it had been eight weeks old and he had read it that morning.

It said a number and a field and the field was empty and there was a date on it when the field went, and the date was the sixth of December, and the seventh of December, and the eighth.

"What's your round called."

"Fisher Lane and Sillick. It's got about two hundred and ten addresses and I've had it since the summer and I'm the only one on it because the other one's on long term sick and that's not my business." He put the handheld down and shifted the sack. "It's not the first one either. Last week I had a road up at Broad Street with about nine addresses on it that came off in three days and nobody can tell me why, and the depot says the sheet's right, and the sheet is *not* right, mate, I know what I deliver to."

Elias Venn took out a pocket notebook, and wrote down four things in about nine seconds, in his own hand, in the ink and the pressure and the lean he could not improve on in his life, and that is the last entry in the notebook for six weeks:

> **Sun 13 Dec, 11:40. Sillick Lane, bottom of. Fisher Lane & Sillick round, about 210 addresses, one man, other postman long-term sick.**
> **Number 88: name field empty since 6 Dec. Number 86 on the job sheet, and there is no 86 and there never has been.**
> **Broad Street, nine addresses off in three days, week before last, same sheet, depot says the sheet is right.**
> **This is not the mill and it is not 1974 and it is not a bell. It is a name field and it is empty and it is the same size as a person.**

"Have you told anybody?" he said.

"I've told the postmaster and the postmaster has told the depot and the depot says the sheet's right." The postman shifted the sack again. "The sheet's never not been right in my life. That's the thing. A name field doesn't go empty by accident. Somebody's typing."

Then he went up the street, and Elias Venn stood at the corner outside number 88 in Low Ward for about a minute and a half, and then he went up the street after him, because there are six addresses on this row with a name field on them and a woman of eighty-one lives at number six and a man of seventy-four lives at number fourteen and a woman of fifty-four has a hundred and forty names in a drawer in number fourteen, and the last of those three had told a systems technician on a Sunday afternoon in a gallery that the ones she cannot get are the people who never had a letter, and that there is no list of those, and that there never will be, because you cannot take a list of a thing nobody kept.

He had been not asking that woman a question for eleven weeks. He had got her sentence off a man of twenty-five and given it away and then stood in a street and worked out why, and he had written it down and crossed out most of it.

He went up the street and knocked on a door, at number 88, on a Sunday morning in December, at about ten to twelve, and it was a door he had never knocked on, and nobody in it had ever been asked for anything by anybody, and there was no form in the world that could help him, and there was no anchor card and there was no card and there were no nine minutes.

He knocked anyway.
