# Chapter 0025 — The Number Is a Wage

The material-change inspection was at nine o'clock on Monday morning in a room that had been shut to the public since Saturday, and the member of the public present was Elias Venn, and he got in by signing a visitors' book.

He had done it once before, on the night of the twelfth of October, in a gallery four miles from the public entrance, with a torch, and had been a member of the public in the only sense available to him at that hour. This was different. This was at nine in the morning, on a clipboard, with a printed line at the top of a page, and the line said:

> **MEMBER OF THE PUBLIC ATTENDING — QUALIFICATION (Registry procedure 6.2).** *Name. Address. Nature of interest. Reference of any request made by you under section 9.*

He wrote in the box marked *name* his name, and in the box marked *address* fourteen Mill Lane, Low Ward, which is not where he lived and is the only address he has ever been able to give this building, and in the box marked *nature of interest* he wrote: **Requester. Form lodged at front desk 19 Oct.**

And in the box marked *reference of any request* he wrote **QA/CR/1187**, which was the number on the form he had put into a plastic tray on a Monday morning a week ago, and he put a date against it, because a form with no date on it is a rumour and a form with a date on it is a record, and he had learned that on a Saturday night in October from a woman with a stopwatch.

He stood in the visitors' book for a moment with the pen on the line.

Fifteen days. He had made himself a member of the public of his own building by writing down the thing he had done in a flat block hand on a form, and the building had to let him in, and the reason the building had to let him in was that a statute said so, and the statute had been on the notice board at the front desk for six years and had been used four times and three of them were requests to have a painting re-hung.

He put his initials against the entry, which nobody had asked him to do, and went up.

---

Aurelia Roth came at nine o'clock with a case and a satchel and a form, and she was not the assessor from the twelfth of October and she was the assessor from the twentieth, and she was about thirty-four and she had a statutory duty and no interest in anything in the building except the object and the room.

There were three other people in the room, and Elias Venn counted them, which he has not been able to leave off since he was sixteen.

The first was Spence, the metals conservator, who had re-anchored the bell on the twentieth and had come in at a quarter to eight to put it back on a plinth that had been reset the previous afternoon, and who was in the room because a metal object going back into a public case at eight in the morning on a Monday needs a conservator and not a technician.

The second was a man in his fifties in a conservation department cardigan, with a condition form on a clipboard, and Elias Venn learned within two minutes that he was a conservation officer and that he had been one for eleven years and that he was, in the most complete and least dangerous sense Elias had yet encountered in this building, a man who loved paperwork.

The third was a woman of about thirty-four in a plain coat with a folder, who was introduced as a *member of the public* with no surname read out, and who put her hand on the visitors' book and signed it and wrote **QA/CR/1420** in the reference box, and who did not look at Elias Venn when he read the number, and whom Elias Venn did not know.

Then the assessor read the requests, because procedure 6.2 required it, and there were two of them, and she read them out loud.

"QA/CR/1187, lodged at the front desk on the nineteenth of October by E. Venn, member of the public. Reason, six words: **the object is out of condition.**" She looked up. "That is a complete and sufficient reason and it is the only request in six years that has been complete and sufficient. It is also the only one that has been true when it was written, which is not the same thing, and I want that on the record because it is going to be a problem in about six weeks."

"And the second one?" said the conservation officer.

"QA/CR/1420, lodged at the front desk on the twenty-third of October by W. Loach, member of the public. Reason: **does anything here reach a person.**"

The room did not do anything. Elias Venn, who had spent fifteen days learning to read a room, could not see a single thing happen in that room at that sentence, and he understood about four seconds later that this was because there was nothing in the sentence to react to — it was six words with a question mark and a member of the public's full name on it, and the Registry had used it because that is what a request is.

"Sit down, both of you," said Aurelia Roth. "You may ask questions. You may not touch anything, you may not address staff, and if I say *recorded* then everything said from that point is written down verbatim and you may not speak out of turn. Do either of you want to withdraw?"

Nobody withdrew.

---

It took an hour and forty minutes and it was funny, and it was dreadful, and Elias Venn wrote down all of it afterwards in a notebook in the block hand and could not make any of it into a sentence about a god.

The object was on plinth thirty-four under a new seal, and the new seal's number was on the condition form, and the anchor plate QA/A/2 was under the plinth where it had been since the twelfth of October, and the crutch under the east side of the case was in, and the lead was off the surround to the building earth, and the small green telltale at the base was up.

The object was a hand bell, unpitched at rest, with a bright machine staple through a fracture in the shoulder, and the fracture was still visible, and the telltale was on, and the assessor wrote all of that in a hand that had clearly written it eleven times before.

And then they went down the corridor to the Gallery of Unnamed Hands, which had been shut since the spring, and which was a shuttered room off the far end of the Hall with nine plinths down the centre and six flat files down the other side, and which had on the wall an inventory sheet dated 1988 that says the room holds nine objects.

It holds eight.

Plinth nine is a plinth with a felt top and no case on it, and a card on it that says **SEE NOTE ON DRAWER 6B**, and drawer six B in the second unit is open, and has been open since the case came off it, and inside it, flat, under a weight, is the Gallery's own acquisition correspondence for 1974, and the carbon receipt is on top of it, and the line that says **9. Lamp, fitter's, electric, navy enamel** is in Doreen Sarn's ballpoint in the top right corner of the receipt, in a hand nobody has ever asked about.

The conservation officer stood in front of plinth nine with his condition form and got out a pen.

"Condition line," he said. "Standard vocabulary." He wrote, in a box headed *condition at inspection*, in neat capitals: **UNPITCHED AT REST.**

And then he stopped.

He looked at the form for about two seconds. Then he put the pen back in his pocket, took a different pen out, and struck the words through with a single diagonal, and initialled the strike, and dated it, and wrote underneath, in the same capitals, **N/A — ELECTRICAL, NOT A SOUNDED OBJECT**, and signed it.

"Sorry," he said. "That's a habit and it's a bad one and I have never been able to stop it. Eleven years. Every condition form in this building has that line on it and the line is for bells, and I write it and I strike it, and there are four hundred condition files upstairs with a struck-out *unpitched at rest* in them, and if anybody ever counts the crossings-out, which nobody ever does, they would find that the word was in the template before a single one of the objects was in the building."

Aurelia Roth did not look up from her own form.

"Mr Wilkes," she said. "Is that a professional opinion or a habit?"

"It's a habit."

"Then record it as a habit, and do not record it as a finding, and put in the marginal note that the struck entry is standard vocabulary and carries no evidential weight." She wrote for about fifteen seconds. "I do that eleven times a year and it is the only thing I have ever written in a form in nineteen years that anybody has ever read back to me."

Elias Venn stood eight feet from a felt-topped plinth with a card on it and something in his chest that he had not had since a Sunday morning in a laundrette.

It is not a finding. The word on four hundred forms in a building is a word that was there before the thing was, and the fact that it has been struck out four hundred times does not put it right, it puts it *in the record four hundred times*, and a room full of struck-out words is a room in which the wrong word has been written down and cancelled so many times that it has become the ordinary furniture of the institution.

He had spent ten days writing a number in a notebook because the word *wage* was on four hundred forms and not on one of them.

---

The inspection concluded at twenty past eleven with a finding of *no material change other than that recorded*, and a certificate of condition in one sentence, and the object's classification untouched, and the public view resuming the following morning at ten o'clock.

And then Aurelia Roth asked the Gallery of Unnamed Hands whether the item accessioned 1961/LW/51 was in the building, and the answer came back down the corridor from a man in overalls by telephone, and the answer was that it was at Quay 3, Broomhead Reach, bay as allocated, in bond, and that a return had been requested and was not scheduled for that week.

The conservation officer wrote it in the *location* box, and the assessor read it twice, and Elias Venn watched her read it twice and understood that she was doing the same thing he would have done, which was to look at a location and try to work out what the sentence was actually about, and that she was better at it, and that she would come to a different answer because she had the object register and he had the notebook, and neither of them had a cage in a corridor.

"You have had an item out of a plinth in a public gallery," she said, "on a Saturday morning, in bond, off the building, without a docket for its return."

"Yes."

"There is a memorandum from you dated the twenty-fourth at ten past ten in the morning asking for it to be brought back, and it is on the movement register, and it took an hour and five minutes to stop a lamp from being put away in a store in Northglass." She wrote it down. "I am going to record, as a finding, that an object was removed from its plinth in error, that no seal was broken, that the removal was reported by the Museum within ninety minutes, and that the object is returning. I am not going to record that it should not have gone, because in my judgement it could not have stayed, and I have read the works order, and a plinth cannot be coated with a Schedule 12 object standing on it."

Elias Venn did not say anything, and it was the first time in a month that he had wanted to say something and had a sentence ready and had let it pass, and what he let pass was this: that the sentence the Museum had signed at four o'clock on Saturday afternoon, in a registrar's own hand, with a note about the whole of the risk on it, was true, and that the woman with the satchel had read it, and that it was on the file, and that no one in this room would ever read it except a person with a duty.

The Gallery was locked behind them at ten to twelve. There were eight objects in it and nine plinths and forty-one drawers and a sheet on a wall saying nine, and it had been eight since a Thursday in October when somebody with a good coat read a number off a tag and wrote it in a book and did not go near it.

---

At twenty past eight on Monday evening Elias Venn walked the length of the west range service corridor, past the lavatory door and the handrail that was five days old, and stopped in front of cage 2.

Cage 2 had a red anodised disc on it with W. Ward's initials in ballpoint and a padlock with a Registry tag on it, and behind the mesh, on a pallet, a wooden box with **1961/LW/44** on the end of it and **PF 118** underneath in the same ballpoint, and the box had not been opened and had not been moved, and it was going nowhere at all, because a docket for it could not be countersigned by a person who is not an officer or employee of this Museum, and there is not one, and the Director is not going to buy one.

He did not have a question.

That was the thing. He had gone down that corridor on the Saturday of the second week of October with a card and a pencil and a stop word and a bound, and on Saturday night with Ivo in a doorway and a table and a named cost, and on Monday afternoon of the twenty-sixth of October he stood in a corridor with his hand on the mesh and there was nothing on the card because there was no card, and nobody was going to say a word, and he was not going to have a card afterwards.

He put his hand on the box.

The room was not a room. It was a corridor with a lavatory door on a swing and a handrail and a smell of cold stone, and the corridor was the room, and it went in about three seconds and it came out in about ninety.

The pay office, lamplit, from above and behind, because an envelope is a thing that is looked down at.

A table. A hand. A pen. And a date written in the corner of an envelope in a hard hand, in biro, on the top right corner where you write a date on an envelope so that you know which one it is when there are forty of them on a tray, and the date was the twenty-second of October, 1974, and it was in a woman's writing, and it was the only piece of evidence in the entire business that was made deliberately, by a person, for the purpose of being found, and it was in a sealed envelope in a cage in a corridor.

A tray. Not a sack. A tray, and on the side of the tray a card, and on the card in pencil, in a man's hand: **2ND POST — 3 DAYS.** And the thing about a second post in a works office is that the first post is a sack and the second post is a tray, and the second post went out three days in the week, and a tray means three days, and a sack means a week, and a clerk who wants a thing to be known to have gone on the day it says puts it in a tray and writes the day on the corner of it.

Which meant it was not lying in a van for fifty years. It had been *delivered*, within three days, to somebody, in the second week of the last trading week of a mill that was going to close on the Friday.

And then the tin, on the shelf above the counter, with the pay-office stamp on the front of it, and the tin was a gate-book box, and the gate book was in it, and the gate book had leaves one to ninety-six present, and the hand in the echo put two fingers on the edge of the gate book and turned a corner, and in the corner of the leaf in a hard hand in biro was **twenty-four**, and that was all it was, and the hand went away.

And a card. Not a leaf. A card, a printed leaving card, and a hand filling it in, and on it a surname and a forename initial and a grade and a figure and a word.

**MALLOY, J. — Grade 2 — 2,041 hrs — NIL.**

Two thousand and forty-one hours a man's own hours on his own card and nothing at the end of it, and the word NIL, and a mill's leaving card and a museum's leaving card are the same piece of card with the same word on it, and a Museum's NIL means *no balance* and a mill's NIL meant *no entitlement*, and the two things are a hundred and forty years apart in the life of an institution and identical on the face of a form, and neither of them is ninety-four, and neither of them is a wage, and it is a man's own hours, which is the only figure a person can prove about themselves without a book.

Ninety seconds. He came out of the corridor at 20:22 and wrote the time in the notebook on the loading-bay desk, and the date, and then he did not write anything else for eighteen minutes, because he had been in a corridor for ninety seconds and the room he had to go to was eleven yards away and thirteen people were in it.

---

The loading-bay office held thirteen people on the evening of the twenty-sixth of October and it held them because Elias Venn had spent three weeks on the telephone, and eleven of the thirteen were the meeting, and it held them with a method.

There is no public room in this city. Hattie Bramm had said it to him in a kitchen in Sillick Lane in the third week of September, and she had said it the way you say a thing that has been true for a long time, and she had said that the last public room in Morrowgate where anybody said a wage out loud was a gate, and the gate shut, and everything since had been a queue.

So he had built one. Not a god and not a shrine. A room with a rule.

**One question.** Anyone in the room could ask one question, once, and it had to be a question, and it could not contain an accusation.

**Everything said is recorded, with the speaker's name on it.** A municipal conciliation field officer named Marsh had come at half past eight on the Monday because Elias Venn had telephoned her office on the Friday, and she had said on the telephone that the meeting was not a meeting and could not be minuted and would be an unlawful assembly if more than a dozen people turned up and spoke, and he had said that was fine, and she had said *then I'll come and take minutes anyway, and I shall not call it minutes*, and that was how it happened.

**Nobody is to be asked a question they have not volunteered for.** A sentence he had put in because of a woman of fifty-four in a corridor on the fourteenth of October.

**The figures go to the speaker.** Every number that was said in the room had the name of the person who said it attached to it, and no figure was recorded unattributed, and if a figure was disputed it was recorded as disputed and both versions went in with both names.

There were thirteen people in the office and eleven of them were the meeting. Nine of the eleven he could put on the gate of the Sillick Mill on the morning of the thirtieth of October in 1974 by name, out loud, and two of them he could not, and he has never been able to say how that came about, and it took a retired pay clerk of seventy-eight saying *I know of a hundred and forty* and a woman of fifty-four with a nine-year chase and a folder. **The two who were in the room and were not on the floor of anything were the registrar, in her own person, and the officer, with her form, and neither of them was ever on a mill gate, and he had not thought about it until the officer sat down and by then it was too late to be the man who had thought about it.**

**The two of the eleven who were not from a mill were a documentary filmmaker with a camera and a radio producer with a recorder, and neither of them had been invited by Elias Venn: both of them had rung him,** and he had said yes to both of them on the telephone on Friday afternoon with the whole of the risk in his mouth and had not told anybody in the building that he was going to say a number in a room with a tape recorder in it.

Mara Iles was in her own person. Not the Director's, not the Board's, not conservation. She was in the corner with a chair against the wall and a folder on her knees and she had not brought the shared log, and Elias Venn had not asked her to, and she said, in front of everybody, at ten past nine, before the officer had opened her form: "I have not brought the protocol. He is not doing a protocol tonight and I am not being his witness, and if anybody in this room wants what is said recorded as evidence, then this meeting is the wrong meeting for it and I will say so on the tape."

(Field Officer Marsh: *I shall make a note that the registrar has stated a limitation in advance, and I shall make it before the meeting begins and not afterwards, because afterwards it would be a thing I had decided.*)

Mara Iles: *Make a note of it in those words.*

(Field Officer Marsh: *I shall.*)

Elias Venn stood up at about twenty past nine in the evening of Monday the twenty-sixth of October, in a room off a loading bay, in a building he was permitted to stand in, in front of eleven people, four of whom had driven there and one of whom was on a night shift, and there was a recorder on the table with a red light on it and a camera on a tripod with the lens cap off.

He told them what it was and what it was not and what he was going to do and what it would cost him, because he had promised himself in a corridor that if he ever did this he would do that part first, and because a person who has been the only person who knows a thing cannot start by producing the thing.

It took him four minutes. He did not use the word *god* once. He said: *there is a small bronze bell on plinth thirty-four and it was rung at a mill gate and the last thing it was used for was a count of the people who came in to work on the last morning the mill ever ran, and there is a woman in a flat in Ferry Bank who was the pay clerk and she has known for thirty-one years that the figure on the pay run did not match the figure at the gate, and I have read it out of the object, and I am the only person in Morrowgate who has read it out of the object, and that ends tonight, and it ends because it is in a room and not because it is in a man.*

Then he said the sentence.

"**Ninety-four people were on the gate at Sillick Mill on the Friday morning of the thirtieth of October in 1974.** And the pay run that went to the bank listed fewer than were on the gate."

Then he said the two words that had never left his mouth.

"The pay run said **ninety-two**. And **two people worked and were never entered in any book.**"

The room did not make a sound.

Elias Venn counted it. He could not have told anybody why. Thirteen adults, eleven of them the meeting. He had said it at twenty past nine at night on the twenty-sixth of October to eleven people and a tape and a camera, and not one of them said anything at all for about four seconds, and then a man of about seventy-six at the back said, "Say the last one again," and Elias Venn said it again, and the woman beside him put her hand on his arm and he did not know her.

(Field Officer Marsh wrote: *Reader states: 94 on gate; 92 on pay run; two persons worked and not entered in any book. Attributed to reader. Reader asked how the figures were obtained and declined to answer. Recorded as declined.*)

And then Hattie Bramm stood up.

She had been sitting with her hands in her lap for an hour and she had not spoken once, and she had refused, on the telephone on the Friday, to be the person who said any of it, and she had said that if she said it she would be a person with a microphone in front of her, and she had spent nine years making sure she was not a person with a microphone in front of her.

"I was the gate clerk," said Hattie Bramm. "On the last shift. That's all. I want that said once and I want it said by me and I want it in your book with my name on it, because if nobody says it then you are all talking about a thing that happened in a building and not about a day that forty people stood in the rain for."

She put her hand on the table.

"We were on the gate. We were the last ones through, and it did not finish until about twenty past six, and then the wages came round on the Friday and the room went quiet. That's the whole of what I have. That's what I have had for thirty-one years and I have never once said it in a room, and I have said it in a queue on a Friday, which is a room with the wrong people in it, because the queue is the only room anybody ever gives us."

Nobody wrote anything down for about two seconds and then Field Officer Marsh wrote it all down, slowly, with both hands.

The question came at twenty past ten and it came from a chair by the door and it was asked by a woman of about thirty-four in a plain coat who had put a form into a plastic tray at a front desk on the Friday morning and had written six words on it.

"Can you ask it," said Wynne Loach. "Is there a thing there. Is that what you do."

The red light was on the recorder and the camera had a lens cap off and there were thirteen adults in a room off a loading bay in a building on Civic Hill and a woman of thirty-four had just asked a suspended night guard whether he could speak to a god.

Elias Venn looked at her and understood that the room had gone completely quiet in a way it had not been quiet all night, and that the quiet was not the room's; it was hers; and that he had spent three weeks building a procedure for a man with a mop and he had never once thought about a woman of thirty-four who had a bus and a father who worked nights, and that she was the first person who had asked him a question about what he *was* in thirty-one days, and that she had asked it in a room, and that the answer was yes.

"Yes," he said. "I can ask it. Not a question — a thing I do, with one question, and it answers in its own words and it can be wrong, and it cannot be ordered and it has never once been asked to do anything. I did it in October on the fitter's lamp and it showed me a kitchen and no face. I did it on Saturday night in the loading bay in front of eleven people and it showed me a person at a counter with a hand on it, and I do not know who that person is, and I have not asked anybody."

"Is there a thing there?"

"Yes. There is a thing there. It is the reason a bell in a case in a museum is not an unremarkable industrial exhibit, and it is not a demon and it is not a saint and it is not a shrine waiting to be rebuilt, and it is not interested in being worshipped, and I am not able to tell you what it wants in a sentence, and I have been wrong about it four times in a month." He put his hand flat on the table. "What I can tell you is what it did. It counted, for forty years, out loud, at a gate, at a shift, where the people it was counting could hear it, and a count spoken where the counted can hear it does not take being falsified. That is the whole of the mechanism. Everything else in this building for a hundred and forty years has been an argument about what to call it."

(Field Officer Marsh wrote for about forty seconds and then stopped and read back what she had written, and Elias Venn could not see the page.)

*Form QA/CR/1420 uses the word *reader* throughout, meaning a member of the public who has lodged a form, and the word is on the form and is not the Registry's opinion of the person using it. Recorded, on the question of a member of the public, QA/CR/1420: the reader confirmed that he performs a deliberate contact with an object and that the object answers. Reader stated: there is a thing there; it is not a demon, not a saint, and does not seek worship; its demonstrated function is a count spoken aloud where the counted can hear it; reader states he has been wrong about it four times in a month. "Thank you," said Field Officer Marsh, and Elias Venn understood, about four seconds later, that she was not being polite, and that *thank you* was what you said to a person who had just handed you a document nobody had asked for, and that a field officer of a municipal conciliation service had been sitting in a room for two hours and had been handed, on a Friday, over the telephone, a claim in a form she had never been designed for, and that she was going to take it home and put it in a file and the file would be a record and the record would be a number.

They had it until a quarter to eleven and then a man of about seventy-eight put his hand up and asked a question about the mill.

And then somebody asked Hattie Bramm how many people it was, and it was the obvious question, and it was the question Elias Venn had been dreading since the third week of September, and she looked at the room for about four seconds.

"No," she said.

"Ma'am—"

"No, and I'll say why, and then nobody in this room asks me again." She did not raise her voice and she never did. "You are all about to do a sum. You have got a number on a tape, and you have got a folder, and you have got a woman in Ferry Bank with a cash book, and in about four minutes one of you is going to add them up and get a figure and say it out loud, and the figure will be on a recording, and in about three months it will be in a newspaper, and the number will become the whole of the argument."

"There is a number—"

"There is a number on a plinth in a man in a corridor," said Hattie Bramm, "and it is not mine, and I did not give it to him, and I have watched it go round this city for three weeks on a pair of lips and it has cost a man his job already. And you are all about to make a *total* out of it, and a total is a number that can be checked, and a number that can be checked is a number that can be stopped." She put her hands flat on the folder. "I will not do a sum in a room. I have never done a sum in a room in nine years and I am not starting on a Monday night in front of a camera. When I have a hundred and forty names on a piece of paper with streets on them, in my own hand, then somebody can have a number, and until then it is a queue and a folder and a woman on a Friday, and that is the only form it has ever been any use in."

(Field Officer Marsh wrote: *Member of the public declined to state a total. Reason given. Recorded as declined. No total was stated in the room by any person.*)

And Elias Venn stood at the side of a table in a room off a loading bay and understood that the most competent person in it had just refused, out loud, in front of two recorders, to be the most useful person in it, and that she had refused for the reason he had refused the number in a street in October, and that he had had four seconds about it and she had had about four years.

The hearing was set up before they left, and it was set up by Hattie Bramm, and Elias Venn did not set it up and could not have set it up and would not have got it right.

Marsh had a form and a form is a machine for making a thing repeatable, and the thing being made repeatable was the worst thing in Elias Venn's professional life, which was a sentence said once, out loud, in a room, by a man who had kept it.

> **MORROWGATE — MUNICIPAL CONCILIATION: FIELD RECORD F/28**
> **Hearing: Sillick Mill closing shift, 30 October 1974. Alleged short payment on the final pay run.**
> **Venue:** to be confirmed, a room with step-free access, microphones, and a public notice. **Date and time:** Thursday 26 November, 18:00.
> **One question per speaker. No interruption. Every figure recorded with the name of the person who said it. No figure to be recorded without a name. Every disputed figure to be recorded as disputed with both versions and both names.**
> **Undertaking by the convenor:** everything said at the hearing will be read out in full to those present, and will be retained in full, and a copy will be available to any person on request. The convenor undertakes that no part of it will be withheld, summarised, or corrected after the event.
> **Fee:** £140, from the convening budget, payable in advance, to the persons named on the list of attendance, whether or not they attend, and whether or not they say anything.
> **Witnesses:** the convenor is not a witness and will give no evidence. The reader is not a witness and will give no evidence. **No person will be asked a question they have not volunteered for.**

Marsh read it twice, and said, "That fee clause is the only reason any of this happens."

"What's the fee for?"

"It's for the people who come and then can't speak when they're asked to, and go home, and are not thanked." She squared the page. "Fourteen of your hundred and forty are over seventy. It is a hundred and forty pounds and it is not enough and it is the only line on the page that is doing any work."

---

They cleared the office at eleven, and Hattie Bramm would not give evidence, and said so three times, and said the reason three times, and the reason was that a woman who is a witness is a woman who has become a document, and that she had watched a man of seventy-four become a number in a consignment note in October, and that she was not going to be one.

"She stays a claim," Elias Venn wrote in the notebook at the desk after they had gone, in the flat block hand, with the time against it, at 23:14 on Monday the twenty-sixth of October, which is the fifteenth day.

> *Said it out loud. In a room. 20:22, 26 Oct. 94 on the gate. The pay run said 92. Two worked and were never entered in any book. Ninety-two and two are the only figures I have had since 10 Oct and they are now on a tape in three rooms.*
> *I said the two and I cannot say who they are. The field officer asked for a name for the two. I said I do not have one. She wrote: two persons, unnamed, not in any book, and read it back, and I initialled it, and it is the only piece of paper in this business with a hole in it that is the right shape.*
> *There are two people on the floor of that room tonight and I cannot put either of them on that gate, and Hattie Bramm did not say they were the two, and I did not ask her, and that is the second time in a month I have not asked somebody and it is the first of them that has cost me nothing at all.*
> *Hattie would not do a sum. Nobody did a sum. There is no total in the world tonight.*
> *The bell is on plinth 34 under a seal and nobody has asked it anything, and there is a case cover standing against the wall of that office that was made for a table and has never been used on a case, and it will be used on a table on a Saturday, and I have written that down because I have got it wrong twice already this month and the third time it will be somebody else's problem.*
> *No total. No shortage. No god. Not a shrine, not a devil, not a home. A wage.*

He put the notebook in the inside pocket of his coat, where it had been every night since the tenth of October, and which was the one thing in this building that no standing instruction had yet found a clause about, and then he went and did the dead letters, because it was Monday and there were two hundred and fifty-three of them and a clerk's is a slow job, and it is the last thing he did that week that nobody could hold against him afterwards, and he noticed that, and it was the fourth of October's habits coming back wearing a different coat.
