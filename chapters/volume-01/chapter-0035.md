# Chapter 0035 — Two Lists of the Same Van

Quill & Frayle had been at 14 Lincoln's Lane since 1871 and had not moved, and the reason it had not moved is on the top of the lease and is not a matter of sentiment: the freehold is a building with a strong room under the pavement and a deed of covenant going back to 1889 that says, in the fourth clause, that no paper, print, or document of any kind may be stored at any height above four feet from the floor of the strong room, and a firm that has kept a deed like that for a hundred and fifty-three years does not move.

The door was open at ten past nine on Saturday the fourteenth of November because it was a Saturday, and the reason a solicitors' firm in Irving Quay is open on a Saturday is that Saturday is when the property men come in, and where property men come in, everybody opens.

The street was about a hundred yards long and had a chandler's on it and an auction room and a place that sold fencing, and it was cold in a way that gets into a man's coat and stays there, and there were four skips on it at the far end, all of them empty, all of them belonging to somebody else.

Elias Venn had never been inside the building. He had a firm a few hundred yards off the same quay — Quarry Bank, at number four Irving Quay, two partners, one of them a former hospital-laundry union shop steward — and he had been in that building nine times, and he had a receipt from it dated the twenty-first of October with a blank form in its safe, and he had never walked this far.

Mara Iles had the seller's carbon in a grey archival wallet and she had taken it out of drawer 6B herself, at twenty past eight that morning, with a key, in a public gallery, with nine people in the room, and she had put a slip of Museum paper in with it that said **1961/LW/44 — item 3 of 11, drawer 6B, lifted 14 Nov 2024, 08:20, M. ILES**, and the slip and the sheet went back into the drawer together at four o'clock that afternoon before she left the building, which is where a registrar keeps a document she has taken out of a drawer, and which is the only place in this chapter where the Museum's copy of the 1974 receipt exists.

He had asked her whether she was going to do that. She had said: "I am going to open a drawer in a room I have the keys to, and take a sheet of paper out of it, and put it in a wallet, and a registrar may do that at any time of day or night without telling anybody, and the reason I am telling you is that I am taking it out of a drawer that has been open in a public room since the twelfth of October and somebody is eventually going to ask me why it was open, and I would rather the answer was in my own hand and dated."

And then, at the door of the Gallery, before they went down:

"There is a card on plinth nine that a systems technician wrote on Tuesday which says the lamp is in stores, and the plinth is empty, and a girl of about eleven has asked me twice this morning whether the Museum has taken it away, and I have said yes, and it is in a cupboard, and the cupboard is the correct answer, and I would like it recorded that the correct answer has cost this gallery the only object in it that a member of the public had ever asked about by name."

---

The room they were put in was at the back and it was about eleven feet by nine and it had a table in it, and the table had a document camera on an arm above it and a foot pedal, and a lamp, and two jugs of water, and a wall clock that was four minutes fast.

The man who came in was called Emyr Rhys and he was fifty-eight and he had been at that firm for thirty-one years and he was, by his own account, the person who deals with what comes in.

"That's what comes in," he said. "Not the clients. The post, the deeds, the boxes, the old files. Thirty-one years of what comes in. If it's a document in a wall, I know which wall." He had a cardigan on and a tie and no jacket and he was not in a hurry, and he had made tea. "Now. I want to say three things before we start and I want to say them in this order, because I've had a telephone call from a man in your building on Friday and I've thought about it since."

"Go ahead."

"One. I have known about the Sillick Mill consignment since 1974, because my firm sold it, and I have the job file, and the job file is upstairs, and it is not shut, and nobody has ever asked me for it."

Elias Venn did not move.

"Two. There is a thing in this firm called a clearance schedule. It is a document. It is a list of what came off a site and where it went and who took it and when. There is one for the Sillick Mill site, dated the nineteenth of October, with about forty lines on it, and it is a client's document, and the client is Hallowgate Foyston, and I am not permitted to hand it to you, and I am not going to, and I would like to explain why in about forty seconds and then never have to do it again."

"Then do."

"Because it is not mine." He said it without any weight at all. "It is not the firm's, either. It is the property man's, and the property man is a partner, and a partner can give it to a member of the public if he wants to. He does not want to. Why he does not want to is his business and not mine, and I have known him for twenty-two years and I am telling you that he is not being cruel to you, and I am telling you that I have asked him twice in the last three days and been told no twice, and both times he gave me a reason that was a reason." Rhys spread his hands. "And that is the whole of it. I have a clearance schedule I cannot give you and a job file I can, and if you want to spend the morning on the thing I cannot give you, you will waste a Saturday, and I would rather you didn't, because you look like a man who has been up since seven."

"Three," said Rhys. "There is a Mrs Marsden in the transcription office and I have asked her to sit in on this, and she has been doing this for twenty-six years, and when she reads something out loud she reads it as it is and not as it might be, and if I have said anything in this firm in twenty-six years that has improved a document, you should stop me."

---

The door opened and a woman of about sixty came in with a folder and a reading frame on a chain round her neck, and she was Beverley Marsden and she had a pro forma, which was a printed sheet with two columns and eleven ruled lines and a box at the foot.

The right-hand column was empty and had been empty since 1974, or since this morning, depending on how you look at it.

And then Arthur Neve came in at five past ten.

He was seventy-eight years old and he had a frame and a plastic carrier bag and a bad coat, and he had a chest since a pleurisy in 1969, and he had been up since half past six because the first bus is early and the second one is not early enough when you want to be somewhere before somebody else is, which is a thing he had worked out over five weeks and had not told anybody.

Mara Iles had the door. Elias Venn had said, in a corridor at the Museum on Wednesday morning, that he would not take the wallet, and she had said that was correct and had taken it, and she stood on the left of the door with her hands at her sides.

She knew his name. She knew it from a claim file and a hearing list and a police record and five weeks of a man at a table in a plastic wallet.

"Mr Neve," she said. "Elias Venn is here as a member of the public. I am the registrar of the Morrowgate and I do not sign as an officer. There is no Museum officer in this room. Mr Venn has no authority of any kind in this room and I would like that said on the record of this meeting before anybody says anything else."

"That's proper," said Arthur Neve. "That's proper, and it's the first time anybody's said it."

He gave her the carrier bag. He did not give it to Elias.

He took the plastic wallet out of it, and out of the wallet he took a single sheet of thin carbon paper folded in half, and he put it on the table, and he put his hand flat on it for a second, and then took his hand off, and then put the heel of his hand back on the corner of it, where it stayed for the next two hours.

"Before we start," he said. "That is coming off me on Saturday and going back in my bag at the end, and the two of you will have copies of what Mrs Marsden writes and not copies of that, and I want that said now, in front of Mr Venn, because I have been asked twice this month whether I would hand it over and the answer is the same both times and it is not about you."

"That is your document," said Emyr Rhys.

"It is my document until the twenty-sixth of November," said Arthur Neve, "and on the twenty-sixth of November at six o'clock it is the conciliation service's document, in a room, with a field officer in it, and I am seventy-eight years old and my chest is not what it was and I have told nobody that until now except the four people who had to know. I was shown it on a stair in Northglass in October and I read it once in a room in Cattlemarket and I am not doing either of those things again before it is read out, and I would rather be difficult about it on a Saturday in a solicitor's office than difficult about it on the day."

"I said no twice," he said. "On the Wednesday and on the Friday morning. I'll tell you why and then I'll never mention it again. Because the first time I said no I did not know what I had. And the second time I did know, and I had had a bad night, and I am seventy-eight years old and my chest is not what it was, and I worked out that if I said yes on the Friday morning then I would be sitting in a room for four hours before Friday was out, and that I would come out of it on Friday night, and that I have not had a Friday night since the end of September."

"And this is Saturday," said Elias Venn.

"This is Saturday," said Arthur Neve, "and I got here at twenty to ten, and I have not been in a room since the Thursday before last, and I have had a very good week and I am not going to be told why that is."

Nobody made him sit at the head of the table. Mrs Marsden, who was sixty-two and had a reading frame and a pro forma, put the museum's side at the left and his at the right and squared both of them up to the edge of the table, and said:

"Now then. I am going to read both of these out, in full, in order, from one to eleven, and then I am going to read them out again from the other end, and I am going to read them the same way both times, and if you want to stop me, put your hand on the paper."

She read them.

And the thing Elias Venn had not been ready for — the thing he would think about for the rest of that week and for a good deal longer — is that it was *boring*. It is a list of a van. It is a list of eleven objects put into a steel box on the back of a lorry on the thirtieth of October 1974, and it is in two hands, and reading it out loud in a room in Irving Quay on a Saturday in November is exactly as boring as a shopping list, and the two hands are not two people, they are two clerks in the same trade doing the same job on the same afternoon about the same thirty items, and the difference between them is that one of them was on the buyer's side of the table and one of them was standing by the van.

> **Item one.** *Weavers' time book* — both.
> **Item two.** *Dye-house time book* — both.
> **Item three.** Seller's carbon: *gate book (leaves 1–96 present).* Buyer's carbon: *gate book, with entries to 30 Oct.*
> **Item four.** *Posted wage sheets loose, approx. 2 dozen* — both.
> **Item five.** *Envelope of loose papers, sealed, contents not examined* — both.
> **Item six.** *Tea chest* — both.
> **Item seven.** *Monthly wages book* — both.
> **Item eight.** *Withdrawn soiled* — both.
> **Item nine.** Seller's carbon: *the fitter's lamp.* Buyer's carbon: *the fitter's lamp.* And on the seller's, in the right-hand margin, in ballpoint, in a hand that leans: *see below re F/B.*
> **Item ten.** Seller's carbon: *bundle of wage sheets, tied with tape.* Buyer's carbon: *bundle of wage sheets, tied with tape, marked pay office.*
> **Item eleven.** Seller's carbon: *the works-office clock, stopped.* Buyer's carbon: *clock, works office, stopped 05:54.*
> **At the foot of the buyer's carbon, and only the buyer's, and not one of the differences, because there is nothing under the seller's at all:** *received, all, subject to weight.*

She read it the same way the second time.

And then she put her finger on the third line and said: "There. Three."

---

Nobody in the room accused anybody, and it needs to be said plainly, because for four weeks the argument of this entire business has been that nobody in it is a villain, and the first place that gets tested is a table in Irving Quay with a foot pedal over it.

Elias Venn was the first man to say it out loud, and what he said was:

"That's not two people lying."

"No," said Mrs Marsden.

"It's two people writing. On the same afternoon. Looking at the same thirty things in a yard in about four hours in the rain." He put his hands flat on the edge of the table, which is a thing he does. "One of them was standing by the van. One of them was on the other side of it. And they were both writing from what was in their hands, and what was in their hands at item three was a gate book, and a gate book is a bound book with pages in it, and a man in a hurry who has just been handed a gate book by a fitter and has written *with entries to 30 Oct* has written down what he was told, and a woman in an office a week later who has got a list off a fitter and a list off a porter has written down what she was told, and one of those two people was told the truth and the other one was told the truth on a different afternoon, or on a different part of the same afternoon, and neither of them knew there was a book in there with a run of leaves missing out of it."

"And which one is the truth," said Arthur Neve.

"I don't know," said Elias Venn. "That's the answer and it's the only one available. It is a clerical error or it is a decision and there is nothing on either piece of paper that says which, and I'd be prepared to go to a hearing and say exactly that, and a good deal of people in this business have been going to hearings and saying less than that for thirty-one years."

Emyr Rhys had not said anything. He was standing back from the table with his cardigan sleeves up.

"Item three's the important one, though," he said, and he said it carefully. "Not because it's a discrepancy. Because of what it says about a book. *Leaves one to ninety-six present* is a statement about a physical object made by somebody who had the object in front of her. *With entries to thirty October* is a statement about a date made by somebody who had a list. And there's only one gate book in this business as far as I know, and it's in a cage in bay nine in a museum, and it has got ninety-six leaves in it and the last entry on the ninety-sixth is somewhere about the middle of October, and I know that because your carbon says *leaves one to ninety-six present*, and that is a statement about a book somebody was holding, and ours has been in a file on this floor since 1974 and nobody but me has ever opened it."

"Then the last fortnight of trading is not in it," said Mara Iles. "The last entry on the ninety-sixth is about the middle of October and the buyer's carbon says entries to the thirtieth, and that is fifteen days of a works that was still trading."

"Fifteen days out of it at some point in fifty years," said Rhys. "Or it came out of the book in 1974 in a yard in the rain with a fitter who had a hand truck and a van and eleven things to get in. Which is a very different sentence, and both of them are true, and I can't tell you which one, and neither can anybody in this room, and if you write *leaves 97 to 100 removed* in a document you will be asserting a thing you cannot support in a room with a man of seventy-eight in it who was at the gate."

Arthur Neve put his hand up about two inches, which is all a man of his chest can manage at a table.

"I was at the gate," he said.

"You were at the gate," said Rhys.

"Eleven of us on the fitters and eleven on the looms and a gate clerk and a man on the weighbridge and the foreman." He said it in one go, and then stopped, and then said, "I have not said that in a room before and I am not saying it again. That is the whole of it. I am not going into it. You'll have the carbon."

"No," said Elias Venn. "You won't."

---

At item ten, the two extra words.

**Marked pay office.** Two words, on the buyer's side, and it is not a scribble and it is not a correction, and it is in the same typed line in the same hand as everything else on that sheet.

"That's a stamp," said Mara Iles. "That's not a description. That is a rubber stamp on the outside of a bundle, and the man who wrote the buyer's carbon was looking at the bundle, and the man who wrote the seller's carbon a week later was looking at a list."

"Or it went on after," said Mrs Marsden, in the first thing she had said for about twenty minutes. "That's the other thing that could be true and I want it in the room. There's a pay-office stamp in that building, in a tin, and there's a date on the die, and if the die is dated the fifteenth of October then it could not have been on the bundle on the thirtieth, and it went on afterwards, in a store, by a person, and nobody wrote it down then because nobody thought it was worth writing down, and fifty years later two clerks' carbons are the only two people who can say anything about it at all."

"The die is dated," said Emyr Rhys.

Arthur Neve put his head very slightly to one side. "That's a good argument," he said. "That's a much better argument than mine and I haven't had it yet."

"I've had it for thirty-one years," said Rhys, "and I have never had any occasion to use it, and I have never thought of it. That's a horrible thing to say to a man of seventy-eight at ten in the morning on a Saturday."

---

And then she came to item eleven, and Elias Venn had known for seven days what was coming, and had not let himself think about it on the Saturday morning, and had thought about it on the Friday, and on the Thursday, and on the Wednesday night.

> **Item eleven.** Seller's carbon: *the works-office clock, stopped.* Buyer's carbon: *clock, works office, stopped 05:54.*

Mrs Marsden read it. She read it twice. She put her finger on the paper.

"Six minutes to six," she said. "In the morning, in October, on the thirtieth, in the rain, that would be. And that is a figure, and a figure on a carbon is the only kind of figure in this business that anybody can check, and I want to say one thing about it and then I am going to stop talking."

He looked up.

"That is the only number I have ever read out of a document in this room," said Mrs Marsden, "and in this trade that happens about once in twenty years, and it is always the number that matters, and it is nearly always a time, because a man writing a list of a van in a yard in the rain will remember a clock and will not remember a book. If that is the only figure on that sheet then the clock is what that man thought about on the way home."

And Elias Venn sat at a table in a room in Irving Quay with a foot pedal over his head and a number in his own notebook that he has had since the small hours of Sunday the twenty-fifth of October, when he put his hand on the case of a bell in a loading bay and asked it two questions and got a pay office and a man with no face and a third person at a counter with a hand flat on it and a gate-book box with a pay-office stamp on the shelf above it and a clock reading six minutes to six.

The carbon said 05:54.

The bell said six minutes to six.

And the carbon had been written by a hand that did not know it was writing evidence, on the thirtieth of October 1974, in a yard, in the rain, fifty years before a suspended night guard in a loading bay put his hand on an object and got the same number out of it in about four seconds.

He could not say it. He worked that out at about eleven o'clock and he worked it out properly, over about four minutes, with his hands flat on the edge of a table: he could not say it because saying it required saying the other thing, and the other thing was that he had put his hand on a Schedule 12 carried object that was stayed from all public access at a material-change inspection in five days' time and a determination after that, and had done it in a loading bay in the dark in front of eleven people, and had lost four seconds of a name for it, and had been on a tape in five institutions ever since.

So he said the thing that was true instead, which is what he did, and it is the worst thing he has done in five weeks and it is not a lie and it is much worse than a lie.

Mrs Marsden got to the foot of the sheet and looked up and said, in the voice of a woman who has read two hundred documents out loud to people who did not want to hear them:

"Now. Before I photograph this. Is there any other document in existence relating to these eleven items, in anybody's possession, that has not been produced in this room?"

"No," said Elias Venn.

And it was the first half of a true sentence and he left it there, and the room accepted it, and Mrs Marsden wrote *none produced* in a box on her pro forma and initialled it, and then she said, "Including you, Mr Venn," in the tone of a person checking a box off on a list, and he said:

"I have been in a room with an object from that consignment. I have put my hand on it and I have asked it one question, out loud, and it has given me a number, and I am not going to tell you what the number is, and I could not tell you if I wanted to, because I do not know where I got it. That is in a police file in Low Ward with my name on it, in a cautioned statement of the twenty-third of October, in paragraph six, and anybody in this room who wants to read that paragraph can have it in about eleven minutes, and I would like it minuted that I have said it in a room rather than waited to be asked in one."

Nobody said anything.

Arthur Neve looked at him for a while with an expression that had gone further away than the table.

"Young man," he said. "You have just told a solicitor and a witness and a firm's archivist that you have an undocumented source and that it is you. Do you know what I would have said to you on the Wednesday, if you had said it then?"

"Yes."

"What would it have been?"

"That you'd have come straight away and you wouldn't have come because of the carbon." Elias Venn looked at the sheet. "You'd have come because I'd told you there was a number. And that would have been the wrong reason and you'd have been right not to give it to me."

"Yes," said Arthur Neve. "And I would rather have come because of the carbon." He put two fingers on the carbon paper, gently, the way you touch a thing that is older than you. "Because a carbon is a document and a document is a thing you can put in front of a person and say *is this yours, yes or no* and the person can say yes or no. And what you've just described is not a document. What you've described is being in a room with a thing that talks. And I've not got a box for that, and I have got a plastic wallet, and the wallet's no use at all to a man of my age with a bad chest when it's raining."

"That's fair," said Elias Venn.

"It's not fair. It's Saturday, and you're being decent about it, and I want to go home."

---

They photographed it at twenty past eleven and it took about an hour, because Mrs Marsden photographed the seller's carbon and then the buyer's and then both together with a scale rule and a colour card and then each page of the pro forma, and then she made a certified copy of the comparison for each party and signed it and dated it and wrote on the foot of it, in full:

> **Three differences in wording, and one figure. I do not certify that either document is correct. I certify that this is a true comparison of the two documents as they stand, and that both documents were in the room at the time of the comparison.**

And the copy went into Mara Iles's wallet, and a second copy went into the plastic carrier bag, and that is the first time in five weeks that anything has been made that exists in two places, and neither of the two places is this building, and what exists in two places is a comparison and not a document.

Then Arthur Neve put the carbon back into the plastic wallet, and the plastic wallet back into the carrier bag, and the carrier bag onto his knees under the table, where it stayed, in his own hands, for the rest of the morning and on the bus and up six steps and home, and it is a document in this city tonight and it is not in any building that anybody in this room has a key to, and nobody in this room copied it.

And then Emyr Rhys said, "There's one more thing, and I've been sitting on it since about a quarter past eleven, and I want to say it now before anybody's had a drink of water, and I want to say it to the room and not to the table."

He went out and came back with a ring binder and put it on the table and opened it, and it was a schedule of destruction, and it was a printed form with a week in the left-hand column.

"This is our own," he said. "Not a client's. Ours. It is the schedule we destroy incoming post against, and it is a public document in the sense that it is a thing that exists and a thing anybody can ask to see, and I am allowed to give it to you because it is not Hallowgate's, it is ours, and it is about us."

"Where do you keep it?"

"That's the funny thing. Upstairs. In the strong room, under the pavement, on a shelf at four feet, because of the covenant. Everything in this building that has ever come into this firm and gone out again as rubbish is on this, and it is 1971 to now, and it is about four hundred pages, and it is in date order and it has a reference against every line."

Elias Venn stood up off his chair.

"Mr Rhys."

"Sit down."

"October 1974."

"Third week." He turned about nine pages and put his finger on a line and then took his finger off it and handed the book to Elias Venn, which is a thing he should not have done and which he did.

> **INCOMING — w/c 21 X 74 — 1 env. personal delivery, office of origin, per second post — 1 — DESTROYED 1979, per this schedule, batch 34/74.**

The line had four boxes. It had a date. It had a quantity. It had a destination: **1**. And a disposal reference, and a year, and a batch.

It did not have an address on it. It did not have a sender's name. It did not have a subject, and it did not have a contents line, and it was not going to, because it is a destruction schedule and a destruction schedule says how many and when, and everything else about an envelope is thrown away with the envelope.

It was a Monday, and the week beginning Monday the twenty-first of October 1974 ran to the Sunday the twenty-seventh, and an envelope put in an out-tray on Tuesday the twenty-second, with a second-post three-day card on the side of it and the mill's own van down to the sorting office on Wednesday afternoons, would have arrived on the Thursday or the Friday, and the firm was still open, and there was a tray, and the tray had a date.

"So it's there," said Elias Venn. "Or it was."

"It was," said Emyr Rhys. "I'm quite sure it was. It is in the right week, it is the right quantity, and in 1974 a firm this size received forty-one items of post a week on the average and I've got the four years either side and this week is high, and there is exactly one line in it that is not a bill and not a bank and not a firm on another matter, and it says *personal*, and a pay clerk's letter about her own wages is the only thing in the world that is going to come into a solicitors' office marked *personal*."

Elias Venn was holding a ring binder in a firm of solicitors in Irving Quay with the strong room under the pavement beneath his feet and a line on a page in front of him, and his hands had gone, so he put the book down on the table, which is a thing a man does with a document he has been given improperly and which the archivist watched him do and which neither of them mentioned.

"It's gone," said Elias Venn.

"It was destroyed in 1979 under a schedule that was approved by a partner and signed and is on that page with a batch number against it, and the batch of ninety-one items was pulped at a works in the east of this city in March 1979, and there is no process by which anybody recovers a destroyed record, and I want that said in this room by the man who is holding the book, because in about eleven years he is going to be asked whether the Museum ever knew, and the answer is going to be no, and this is why."

"I asked for it on the fourteenth of October," said Elias Venn, "and I did not know what it was, and I did not know it existed until Thursday, and the oldest person it belongs to is seventy-eight years old and lives four miles from this room and did not know that her letter had come here until the second of October."

Beverley Marsden took her reading frame off.

"Then we had better write it down properly," she said. "All of it. Not for you, Mr Rhys, because you'll do it perfectly. For whoever reads it after. Somebody in about thirty years is going to look for this and there is going to be nothing here, and there is going to be a destruction schedule with a batch number on it and a line that says *personal, 1, office of origin*, and that is going to be the whole of the evidence, and it is going to be in a firm that cannot be asked about it by the people it belongs to." She was already writing. "It is going to look like nothing happened. That is what has happened. It did happen, and then it was destroyed under a schedule, and the destruction was lawful and correct and the firm is not at fault and nobody's wrongdoing is in this room and nobody's wrongdoing is going to be in this room all morning, and I am going to write on the face of that schedule, in ink, in your own hand if you'll allow it, Mr Rhys, the name of the person whose letter it was, and the date she wrote it, and the fact that she is alive."

Emyr Rhys looked at the page for a while.

"There's a procedure for altering a destruction schedule," he said.

"I know there is."

"It's a schedule. You don't annotate it. You bring a new schedule up and it's a batch, and the old one gets a line against it, and the old one stays on the shelf exactly as it is because the whole point of it is that it is a record of what was destroyed and when and it doesn't get better by being nicer."

"Then write it on a piece of paper and pin it to the shelf," said Mrs Marsden, "and if that is against procedure, then the procedure has a hole in it the shape of a woman of seventy-eight, and you have been here thirty-one years and you knew it had a hole in it, and this is the morning you put your finger in it."

Nobody moved for about four seconds.

"No," said Emyr Rhys. "Not a schedule. A slip, pinned, initialled and dated, in the same binder, on the same page, in ink, saying that the person named has been identified in 2024 and is alive, and that the identification is not evidence of the contents, and that the schedule is unaltered and is not to be altered." He looked up. "That is not a hole. That is a cross-reference. It is a completely ordinary thing and I have signed about two hundred of them and there is a box for it on the transmittal and nobody ever fills it in."

And he filled it in, in ink, and it took about four minutes, and the sentence at the foot of it is this and Elias Venn has it in longhand in a notebook because he was asked to read it out and check it and did:

> **CROSS-REFERENCE, 14 Nov 2024. Item at this line identified 2024 as personal correspondence of D. SARN, written 22 X 1974, in her own hand, delivered to this office within three days.
