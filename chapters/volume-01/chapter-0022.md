# Chapter 0022 — The Container

The order came down onto the systems terminal at four minutes past nine on Thursday morning and it was a purchase order, and Elias Venn read it standing up at the loading-bay desk because a man on a restriction has nothing else to do with a Thursday.

> **PO-4471.** Hallowgate Foyston, 14 Lincoln's Lane, Northglass.
> *Site clearance services, Sillick Mill site, Sillick Lane, Low Ward.*
> Items: (1) one 40ft steel storage container, sealed, delivered; (2) one low-loader, collection 22 Oct a.m.; (3) crane hire, Hallowgate Crane Hire, 0.5 day, **attendance 08:00**.
> Consignee on collection: Quay 3, Broomhead Reach, Northglass.
> Note from scheduing: *quantity reduced to 1. Attendee count required on site — see prior order amendment.*

Elias Venn read the third line four times and then read the note.

A crane hire booked for half a day, with attendance at eight in the morning, to put a container into a van and take it over a wall. It was not a lift. A container is a box on castors; a box on castors goes up a ramp behind a lorry. A crane is not for that.

Somebody had bought half a day of a crane.

And there was a *quantity reduced to 1* on it, which meant there had been four, or nine, or eleven, and somebody had gone back and cut it, and the schedulers had kept the note and the note said *attendee count required on site — see prior order amendment*, and the amendment was not on the terminal, and a man on a restriction in a building with no authority whatever was not going to be able to find out about an amendment in a purchase order.

He telephoned Ivo on the conservation floor at ten past nine.

"It's a lifting operation on a slip," he said. "Same as October the seventeenth. That's why there's a crane. They have read what happened and they have bought half a day of a crane because a man went into a canal."

"Read what happened? Nobody's told that site anything. There's nothing in the post, there's nothing on the notice board, and there's a photograph of a weighbridge in Lantern Row and a photograph of a weighbridge is a picture of a bridge."

"Two men from Redgate and Kell were up there in the second week of October, before there was a photograph of anything."

"That was before the fall." Ivo's voice went flat. "Elias, I have the address of the hire firm because it is on the order and the order is on my terminal, and I am going to ring them, and I am going to ask them one question, which is who is attending, and I am going to tell you the answer and then I am going to go and do a systems day."

"You don't have a systems day, you have a systems day until three and it is half past nine on a Thursday and you have done nothing for two days."

"I've done the board," said Ivo. "I've done the board and I've read two safety files I shouldn't have and I am tired of being a man who has read two safety files. Ring me if you go down there."

---

He did not go down there. He went as far as the far pavement of Sillick Lane, at about eleven, and stood at the top of the slip's retaining wall with a notebook in his coat, because Quill & Frayle's letter of the seventeenth of October had said in one clear sentence that Museum staff are not admitted to the site without the prior written consent of the company, which is refused, and because a man who walks onto a clearing site for the fourth time in a month is a man who is going to be a name in a file with a word in front of it.

Hattie Bramm was already on the pavement.

She had a folder under her arm and a hard hat in her hand, which she was not wearing, and she had been there long enough that there was a wet patch on the wall where she had been leaning.

"Ninth time," she said.

"Ninth time what."

"Ninth time I've stood on this pavement looking at that wall." She put the folder down on the wall. "Thirty-one years, nine times. And the first six times I was asked to leave by somebody in a hi-vis and once by a man in a very good coat who did not raise his voice, and once I was not asked to leave, I was simply walked out by two men who were extremely polite, and the ninth is today and nobody has come out at all, because they have got a crane."

The site was not bare. That was the first thing Elias Venn had to correct in his head, because the last time he had stood at this wall the site had been three lorries and a shed and a ramp going down into six inches of canal, and it was now a compound.

A steel container stood on the hardstanding with its doors open and its floor of chequer plate already wet, eight feet long, and a low-loader was nosed up to the slip ramp with its tailgate down and two men standing on the trailer bed with their arms folded. A lorry crane stood on the yard side of the slip with its outriggers on mats and its boom up over the ramp at about forty degrees, and on the side of the cab there was a small rectangular plate, and on the plate there was a telephone number.

Halloway Crane Hire. Northgate. It was stencilled, not printed, and it had a seven-digit number under it and the words *24 HOUR CALL-OUT* underneath that, and the paint had been done over once and had gone thin at the sevens.

Elias Venn stood on a pavement and looked at a telephone number in a van, and then he went and found a telephone, because there was a call box at the top of Sillick Lane outside a laundrette that had been there since the year he was born.

He got through on the fourth attempt. A woman in an office said the name and said the yard was on site and had been since twenty to eight, and that there was a lorry, a low-loader and two men with the driver, and that the site was a Hallowgate site and not theirs, and that they had been instructed to attend at eight and to leave when the job was done.

"Who booked it?"

"Foyston, Northglass, for the freeholder. There's a job number on the top of the sheet."

"Twenty-two men on that site."

"I've got twenty-two on the sheet." A pause, and then a woman doing a job properly, which is a thing Elias Venn could hear through a telephone in about a second and a half. "But I've got two on the sheet and two of them are the driver and the slinger, so I make it twenty. And I'll tell you what I'll tell everybody, because I say it to all of them: I only ever send what the sheet says and I always say to the site, out loud, what the sheet says, and if they send me one man for a two-man lift then I write it on my own copy and I write the time on it, and that's the only thing I have got and it's not a lot."

"Did they say how many?"

"They said two."

"Right," said Elias Venn. "Thank you."

---

They were on the ramp at eleven o'clock, and they were the most competent collection of men he had ever watched do anything, and that was the frightening part.

There were twenty of them on the site and two in the cab of the crane, and they were not a gang. They were hourly men from an agency with a printed sheet and a clipboard, in hired boots and a brand-new boiler suit each, and their foreman was a man of about fifty called Upton who had a hi-vis jacket with a company on it and a folding rule in his breast pocket, and he was doing a job, and the job was *getting a container filled and shut and lifted over a wall by four o'clock*, and he had it under control.

They did not open anything.

That is the part Elias Venn wrote down, and it is the part that made him put his hand on the wall of the slip and leave it there for about a minute. There were sacks of loose paper. There were tea chests. There were four cardboard boxes with lids taped and a door on its hinges wrapped in sacking. There was a bundle of what looked like drawing boards, and a fire bucket, and a stopwatch on a string, and two wooden tool boxes, and a hat stand.

None of it was in a box that could be opened. None of it had a lid that anyone was going to lift. Everything went in as it came off the floor, and the twenty men did not stop to look at any of it, and the foreman did not ask what any of it was, and in eleven days Elias Venn had spent every hour of every day of his working life trying to get a person to open a box he had not been authorised to open, and here were twenty professionals being paid by the hour to move a hundred and forty people's working lives off a wet concrete into a steel box, and not one of them, in eleven days, would have opened it if you had paid them a pound a week to.

Because it was not their job. Because the sheet said *quantity: as per schedule*, and the schedule was not on site, and a man who opens a thing he has been asked to move is a man who has opened a thing, and a man who has opened a thing has an account of what was in it, and the account is a document, and a document follows a man for the rest of his working life and gets him in front of a tribunal.

---

The slipway was the reason there was a crane at all.

Elias Venn had watched a man of seventy-four go into four feet of canal on that concrete ramp on the seventeenth of October and he had not been able to do one single thing about any part of it, and he had stood on a wet ramp with a blue polypropylene hawser round a stump of handrail post and had understood, for the first time in his life, what a lifting-operation requirement is for.

Now the pump housing was on the yard side of the slip, on a pallet of scaffold boards, with its impeller out and a man on a spanner, and the water was in the slip, and there was a gang going down it.

Hattie Bramm said the number before anybody asked her.

"Fourth mark," she said, from about nine feet away, to nobody in particular, and then to the man nearest her, who was a young man in a brand-new boiler suit who had not seen her.

The ramp went down from the yard to the old loading stage at the water, and on the retaining wall on the east side there were four stencilled marks, twenty inches apart, put there by the works in a period when the slip was used, and the fourth one was the datum, and the top of it was about four inches above the bottom step.

The young man looked at her.

"Which mark's that, love?"

"That one." She came up the wall and put her finger about four inches from the tidemark in the concrete and did not touch it. "That is the fourth mark and that is the step and I have watched this slip from this wall since I was twenty-three and I have never once been wrong about it, and you are going to put that pallet down on the staging boards and the staging boards are on the bearers and the bearers are on the step, and the step is under four inches, and the boards will sit, and if you put it on the board that is loose you will be in the water in front of thirty people."

Nobody on that site had ever seen Hattie Bramm before, including Hattie Bramm, and the reason nobody threw her out is that there is a thing a site does about a stranger who is right: it stops and listens for two seconds and then decides what she is.

"Sorry," said the foreman, from about eight feet away. "Nell, put it on the good board. Not that one. The green one."

"My name's Hattie," said Hattie Bramm.

"Fine. Hattie. Good catch."

And that was it. That was the whole of the ninth time.

The foreman came back to her a quarter of an hour later with a piece of paper, which was a delivery sheet, and he held it up and said *do you want to see where it's going, love, because nobody ever wants to see where it's going*, and Hattie Bramm said yes.

The destination was a quay.

> **DESTINATION:** Quay 3, Broomhead Reach, Northglass.
> **BONDS / CUSTOMS STATUS:** goods remain in bond.
> **BAY:** 4.
> **CONTENTS:** SITE CONTENTS — GENERAL, AS PER SCHEDULE.
> **ATTENDANT: 2 (TWO).**

"A bay," said Hattie Bramm. "Bay four."

"That's right, love."

"Can you read the bay number out loud, please, so that the man on the gate writes it down."

The foreman looked at her for a moment and then read it out loud, twice, and a man on the gate with a clipboard wrote it on the sheet in block capitals, twice, and initialled it, and Hattie Bramm wrote both down in her folder in her own hand, and put the date against it, which was the twenty-second of October, in a hand she had been taught at some point and had not lost.

Elias Venn was on the pavement and had heard both readings, and had written down the same number, and did not know what he had. It was a bay on a quay in Northglass.

He knew it.

Quill & Frayle's poster on the wall of a corridor said, in a plastic wallet, *destination: quay-side secure store, awaiting re-issue to purchaser*. The Museum's own box, in its cage, in a corridor off the west range, with a red custody disc and a Registry tag on a padlock, was destined for a quay-side secure store in Northglass.

And the entire remaining paper of a weaving and dyeing mill that had closed in 1974 was destined for a quay on the same river, in the same city, four miles from Civic Hill, in a steel box, on a low-loader, with an *attendant* figure filled in at a yard in a different part of the country by somebody who had never heard of the word slipway.

Two consignments. Two documents. One store, and it was possible — it was not impossible, it was *likely*, because it was the only bonded store in the city that took mill clearances — that they were going to the same four rows of racking on the same quay.

And nobody would ever put the two lists side by side, because one list was a Museum accession, and one was a company schedule, and they were eleven items in one and a hundred and forty people's working lives in the other, and both of them described the same van, on the same afternoon, in October 1974, and neither of them was the other one.

---

They shut the container at about twenty past two in the afternoon.

The doors went together and Upton took a bolt and a spanner and went round the four corners, and then the whole thing went up on the crane, and the crane did not drop it, and the slinger on the hook did not swing it, and the twenty men stood back from the arc of the load in exactly the places men stand back from, because they had been told in ten minutes that morning by a woman from a hire firm what the sheet said and they had been paid hourly since eight and it was half past two and they were going to be paid hourly at five.

*Quay 3, Broomhead Reach. Bay 4. In bond.*

Two slings, blue, four metres, one on each lifting point, and a shackle at the apex, and the man in the cab came down off the boom and put a tag on the hook and read the number of the tag out loud, and the foreman wrote it on the sheet, and the tag number was on the sheet, and the sheet was signed.

That was the signature. Not a signature that said anything. A signature in a box headed *received in good order* by a haulage clerk at a yard, on a Thursday afternoon, by a man who would not be there when the container was opened, on a container that was going into a bonded store where it would sit until somebody paid a fee to have it broken open, and a fee that was very much worth paying, and a man on a quay who had been in the job nineteen years and had signed about eleven thousand of them and had never once opened one and never once been asked to.

A lawful removal of the wrong thing is not sabotage. Elias Venn had understood that sentence since Saturday morning and he had not yet understood what it was for.

This was what it was for. It was for the fact that nobody had done anything wrong on this site today, and nobody would do anything wrong at the yard, and nobody would do anything wrong in the bonded store, and the wage sheets of the Sillick Mill would go over a wall in a steel box at ten past three on a Thursday afternoon in October, in charge, sealed, documented, and *properly handled*, and there would not be a single day of the rest of this claim in which anybody could point at a person and say *that one*.

---

Hattie Bramm came back up the wall at about half past three and stood beside him and did not say anything for a minute.

"You know what the worst of it is," she said. "It's not that they've taken it. They'll always take it; you can't stop a company with a clearance contract. It's that nobody up there has any idea what it was."

"They knew it was paper."

"They knew it was paper the way a man knows a lorry is heavy." She shut the folder. "There was a girl on the top that floor in 'seventy-one called Amy who did the second post, and there was a man called Pryce in the finishing room, and there was an old man called Ledger in the time office who sat on a stool and did nothing but put his hand on people as they went past, and if you had asked any one of those two dozen men in those boots which building that was, not one of them would have been able to tell you whether it was a mill or a brewery."

"No."

"It was a mill," said Hattie Bramm. "And in ten minutes' time it is a steel box on a lorry going past the Old Custom House with a seal on it, and the seal is the only thing anybody will ever know about it, and a seal is not a name."

Elias Venn stood on a wet pavement on Sillick Lane and looked at a slipway where a man of seventy-four had been in the water a fortnight ago, and where a woman of fifty-four had just been right about four inches of it in front of twenty men, and he took the notebook out and put it on the wall and wrote one line with the time against it.

> *Thu 22 Oct, 15:44. 22 on the site, 20 hourly, signed 14:20. Container sealed, tag read out, bay 4, in bond. Nobody opened anything because there was nothing to open. H. Bramm read the bay number and it is BAY 4 and I have got it and I do not yet know what it is for.*

Then he put the pen away and said, because he was going to spend the rest of his life not saying it, "Mrs Bramm, that was a good catch on the slip."

"Don't," said Hattie Bramm. "I told you in September and I will tell you again and it is the same. I do not want to be the woman who was right about a pallet. I have got a hundred and forty people and I have had thirty-one years and a plastic wallet and a letter with a considered number in it, and if the sum total of what I am is a woman who is right about four inches of water in front of twenty men, then I have spent thirty-one years being the gate clerk at a gate that did not exist." She put the hard hat under her arm. "Do not thank me for the pallet. Come and stand in a room and be in it when the number gets said."

She went up Sillick Lane to the tram.

Elias Venn stayed on the pavement until five, and then went and rang the claims manager in Northglass from a call box, and got her voicemail, and left four words on it that said nothing at all.

Then he went up the hill and worked until eight, because there were two hundred and forty dead letters on a desk behind him and a clerk's is a slow job, and a slow job is the only kind of competence anybody in this building has ever let him have.
