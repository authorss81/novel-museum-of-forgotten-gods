# Chapter 0016 — What Is in the Cupboard

The bin went out at fifteen past seven on Friday morning and Elias Venn watched it go from the doorway of the loading bay, standing up, in his coat, at ten to eight, with a day book open on a sack of frames behind him and a working day in front of him that he had not been told he was allowed to have.

It was a wheelie bin. It came down the service lane on its own wheels, past the coal chute grille and the bins and the two shutters, and out through the gate onto the lane, and a man in a high-visibility jacket came up the lane behind it with a hand truck and a sheet of paper, and the sheet had columns on it, and the columns were a date and a number of bales and a destination, and he wrote one figure in a box and the figure was two hundred and something, and the whole transaction took about four minutes.

That was it. That was the entire mechanism by which Wendel Case's correspondence for eleven years had been destroyed, and it was a man with a sheet of paper and a hand truck who had not read a single word of what was in the bin and was never going to and would not be asked to.

Elias could have stopped it. That was the thing he turned over for the rest of the morning. He could have gone out into the lane at a quarter to eight and said a sentence to a man in a high-visibility jacket, and the sentence could have been *that bin is a Museum bin and it has got a name in it and I am a member of staff and here is my card*, and the man would have believed him, and the bin would have gone back down the lane, and it would have been a bin in a loading bay with a sheet of paper over it.

He had a card. It was in his pocket, and it was current, and it opened the service entrance and the security room and the staff lavatories and every door in this building that he was allowed to open, and it was the only authority he had left in the world.

He did not go out into the lane.

And he was careful, on the stairs afterwards, not to let himself be a man who had nearly stopped it. Because that was not the truth. A man who has almost stopped a thing and then does not stop it is a man who has found out what he wants. What he wanted, standing at the doorway at ten to eight, was to go out into the lane. What he did not do was the next part, which was to pick up the telephone at seven o'clock and ring the post room and ask them to hold one bale out of a hundred and fifty for a day. A man who rings at seven in the morning to hold one bale is a man who has admitted to a post room that he has been reading the post.

That was the whole of it. He had been reading other people's post for nine days and he had never once been a person who was allowed to, and now there was a bin going out of the door and the only thing between the bin and a shovel was a decision, and he had made the decision, and it took about a second, and it was the first decision he had made in ten days that he had not written down.

---

The tram took forty minutes and the stop was at the top of Sillick Lane and he got off at half past nine and walked down the wrong side of the street in the rain, past the wall and the laundrette and the shuttered shop, and did not go all the way to the fence, because it was not the fence he was going to.

The Sillick Lane branch library opened at nine on a Friday and by ten past nine the back room was full.

A queue, with tickets, and a folding table with a jug of water on it, and a woman at the table writing down names and addresses in a hard hand while a younger woman with a laptop read the letters out loud to whoever had come to the front. It was the same room and the same eleven people and the same jug of water, and there was a young man off a night bus with a head injury who was pretending to be there about a bicycle, and a woman with a bailiff's letter in her coat who had brought it to be understood rather than handled, and a man who had brought a hoover.

At twenty to ten Hattie Bramm put her pen down and said, "You."

"You know me."

"I've a file with you in it now." She did not look pleased. "That's what I said, isn't it. *You'll find out who I am and you'll be right.* And a man came into this room three weeks ago with a photograph of a cat, and he was a stranger, and he stood where you're standing, and he read the whole of the room the way you read a room, and he said a name out loud twice."

"Who was it?"

"That's the thing I'm not going to do." She put the pen back down. "Siobhan Keogh. And if I say that name in this room then a woman in the second row goes home tonight and says it out loud, and in about three weeks that name is a thing a solicitor has, and I have spent nine years keeping a list of names out of this room. So you'll forgive me if your friend with the cat does not get a name at this table."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Sit down. You've come at a bad minute, because we've got a man at the front who's been round twice, and if you sit at the end there I'll not have to keep an eye on you."

He sat down at the end.

---

She did not come to him for fifty minutes. She worked the queue, and he watched a woman with a hard hand and a laptop run a room of eleven people the way he had spent two years running a set of corridors, and understood that it was the same job.

And when she came, it was to tell him she was going out, because the room had got to the point where the room was going to be the room, and she took her coat off the hook and did not put it on.

"Ten minutes. Not in there. There's a bench outside the side door and a bin and a man in a van looking at a mobile phone like it's going to change."

They stood on the pavement. It had stopped raining and then started again at the far end of the street in the way it does in Low Ward.

"I've been thinking about you all week," Hattie Bramm said, "and I'll tell you what's been keeping me awake, and it isn't you. It's that you went away and you didn't come back, and I sat here on the Friday and the Friday after and the Friday before and I watched the door about a hundred times, and I said to myself, he's one of the ones that says a thing and then goes."

"I couldn't come back."

"You could have come back and said you'd had a hard week."

"Yes."

"Then why didn't you."

"Because I'd have had to tell you which week," Elias said, "and the week I was in is not a story I can put in a municipal library."

Hattie Bramm looked at him for a second, and then she laughed, and it was not unkind.

"Right," she said. "That's the first true thing anybody's said in this room in a month. Now come and look at this."

---

She had it in a plastic wallet in her inside coat, and she did not take it out on the pavement.

"I've had it three weeks and I've not answered it and I've not told a soul, and I'm telling you because you are the only person in this city who has been rude to me for money, and I want somebody to tell me I've read it right."

"You want somebody to tell you the right answer, and you don't have anybody."

"I want somebody to tell me I've read it right." She turned the wallet over in her hand. "It's on their letterhead, the site people, the ones that bought it off the receiver. Third week of September. It says the new owner has reviewed the file and that in the interests of all parties, and I quote, *the company is prepared to arrange an independent review of the final shortfall as recorded in the works' office papers, at the company's cost, without admission of liability, on the basis that the correspondence at present in circulation between individual former employees and the company's solicitors ceases for a period of twelve months from the date of acceptance.*"

"And?"

"And there is a number in it. There is a number in it and it's not a round number, and a round number is a bucket and a number that isn't round is a *considered* number, and it's the first considered number anybody in this city has put in front of me in nine years." She put her hand flat on the wallet. "And the twelve months is the whole of it. Twelve months, and it starts when I sign, and I stop writing to anybody, and after twelve months the position is that a woman in Ferry Bank who has been writing letters since before you were at school has stopped writing letters for a year."

"And you haven't replied."

"I've not replied because I've not told anybody, and I have not told anybody because the day I tell this room is the day it is out, and the day it's out is the day that somebody in a solicitor's office knows I have been offered a considered number, and from that minute I am negotiating." She looked at him. "That's it, isn't it. That's the whole trick of it. The ledger's the only thing that can't be divided up and shared out and settled in three rooms. A number in an offer letter can."

"Yes."

"So you know what I did." She turned round and looked at the back of the library through the glass. "I did nothing, and I've done nothing for three weeks, and I've been sitting in this room every Friday with a wallet in my coat, and there are sixty-one people on my list and I cannot tell one of them, because the second I tell one of them I have to tell the lot, and the minute I tell the lot this offer is dead and it was never worth more than the paper it's on."

Elias looked at the wet street.

"And nobody in the world knows you have it," he said.

"Quill and Frayle know, and the man in the van knows, and my niece knows because she's post, and that's three people and two of them are paid."

"Then nobody in the world knows," Elias said, "and it stays that way, and you have not told the Museum, and you are not going to tell the Museum."

"Are you all right?"

"I'm extremely all right." He was, and he heard himself say it, and understood at the same moment that the sentence had been produced by something older than thought. "Mrs Bramm. There is a thing in this city that could be printed tomorrow, and it would print your name and your letterhead and the number, and it would print it on a Thursday. And you would be finished as a person anybody in this building could help, because the moment a number is public, a number is the whole of the argument, and there is nothing else to say. So no. Nobody in the world knows, and it is going to stay that way, and I am not going to tell anybody, and if anybody comes to me and asks me whether anybody has had an offer, the answer is that I have never heard of it."

Hattie Bramm looked at him for a long time.

"You've never heard of it," she said. "Say it again and look at me while you say it."

"Fine. I've never heard of it."

"Good." She put the wallet back in her coat. "Now. And this is the last of it and then I'm going back in, because there's a woman on the second row who has been crying quietly for twenty minutes and that's not her business and it's going to be my business in about a minute."

"Go on."

"Where is it."

"Where is what?"

"The thing in your building that you won't tell me about." She took her glasses off and cleaned them on her sleeve, which Doreen Sarn also did, on a tablecloth, in Ferry Bank, three days ago, and Elias found that he nearly told her both of those women at once. "I've had nine years of nobody telling me anything, and I have worked out what it is that they think they're protecting me from, and it isn't me, love. It's them. It's the answer to *what is in the cupboard*, and the day there's an answer in the cupboard, somebody's going to have to be in the room when it's opened, and it isn't going to be you and it isn't going to be a solicitor with a considered number. It's the woman who wrote the figures down, and she's the only one of us who's still alive who did it, and I have been trying to find out who she is for two years."

Elias Venn stood in the rain on a pavement in Low Ward with a queue behind a door and understood that he had a choice, and that he had about four seconds in which to make it, and that this time there was no gap in which nothing had happened yet.

He could have said *I can't tell you that*, and it would have been a good answer, and it would have been true, and it would have closed the conversation, and nobody in that room would ever have heard a name or a place.

He had had the choice and he had not taken it, and the reason he had not taken it was a table in Ferry Bank on Wednesday afternoon with a cloth on it and a hardback cash book with a rubber band round it, and a woman saying *thirty-one years I've had a copy of that sheet and nobody to give it to*, and four photographs in a registered envelope in the Monday bag that had gone out of a building on a Wednesday, and a mint in a basement that he had not opened, and a man's letter in a post room that Mara Iles was taking to the Board Secretary by the end of the day.

He had handed over everything he could get hold of. Four times in four days. To a duty manager, to a duty manager again, to a registrar, and once by not doing anything at all.

And the private channel was still there, and it was still him, and it was the only place in Morrowgate where a fact was safe, and every single time he had put a fact in it this week it had cost somebody the ability to see it.

"The Hall of Former Faiths," Elias said. "On the ground floor, the long room with the plinths down the middle. The case that's at the end of the row on the east side, under a brass surround, and it has a small bronze bell in it about the size of a fist, and there's a staple through a crack in the shoulder, and the label on it says it was cast in 1902 and it does not say where it came from, and there's an inspector's tag on the cage it is in at the moment, in a corridor by the lavatories on the ground floor, with my Director's initials on it."

It took about eleven seconds to say.

The library was behind a glass door and the door was shut, and he had not raised his voice, and there were eleven people in that room and two of them had a bad chest and one of them had been crying for twenty minutes and Hattie Bramm was standing on a wet pavement a yard away from him with her coat off.

"Did they hear that," he said.

"The man by the door heard that. He's been coming three weeks and he's never spoken to anybody, and he's got a hearing aid he doesn't turn on." She was already turning to go. "I told you I would not ask him. He comes because it's a Friday and there's a room where nobody asks you anything. That's the whole of what he's after and it is not my business to find out what it is."

"Was there a number," he said.

"Say what?"

"When I said *plinth*." And then he heard himself, and he heard the four seconds go by, and he stopped. "No. I don't have a number. There's a number in this and it's not mine to give out and it is not the number you want, and if I say it in this street it will be in four rooms by Monday and that will be the end of the offer in your coat and the beginning of something much worse for you. So I am not going to say it, and I am not going to say a name either, and I want you to know that the not-saying is the hardest thing I have done in ten days and it is not a trick."

"Say what name."

"Any name. Mrs Bramm. There is a name in this that I could say in about a second and it would be the most useful thing I have said all week, and I am not going to say it, and I am not going to say it because I have watched what happens to a name that gets said out loud in a room, and I have watched it happen to a woman this week who has been doing this for thirty-one years and who is better at it than me, and I am not going to do it to her in a municipal back room on a Friday afternoon."

Hattie Bramm stood very still on a wet pavement.

"Case," she said.

"—"

"There's a Case at number four. Sillick Lane. He can't write and he's in his sixties and his daughter does his post, and she came to me in the spring about a letters thing and I gave her the same slip of paper I give everybody." Her voice did not change at all. "And you have a name in this that starts the same and you won't say it in this street, and I have known the name Case since the spring and I have not said it once in this room and I am not going to start now, and that is not because I'm being careful. That's because I have been the person who knows a name in a room for thirty-one years and I know exactly what it costs the room to have one, and it costs me most of all, and I have paid it every time."

She went in.

Elias stood on the pavement in the rain and had the four seconds again, and this time what came out of them was not a number and not a name, it was a nausea, because he had been about to be a man who keeps people safe, and he had just said a room out loud in a street in front of a glass door, and he could not take any of it back, and it was the third time in five days that he had put a fact in a public place and told himself it was because the person was owed it.

---

He waited until one.

It was a Friday queue and the queue went on past the hour and then thinned, and the room emptied out into a Friday afternoon the way rooms do, and the woman with the bailiff's letter left with it still in her coat, and the man with the hoover put it on again, and Hattie Bramm squared the list and put the cap on the pen.

"That's the queue," she said. "That's it, that's the whole of what it's for, and there'll be another one next Friday and the Friday after, and there's not a dollar in this city that changes that, and I've stopped hoping it will."

"Can I help?"

"You can carry the table out to the van." She looked at him. "And you can come back next Friday and you can do it again, and you can tell me your name, because I have not got it, and I have had a man in my room three times who I could not swear to, and it turns out that is a thing that keeps me up at night in a way I had not expected."

"Elias Venn."

"Elias Venn." She wrote it in the back of the list, under the line. "Right. That's the first thing I've got on you. And now go and see the old fella."

Elias stopped.

"He's not in the queue any more. He was, though, for an hour, and he's still here, because he came for the queue and there's nowhere to sit if you're not in the queue, and that's a whole thing of its own and it's not mine to run." She tipped her head at the far end of the room.

The man by the door.

He was in his sixties, grey, in a plain dark coat, and there was a shopping bag on his knees with the top of a tin of tea sticking out of it, and a pair of work boots under the chair that had been resoled once, and a hearing aid he had not turned on, and he was sitting on a chair in a municipal library in Low Ward on a Friday afternoon with a piece of paper in his hand that had nothing on it.

Elias Venn stood three feet away from him in a room with eleven empty chairs in it.

He had the name. He had the name and the grade and the eleven years and the Saturday shift and the tin of tea in the basement and the number on a piece of paper in a pocket, and he could have said *Perry Loach, you're on Saturdays, you were at the dye house, you said a number on Saturday night at plinth thirty-four and you stopped* and had the whole thing in about eleven seconds, and the man would have looked up.

And he stood there for about ten seconds, and did not say it.

Because the man had not asked him anything, and because a man who says a number at a plinth at ten o'clock on a Saturday night has not consented to be asked, and because asking was a thing that needed a form and a supervisor and the man's own hand at the bottom of it, and because he had watched, on Saturday night, a woman with a stopwatch decline to fill in a box that wanted exactly that, in front of him, for reasons she had said out loud and for reasons that were entirely correct, and he had not understood them at the time.

He had the name, and he did not spend it, and he understood — standing in a library with his heart going and a tin of tea in a shopping bag four feet from his hand — that this was the second time in five days that he had decided alone, in a room, that somebody was safer not knowing something, and that the first time had been in a basement, and that both times he had been right, and that both times he had also been the only person in the building making the decision.

He walked out.

Hattie Bramm was at the door with her coat on and she watched him walk past the man and out, and she did not say a word, and that was the single most damning thing anybody did to him that week.

---

He got the tram back up the hill at twenty to two.

He had given a room, a row, a plinth, a date and a case to a woman in a street in front of a glass door, on a Friday afternoon, and he had said a number in the same street four hours ago to a woman in a queue, and a number in a queue is a thing that a man in overalls has to guess, and a number guessed wrongly in a room of eleven people is a rumour, and a rumour in this district travels four miles in a day and gets into a solicitor's file in a fortnight.

And the number was ninety-four.

And the number was a count of people on a shift on the last morning a mill ever ran, and the pay run had listed fewer than were on the gate, and nobody in the city knew that but him, and he had just put it into a room.

He sat on the tram with his hands on his knees and did the only arithmetic there was.

Doreen Sarn had a copy of the shortfall sheet in a hardback cash book in Ferry Bank and she was seventy-eight and she had no one to give it to.

Hattie Bramm had sixty-one names and a nine-year chase and a considered number in a plastic wallet in her coat and nobody in the world who knew, and now, since twenty past ten that morning, a description of a case in a gallery in a museum four miles away.

I. Case, at number four, writing for his father, saying *we know about the money*, in a letter that a registrar was carrying to the Board Secretary by the end of the day, about a bit of paper in a tin in a locker in a basement that nobody was going to open, because the man who could have opened it was a suspended night guard standing in a library choosing not to say a name.

And a night cleaner in a shopping bag in a municipal back room with a piece of paper with nothing on it, who had come for three weeks and had never spoken to anybody, and who was on the floors tomorrow night with a mop, and whom no one had asked, and whom Elias Venn had walked past.

Four doors, and four locks, and every one of them was locked by him.

He got off the tram at the top of the hill at five past three and stood in the rain outside a building he was not allowed to walk into, and understood that he had spent five days giving things away to every person who had asked him for one, and that the only thing he had managed to keep hold of in ten days was a man in a chair who had not asked.
