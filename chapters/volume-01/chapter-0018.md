# Chapter 0018 — The Two-Hands Thing

He did not sleep, and he was not surprised, and at ten on Sunday morning he sat on a bench in a laundrette with eleven machines running and a cup of tea he had not asked for and told his mother the thing he had come to say.

"You asked me on Sunday morning why I never ask you for the letter."

"I did."

"I lied to Mara on Saturday night." He put his hands round the mug. "On Saturday night, at about ten to eleven, she asked me a straight question — did I have anything in the notebook that was not on the page — and I said no. And there was. There's a number and there's a man's name and there's four seconds of a night I can't account for, and I said no to a question that she had every right to ask me and I said it to her face."

Helen Venn took the cloth off the machine and shook it out and did not look at him.

"Did she know it was a lie?"

"No."

"Then you've not told her yet."

"I'm going to tell her this afternoon."

"You're going to tell her this afternoon, and she's going to be very good about it, because she's good at her job, and you're going to come out of it feeling better and you will not have been forgiven, and those are three separate things and you've only booked one." She hung the cloth over the rail. "And when she asks you why you did it, you're going to say it was to protect somebody, and it's going to be true, and it will be the worst answer in the world. There's no way of saying that makes it not a lie."

"I know."

"You know it the way you know the tide's out." She sat down on the end of the bench. "Ask me for the letter tonight. Not because you'll do anything with it. Because it'll be a thing that's been said, and you'll want one of those this week."

---

Cliff Sowerby was in a bed in the north of the city with a dislocated shoulder, a wrist, a head, and a temperature of about thirty-four, and he was seventy-four, and he would not be coming home before the end of the week, and nobody knew whether the arm would come back properly, and the arithmetic of that was not going to be settled for six weeks and would be settled by somebody else in an office.

Hattie Bramm had not gone to work, because it was a Sunday and there was no queue, and she was in her own front room on Sillick Lane with the curtains not drawn and a telephone on the arm of the chair and a photograph of a man in a hat in a flat cap, taken on a wet site sometime in June, on a rucksack strap.

"Sit down," she said. "You've come to give it back."

"I've come to give it back, and to say that I said it in a street on a Friday morning with a glass door between me and eleven people."

"Yes, I know. That's why there's a woman in the second row who came last Friday and told her sister before she'd got off the tram." Hattie Bramm put the photograph face down on the arm of the chair. "And I've been sat here since a quarter to six yesterday morning with the telephone ringing off the wall, and I want to say something to you first and then I'll hear the rest."

"Say it."

"You've killed it. Whatever was in that letter, whatever they were going to pay me to stop — it's dead, and it's dead because there's a man of seventy-four in a bed in the north end and the whole of Low Ward knows it by lunchtime." She was entirely calm. "Nine years. I had it in a plastic wallet in my coat for three weeks and I hadn't told a soul, and it was worth more than the money, and it was worth more than the money because it was *mine*, and I could not put it down for one second without somebody in this city being able to say I'd been bought. And now it's a photograph in the *Chronicle* with a hoist in it and a man's arm sticking out of it, and there's not one person in this district who will ever hear the word *review* again."

"Yes."

"So don't stand there being sorry. Being sorry's for people who did it on purpose." She pushed the chair towards him with her foot. "Go on. What else."

---

The rest of it took twenty minutes and he told it in order, standing in a front room in Sillick Lane with a photograph of Cliff Sowerby face down on the arm of a chair, and Hattie Bramm did not interrupt him once, which after Wednesday was the single most surprising thing that had happened to him all week.

The site. The lorry. The consignment note. The rail that was off and the board that went. The woman in the cabin. The line. Four minutes.

"You didn't go in."

"No."

"That was right. A man of seventy-four in six-degree water with a bad shoulder doesn't need a second man in it, he needs to be got out of it by somebody who knows the throw is coming." She said it without a flicker. "Did you know a throw was coming?"

"No. I went up the office steps three at a time and there was a coil of line on a nail in the first aid box and I had never seen it before in my life."

"You went in a locked office on a fenced site and took a stranger's property off a nail."

"Yes."

"And if there hadn't been one."

"Then Cliff Sowerby is dead on a slipway in a canal and I am stood in a museum corridor four miles away having been told about it by a woman at a desk." Elias Venn said it flatly. "That is the whole of what I did all day, and I have been a guard for six years and I have never once been in a position where the correct thing to do was *nothing* and I did nothing, and it was the correct thing."

She let that sit for a moment.

"Was your name on a form?"

"It was on a form. This afternoon. And they read it out, and it's got a crime number on it, and I gave them the true version, which is that I was on a site where I had no right to be because I knew there was going to be a man of seventy-four in a canal and nobody else in this city knew that. And the man with the clipboard wrote it down and didn't invent anything, and he's going to hand it in tonight, and it's a piece of paper that says my name on a fall at a place I was trespassing."

"Good."

"'Good'?"

"You said the true version, to a policeman, about a trespass, about a man of seventy-four, and you gave him the consignment note." Hattie Bramm picked the photograph up off the arm of the chair and looked at it. "I've been in nine hearings, love. Nine. And the thing that sinks a claim every single time is not a bad man, it's a quiet one, and it's a woman who says *well I've not got anything to hand you* and puts her bag on her knee. And you've handed them the line off the lorry and your own name and a man on a trolley. That's four things that people will still be able to use in three years when everything else has gone cold."

Elias looked at her.

"Why have you still got the photograph."

"Because you gave it to me and I took it."

"That isn't a reason."

"It's the only reason I've got." She put it down, face up, on the table. "Here's what I've done with it and you're not going to like it. It's in a drawer in this house, in a house where a man of seventy-four used to live, with a man of seventy-four in a bed in the north end. Not in your building. Not in a museum. Not with me. And you keep your end of it and I'll keep mine, and we don't put them together, and the day the two of us want the same thing we'll find each other without a lawyer between us, because a lawyer is the only person in this city who benefits from the two halves of a thing being kept apart."

She stood up.

"Now go and tell the woman with the log."

---

Ferry Bank at half past one on a Sunday afternoon.

Doreen Sarn had the net curtain half down and the plastic tumbler on the sill and the radio going very low, and the blue folder was on the table and the hardback cash book was on the table with the rubber band round it, and she had put her cardigan on over the pinafore and had not put her coat on, which meant she was expecting somebody.

"I want to tell you something and I want to tell you before you sit down," she said. "I've had the fire lit since nine because I knew you were coming, and I've had it lit because I've been sitting here since Friday night going through every single thing I did in that office, and I'd rather do it warm."

She sat down and folded her hands and waited for him, and he told her.

Not the part about the object in the case, and not the number, and not the two-handed notebook, and not the reading, because those were not the part. The part was a table and a cloth and a hard hand and a pen going onto a figure twice and a woman's voice saying *you'll have to ask the clerk, love, you'll have to ask the clerk for that*, and a sheet folded in three and a cloth going over it and a sentence the lamp had kept for thirty-one years.

He told her he had been in a room on Civic Hill at ten o'clock on a Monday night and put his hands on a lamp that had been in her flat, and that it had shown him her table, and that it had shown her hands, and that it had not shown her face and had never been able to show her face because a lamp on a table cannot see a face.

He told her that he had asked the lamp who carried it out of the last building it was in, and that he had got a kitchen.

He told her that it had repeated her sentence back at him word for word, after thirty-one years, and that this was not a ghost and not a message, and that the reason it had repeated the sentence was that it had been in the room when she said it and it was a lamp and it had nothing else to do with a sentence.

Doreen Sarn sat in a chair by a lit gas fire with a plastic tumbler on the sill behind her and did not interrupt him once, and when he had finished she sat for about a minute looking at the tablecloth, and then she said:

"Did it show my writing?"

"Yes."

"Then it's a document." She said it in exactly the way Mara Iles said things, and Elias understood that it was not a coincidence and that a person who has spent forty years making columns agree does not need to be taught what a document is. "Because a document is a thing a person wrote, and it's the writing that makes it theirs, and the hand's on it, and you can't get that out of a lamp. That's just me. And everything else in that thing was the room, and the room doesn't know anything and it repeats itself, and I've had thirty-one years of a room that doesn't know anything."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And the man at the side of my table. It didn't give you a face."

"No, ma'am."

"You couldn't tell if it was him."

"I could not tell if it was any of them." He had not said that to anybody and it came out of him without being asked for. "That is the part I've been carrying since Monday. I've got a receipt in a drawer that puts a name to him and I've got a woman at a table, and I cannot tell you that they are the same man, and I cannot tell you that they are two, and that is not a small thing, Doreen, and I have not said it out loud to anybody and I have said it out loud to you."

"Then you will not do it again on your own."

"No."

"You'll do it again next week. I can see it on you. You'll do it because there'll be a night and nothing to do and something will make a noise." She had not raised her voice once and she was not going to. "Right. Listen to me, because I'm seventy-eight and I'm the only one in this city who can say this and mean it. There is a thing in you that can do that, and you have worked out how to keep it safe by being the only person who has it, and that is exactly how it will kill you, and not by doing anything to you. It will kill you by being the only place a thing lives."

"Mara said—"

"Mara's a good girl with a book." Doreen Sarn cut across him. "I'm a pay clerk who's been the only copy since 1974. And I can tell you what a book does, son. A book gets lent. A book gets copied. A book gets read in a room with the people it is about in it, and then it belongs to more than one person and nobody can be got at over it. And the thing you've got is a coat pocket, and a coat pocket is a book with one reader, and one reader is a *liability*."

"Elias," she said — and it was the first time that afternoon she had used his name — "you'd better not be the only one who can do it. Because if you're the only one, then by Christmas there's a hundred people in this city who need you to do it, and you will do it, because you can't not, and you will be twenty-six years old in a bad night with a headache and a hundred people waiting, and there'll be nobody to say the word."

"There was nobody to say the word on Monday."

"I know. I heard you tell me." She put her hand flat on the cash book. "So build a room with somebody in it who can say it. That's the answer. It's the only answer there is and it's not mine, it's just what's true."

---

The *Chronicle* came at four o'clock and it was on the counter of the laundrette because Helen Venn bought one every Sunday and it was on the arm of his chair when he got back.

It was a photograph of a lorry on a slipway, taken from a distance, badly, in the rain, by a man holding a camera one-handed. Cliff Sowerby was not in it. Underneath it, three lines about a seventy-four-year-old, and a temperature, and *police are anxious that anyone was on the site.*

And beneath that, cut off at the bottom of the column and set much smaller, a second photograph, and it was not of the lorry.

It was a photograph of a gate.

An iron gate in a brick wall with a hump over it, and a yard behind it, and a weigher's hut, and the caption said: *The Sillick Mill weighbridge, which was demolished in 1998. Picture: Northglass Picture Library.*

And under that, in a box:

> **CLEARANCE BEGINS TODAY.** The last of the Sillick Mill site in Low Ward is due to be cleared by the close of Sunday. Hallowgate Foyston, which bought the freehold from the receiver in 2019, said the site had "no significant features of architectural interest."

Elias Venn sat in a laundrette in Low Ward and read *no significant features of architectural interest* four times, and understood that a man in a good coat had written that sentence in an office, and that it was, in the narrow sense, quite true, and that it was going to be in the hands of about forty thousand people by teatime tomorrow, and that in about a week it would be in the hands of a claims agent in Northglass, and that there would be no way on God's earth afterwards to write a headline that said *a weighbridge and a man in a flat cap and a gate count*.

A gate, photographed, in a newspaper, on the day the last of the paper came off the site.

He had spent ten days learning that the object could not speak to anybody and could only repeat an act. And on Sunday afternoon a photographer had done the same thing a hundred times better than he ever could, and had been paid for it, and it was on a front page.

---

The conservator came down at half past four and signed the book at the service entrance herself, and wrote her own name and the time and the word *escort* in the column for the department, and did not make a joke about it, and Elias noticed that she had not made a joke about it and understood that this was the single most alarming thing about the afternoon.

The label room was windowless and cold and lit by two strips, and the card cutter was on the bench with a piece of card in it half cut, and there were trays of cut blanks, and the boiler was off on a Sunday.

"Twenty minutes," Mara Iles said. "And I want you to know that I have thought about the room before I asked you to come down here, and I chose it, and here is why."

"Because it's the only room in this building with a card cutter in it."

"It is a conservation workspace, and it is entered by a card, and there is a camera on the lintel that covers the door and the bench and about a metre of the room, and you are on a restriction, and I have put us both in the one room in this building that photographs us." She put her bag down. "And neither of us can be called out of a gallery by a member of the public. And it means that in about four hundred years somebody will make a card in here with our two names on the back of it, and I thought that was worth the walk."

"Sit down," she said. "No. Stand up. I want you standing, because I am going to read something and I would like you to hear it as a document and not as a voice."

---

She took a sheet out of the file. Not the notebook. A typed list, in her own hand, on a sheet of conservation department paper, dated the eighteenth of October, and she gave it to him upside down and he read it upside down because she wanted him to.

> *Result: a number.*
> *Occupancy line: one cleaner.*
> *Account of any person present during result: [blank]*
> *Time limit as agreed: eleven minutes. Time taken: eleven minutes. Stop word: fault, said by witness and by reader.*
> *Cost named before: nosebleed; headache, previous instance eleven hours.*
> *The reader did not say whether the number had an object. The witness asked. The reader said he had given the shape and the units and no object.*
> *The witness did not fill the account box. The witness's reason, given aloud: the box takes a voluntary statement in the person's own words and countersigned, and the witness will not take one off a night cleaner at a plinth without his supervisor, and will not put a guard's second-hand version in a box that wants the man's words.*

"That is the whole of the first one," Mara Iles said, "and it is accurate, and it is in the file, and there is nothing wrong with it anywhere, and it is going to be in the file for ever, and if somebody in four years asks me what happened in this building on the tenth of October, that is what I will hand them."

"Yes."

"Now the second one. I am not reading this from the file because there is no file. So I am going to read it from the record of a car park in Northglass, which is the only place a record of it exists, and it is a record of a car space."

"Mara—"

"Hold on. I am doing it and you are going to let me." She squared the sheet. "Four photographs, deposited by you on Tuesday, sealed, in an envelope with your name on it, and a covering statement of four hundred words in which you set out that you broke a printed rule twice, that you put a card back out of true deliberately, that you signed a device-book entry with no destination, and that a fault report of yours contains four false words. The first four of those are on the record. The fifth is on the record because you told a duty manager about it in a day book at six o'clock in the evening and I read the day book on Wednesday because I read the day book every day, and I have read the day book every day since the tenth of October, which you did not know, and I would like you to sit with the fact that I read it every day and that it took me two days to decide what to do about it."

Elias stood very still.

"And on the eighteenth of October, at four o'clock this afternoon, I sent a covering note to the Board Secretary, and to the audit provider, and I have initialled it and dated it, and it says the following, and you may read it."

She turned the sheet round and gave it to him the right way up.

> **TO: BOARD SECRETARY. COPY: AUDIT AND ASSURANCE (rev. 2, records).**
> **RE: MATTER OF 1961/LW/44 (Sillick Mill papers), fitter's lamp 1961/LW/51, unaddressed and returned correspondence, and locker 81.**
>

> 2. A request is hereby made to the Board for a written instruction as to the personal effects and correspondence held in staff locker 81, which bears a brass plate reading **CASE, W.** and which has not been opened. > 3. I record that a *name* should be entered in the box headed *account of any person present during result* on the protocol form of 10 October. The name is withheld in this note. I do not withhold it from the Board. I withhold it from the file, because the file is a document and a name in a document is a thing that follows a person, and this week in this building a man was asked about a drawer by a woman with a lanyard and I have not found out what she asked him.
> 4. I record that a photograph of a card, taken by the reader on 8 October, shows an erased name, and that the Registry photographed the same card on 12 October and described the erasure as *partial*, and that both of those documents are now in two institutions' files and neither of them says the name.

Elias read it twice.

"You've put a name in a document."

"I have. On a Saturday, in a room with a table, on a piece of conservation department paper, with a date and a time and my initials, and I have sent it to a member of the Board and to an auditor." She put her hands flat on the bench. "And I have done it because on Friday you told a room of eleven people where a bell is, and if that bell is ever moved, or ever re-labelled, or ever goes into a store, then the person who is owed the most by it is a woman of seventy-eight in Ferry Bank and a woman of fifty-four in this city, and neither of them is ever going to be in a room with a Board paper, and if there is not a name in a document somewhere then the only record that the person called Case ever worked here is a rubber on a card in a drawer in a room with no windows."

"That's—"

"That is me breaking a rule," Mara Iles said, "and I have done it, and you are not to thank me, and we are not even. And now sit down, because I have not said the other thing yet and I have been putting it off for seven days and I am not going to put it off in a room I chose."

---

She stood with her back to the card cutter.

"Say it."

"I can't."

"You can."

"Mara." He had his hands in his pockets and he took them out again. "On Saturday night, at about a quarter to eleven, you asked me a direct question and I said no. And I want to say the sentence out loud so that it is out loud, and then I am going to stop, and then you are going to do the thing you are going to do, and I am not going to argue with it."

"Say it."

"I said no to your question, and I lied to your face, and I did it twice over, because I had already not told you about a man with a mop, and I told you that was nothing to do with what I was telling you about, and it was." He looked at the bench. "And I did it because I thought if one person knew a fact and that person was me, then nobody could get it off me and nobody could use it, and I have been doing that since the Tuesday of the sixth of October and it has got worse every single day and yesterday it got a man of seventy-four into a canal."

The room was very quiet. The boiler was off and the strips hummed.

"I am not going to forgive you," Mara Iles said.

"I know."

"I am going to say what I am going to do, and then I am going to stop talking about it, and you are going to have to live in it."

"Please."

"Everything I have is in a file with a number on it, and that is what I am for, and it is the only thing in this building I have ever been sure of." She turned round. "So. One. You do not keep a fact in a coat. Not a number, not a name, not a photograph, not a set of initials. Anything you find goes into the file the same day, and if you cannot put it in the file because the file cannot take it, then you bring it to me and I will build a form that can take it, and I will do that in my own time and I will not thank you for the work."

"And if I can't get to you."

"Then you write it down on the back of a duty roster and you telephone me and you say *I have got something and I cannot get it to you*, and I will come and get it. That is the whole arrangement and it is not a condition, it is just what I am offering." She put one finger up, and it was the same finger as Fenn's, and it was the same finger as her own on Saturday afternoon in a corridor, and by that evening there were three women in this city who held one finger up and would not put it down. "Two. And this is the one you are not going to like. Nobody in this building decides on their own that a person is safer not knowing. If that is going to happen, it happens in the file, with two names on it, and the person it is about gets told eventually. Not never. Eventually."

Elias Venn did not say anything for a while.

"You put that in because of the locker."

"I put that in because of the locker and because you told a room of eleven people where a bell is on a Friday morning, and because the man in the locker has a daughter who writes letters and I have read that letter and it is in a file now with a number on it, and it says *we know about the money*, and that sentence is now in this building's records and it cannot be taken out, and that is a thing that was a woman's letter on a Friday and is a document on a Sunday, and you did that as surely as if you'd written it yourself." She picked up her bag. "You were careful with a number. You were not careful with a room."

---

He walked her out to the lift, or as far as the stair, because the label room is a conservation workspace and the lift is in the conservation corridor and the card reader there is a card reader and he does not have a card that opens it, and they stood on a landing.

"Two things," she said. "Both of them are about other people, and neither of them is about us, and I want to say that at the start so that nobody can be clever later."

"All right."

"One. A Registry assessor has this building on a rolling programme and has named the Hall of Former Faiths for a material-change inspection, and it is a form, and it takes a *request*, and a request has to come from a member of the public." She said it without any expression at all. "Anybody can put it in at the front desk. It takes a name and it takes a reason and it does not have to be a good reason. It is a public right and it is on the notice board and it has been on the notice board for six years and nobody has ever used it, because everybody who works in a building assumes the building is theirs."

"Mara—"

"Two." She was still holding her bag. "The account box is blank and it is going to stay blank, because it takes a voluntary statement in a man's own words and a countersignature and it is not going to take a guard's version. I told you that on Saturday and I have not changed my mind and I am not going to. Which means that a man who said a number at a plinth at ten o'clock on a Saturday night and stopped, and who has not been asked a single question, stays outside every record in this city until somebody goes and asks him, and nobody in this building has any apparatus for doing that."

"Then we build one."

"Yes," said Mara Iles. "We do."

"You."

"I do. Not you, and not in a week, and not at ten at night on a Monday." She shifted her bag to her other hand. "It will be a form with his name on it and his own words on it and his own signature at the bottom, and it will say that he may leave it blank, and it will be countersigned by his supervisor, and it will not be filled in from a memory. And it will be the only document I have ever written that anybody can be *proud* of, and I am not going to let you write a word of it."

"Understood."

"I want to be quite clear about one thing, because you have had a bad fortnight and I am not going to be kind about it to you by accident." She was at the stair door. "I am not doing this for you. If you go tomorrow, or if you are barred, or if you are never allowed inside this building again, I will still build it, and I will put your name on it, because the man who went into a canal for him was you. But you do not get to think that this is us. We are not us. There is a protocol and there are two conditions and one of them is a relationship, and the relationship is that there is no more private channel, and I am telling you that so that you understand that you are not getting it back."

Elias Venn stood on a landing on the first floor of a building he was not allowed to be on and understood that he had been told, out loud, in a room with a card cutter in it, by a woman with a bag, that she was going to build a thing that would outlive him and that he was not going to be allowed near it, and that she was going to do it anyway.

"Yes," he said.

"Eleven on the Monday," Mara Iles said, "half past two, and you will bring the notebook, and we will put them side by side and you will tell me which of them you did not write, and there will be a figure in that notebook that is not in my file, and I am not going to ask you for it in a corridor, and I am going to ask you for it at a table on Monday, and you are going to have to decide what you say."

---

He got off at half past ten.

The last post had gone down the lane at about seven and the blue bales were standing by the roller door in the rain in a row, three of them, with a sheet of paper over the top of them weighted with a brick, and somebody had come at about ten and taken it away, and there was a wet rectangle on the concrete where it had been.

Four hours since Mara Iles had left the label room, and the schedule of the last fifty years had come off the front of a Sunday paper in a photograph of a weighbridge, and a man of seventy-four was somewhere in the north of the city with a temperature of about thirty-four and an arm, and there was a dead man's post in a pulp mill in Northglass, and a box in a cage with a Saturday on its docket and a clearance finishing on Sunday, and a bell in a cage with a red disc on it and a staple through a crack in its shoulder, and the crack was still there, and it was not going to heal, and nobody was ever going to put that in a condition.

Elias Venn stood in a loading bay in the rain and did the only arithmetic there was, standing up, in his own notebook, in the flat block hand, with a time against it, because a fact with no time on it is a fact with no end on it.

Then he wrote one line at the bottom and put the book away.

> Sun 18 Oct, 22:40. Mara has it and I do not, and that is the correct position and it is the whole of what I have learned in seven days and I have learned it from a woman with a cash book and a woman with a stopwatch and a woman with a bone folder, none of whom can read a bell and all of whom can read a room.
>
> I have still got one thing in the book that is not in her file and I am not going to write it down here.
>
> On Monday at half past two I am going to have to decide whether to say a name in a room, and I already know that I will not say it, and I already know what that costs, and the cost is that the only form that will ever hold that man is one I am not allowed to build.
