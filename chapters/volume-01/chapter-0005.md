# Chapter 0005 — One Frame Short

The headache had gone at eighteen minutes to eleven on Thursday morning, in a shop on Lantern Row while Elias waited for a woman to mend the strap of his rucksack, and it had left without so much as letting him know it was going. Eleven hours and a minute, and the last thing he had done about it was write down the time it started, because a cost you cannot time is a cost nobody has to believe in.

That was the part he could not put down. Not the pain. A pain that stopped was a body. A thing that stopped arriving at the end of a sentence you had not finished being told, without a headache in between, was a sentence you had not finished being told.

He had slept three hours. He had dreamed about numbers and could not have told anybody which numbers.

At seven that evening Ivo Pell came in with a toolbag and a paper cup of the machine coffee from the shop and the specific cheerfulness of a man who has been awake for nineteen hours and has decided to be funny about it.

"Systems night," Ivo said.

"Systems night."

"I did a systems day."

"I know."

"I did a systems day and then I did a night, and tomorrow I do a systems day, and on Friday I do a systems day and a controller swap, and I go home at half past midnight and lie in the dark calculating whether Friday was worth it." He dropped the toolbag. He had lost weight since the summer. "So. What are we doing. Give me something with a screen in it."

Elias took the chair by the monitors and put the notebook on the desk, and for a long moment he said nothing, and then he said, "Wendel Case."

Ivo stopped with the cup halfway up.

"Ring anything?"

"No."

"So what's he."

"He was a night porter. He's been in this building nineteen years. He was on the rota, he had a locker in the basement, and eighteen months ago he stopped being on the rota, and his locker is still in the basement, and nobody has taken it out."

Ivo put the cup down. "Elias, has anyone *left*? People leave. There's a difference and it's a very boring difference and it involves a card being handed back and a leaver's form."

"He's not on any list. Not the current one. Not the archive. I read the ring binders behind the fire plan on level two, which I now know is the complete personnel record for this building since 1974, and there is no W. Case anywhere in it, and there are four C-surnames and one of them is Callaghan and one is Crowther and neither of them has ever been a porter."

"Then he left and his paperwork is somewhere else. Municipal. Ward. Agency. You know what this city is like, a man's service record goes three places and one of them is a filing cabinet in a basement in a district office that hasn't had a fire drill since the jubilee."

"Municipal payroll," Elias said. "That's the third place."

---

And here was the thing, sitting on the pinboard in the security room in a clear plastic wallet where it had been every month for as long as Elias had worked here: the monthly remittance statement from the municipal payroll bureau.

Because the Museum did not run its own payroll. Every hourly worker on the books — porters, cleaners, weekend stewards, the two men who do the small electrical work — was administered by the bureau, and at the end of every month the bureau sent a one-page statement of what it had taken and what it had paid, and the guard office pinned it up and reconciled it against the hours sheets, because reconciling it was one of the four things the night post actually consisted of.

Elias got September's down off the board and spread it on the desk, because September was the last statement posted and the last full month, and the one after it would not come until the end of the month.

Two pages, top to bottom, line by line, surname and forename and grade and cost centre and Museum cost centre code. The bureau paid by grade and the grades were on separate lines, so a person took up four lines and a building took up pages. And every single line ended in the same code, which was the thing that made the sheet useful to him: every hourly person in this building carried it. Night porters, cleaners, weekend stewards, the two men who do the small electrical work. Municipal code M.Q.S. 4. Every one of them.

And near the bottom, between a *Clough, R.* and a *Deakin, R.*, on four separate lines:

> CASE, W. — Grade 2 (Porter) — cost centre M.Q.S. 4 — pension std — union subs —
> hours 141.5 — gross —
> CASE, W. — Grade 2 — M.Q.S. 4 — pension std — union subs —
> CASE, W. — Grade 2 — M.Q.S. 4 — pension std — union subs —
> CASE, W. — Grade 2 — M.Q.S. 4 — pension std — union subs —

Pension standard, union subs. Eighteen months of them. Which meant that once a month, for eighteen months, an outside body had taken a pension contribution and a union subscription out of the pay of a man who was called a porter, at this building, and paid it onward to two institutions that would both have written to this Museum in the year if the Museum had ever asked.

Elias put his finger on the line and looked at it for a long time, and then he said the thing out loud in an empty room, because that is how you find out whether you believe something.

"The deduction means he existed. It means money moved. It doesn't mean this building's books ever carried him."

"No," said Ivo. "No, it doesn't."

Because that was the shape of it. The line had been taken out here, in a system this building owned, by a person with a login, in the ordinary way, the way you take a line out of a list when somebody leaves and the leaver's form has gone to the bureau and the bureau has not been told, and nobody has to meet anybody. Two systems that never spoke to each other, and a man's existence living in the one that had not been told, going quietly into two pension schemes and one union, month after month, while the other one got tidier.

Elias had spent two years being paid to be the man who notices. He had never once in two years had to sit with the discovery that the noticing and the having-had-it and the writing-it-down were three completely different activities and that only the first one was his job.

---

The lockers were in the basement under the stair, forty-one of them in two rows, numbered from sixty-one to a hundred and one, and the numbers were on a card by the door that had not been reprinted since the building opened.

He went down with a torch and found it on the second try, because he had looked at the card and there was a gap in it.

Eighty-one. He got the number off the card, not the locker, and he had it before he had the torch on it.

There was a name plate in the brass holder on the door. It was Museum-issue, the same plate as every other one, riveted, and it said **CASE, W.**, and the brass had gone dull round the edge of the letters where a thumb had lifted it off and put it back, a great many times, the way you handle a plate you read every day for nineteen years.

Inside: a hook with the bracket still screwed to the back panel, and a heavy tin of tea, half full, with the lid off, and a comb, and a folded page of the newspaper from a Sunday that was, by the look of it, a long time ago. No coat. Nothing in the door pocket. The tin of tea and the comb and the newspaper and nineteen years, and the door standing open on a hinge that had a spring in it.

It was not locked. It had never been locked. Nothing about that locker had ever required a decision.

Elias stood in the basement under the stair and put the torch into it and looked at a hook and a tin of tea for a while, and then he shut it, and wrote the number on the locker card in his notebook without touching the plate.

*Case, W. — 81.*

He thought about it on the stairs afterwards, and this is the part that frightened him, and it is not the tin of tea: it is that in two years of walking past that card, of reading that card every single night on the way to the door he was about to lock, he had read a gap and had seen a gap, and had seen it the way you see a missing tile.

There had been forty other lockers. There had been a card. There had been a number between two other numbers, and it had been perfectly reasonable to read straight over it, because that is what a card of forty-one numbers is for, and because the numbers either side of it had been there every night for two years and had never changed and were never going to change.

Nothing about the erasure had been hidden from him. That was the discovery, and it sat on the stairs: a person with a login and eighteen months and a legitimate reason had deleted a line from a system, and the deletion had propagated to a payroll terminal and an archive index and a sign-out book, and it had propagated as far as a printed card on a wall, and it had stopped there, and the reason it had stopped there is that a museum does not clear out a dead man's locker unless somebody asks it to, and nobody ever asked it to, because asking would have made a record, and that is the one thing in this building that nobody has ever wanted.

The system had been perfect. The building was not part of the system.

Underneath the personnel columns, on a card index tied into the same rings, there was a decade of posting sheets for the hourly grades. Not a record of anybody. A record of where the Museum had told the bureau to send a man's letters. Grade two, porter, 2001 through 2012, two hundred and eleven lines, and on every single one of them the same address in the same column, in the same twelve words.

> c/o Museum letting, Sillick Lane, Low Ward.

He read it four times standing up. The Museum had rented the mill's porters' row out of the mill's own street and put its own name in front of it for a decade, and the row had gone to tenancies after that, and the tenancies had gone three times over. And somewhere in that street was a man who had a pension and a union subscription coming out of a building four miles from the only address this building had ever written down for him.

---

The corridor camera was the worst of it, and it took him forty minutes to work out why it was the worst of it.

The corridor outside the Quiet Wing had been re-cabled in 2019 and the camera on it was a good one, better than the rest of the building, which made sense, because the wing was restricted. It held thirty-one days. The playback at half brightness on the security room monitor gave him a corridor in grey: a fire door, a radiator, a run of floor, a bracket halfway down the wall that he had never looked at before.

He put the playback at speed and went through the last fourteen nights, and he did it the way he had been trained to do investigations, which was to look for a person rather than for an event. He had done it forty times in two years and it was a thing he was good at: a porter is a shape, a shape is a habit, a habit is a time of night and a place a man cannot help being.

At 01:12, on ten of the last fourteen nights, a man in a dark coat with a lantern came the length of that corridor and turned at the end and went away.

Ten of the fourteen. On two nights there was nothing at all. On two nights more — the seventh night back and the fourteenth night back, both Fridays, both inside the last fortnight — the shape was there, coming down the corridor, and at the moment it should have been fully in frame it was half in frame, and then it was a different half in frame, and the middle was a smear of about a fifth of a second in which the recording had apparently lost its confidence.

Elias wrote the times out. On the seventh: 01:12:41, and then 01:12:44. On the fourteenth night back: 01:12:42, 01:12:45, 01:12:43.

Two nights, five arrivals, the whole of them inside four seconds of one another, and the smear in the same four seconds on both of them and in the same place in the frame. Which meant it was not a man moving badly. It was a camera doing something, on a schedule, at the moment a man walked past the angle that the lens likes least.

Retention artifact. Frame rate dropped for maintenance to fit the buffer. The wall of the corridor at that angle is a white lime wall and the system was doing what systems do to a white wall.

And that was the correct answer, and it was completely true, and it was also the answer that took the man out of the picture twice in two weeks, and Elias sat and looked at it and understood that he did not want the correct answer this time and that wanting it was the whole of his difficulty.

He went and looked at the bracket.

It was halfway down the corridor, at shoulder height, and it was a bracket. Civic pattern, cast iron, the kind they put on river walls and underpasses: a plate with two holes and a stub where something had been. The screws were still in place. Whoever took the bell off it did not bother to take the screws out, because taking out the screws is work, and because a bracket with a bell on it is equipment and equipment belongs to somebody and nobody wanted to be the somebody who threw it away.

No bell on it. No bell on it for years, obviously; it was bare iron with a shadow around the fixing holes.

---

"You know door seven."

Elias had not heard Ivo come up. "I know door seven."

"It dropped again at lunchtime. Twenty seconds, maybe. I was in the shop and the alarm came in on the panel as a fault, not as a door event, which is a thing it has never once done in four years." Ivo sat down heavily. "It's got the old ticket. You know the one. *Reader intermittent, self-recovers, no fault found.* Nine hundred and forty-one days open, I checked. There's a replacement module on order, it went in on the twelfth, it's coming, and I don't have to chase anything because the ticket's open, so I leave it alone."

"How long has it been doing the fault-not-door-event thing?"

"Since the spring. I don't know why. I don't know what the fault is. I get a panel that says *reader fault* and a door that has relocked itself and an hour of my life." He put his head back against the wall. "I keep telling them I need a second afternoon on it and they keep saying the module's coming."

Elias looked at the board. He looked at the board for a while.

"Ivo."

"Go on."

"When it drops tonight — and it's going to drop tonight, it drops about every third night and the night's a Thursday — the leaf relocks inside a second or two, and there is no event, and there is no ticket, and the panel will say *reader fault*, and after that there is nothing on it at all. No start, no restore, nothing. It doesn't get far enough into the conversation to say *door*."

"That's not a fault," Ivo said. "That's a door."

"That's what I'm saying."

"Say it slower, because I'm too tired to be quick."

Elias said it slower.

---

"What I want," he said, "is for you to be standing in that corridor when it drops. Not to do anything. Not to hold it, not to wedge it, not to disable anything, because it isn't disabled, it's *down*, and if it comes back on its own, it comes back on its own. I want you to be standing there, and I want you not to write it up."

Ivo turned his head and looked at him for a moment. Then he sat forward with his elbows on his knees and looked at the floor for a while, and Elias understood that he had made a mistake, and that the mistake was that he had assumed there would be an argument.

"There won't be an event," Ivo said. "Right."

"There won't be an event. You know why there won't be? The door relocks before the controller finishes its handshake. It never gets far enough into the conversation to write anything down, and the half-second when the leaf is standing open is a half-second that happens between two machines and is in no list anywhere." Elias had it now and he had had it for a fortnight and he had not let himself have it. "Which means that if two men stand at that door for a quarter of an hour on a Thursday night, the building will never, in any record, in any system, in any log, in any print, ever be able to show that either of them was on that floor."

"And the fault line is on your shift."

"The fault line is on my shift and I do not care. That is not the point of the conversation and if it were the point I wouldn't be asking you for anything." Elias turned his chair round. "Ivo. If we are caught on this, the story is that the reader faulted. It isn't a lie, it's what the panel said, and if anybody ever asks, I am the one who said it, and I will put my name to it in front of Dael and I will not put yours next to it. I need you to understand that before you agree to it, because I am asking you to put your hands in a corridor on a system that logs doors and not to say so."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. The fans came on a floor up and went off again.

"I want it said out loud," he said. "Not on paper. In this room, now, so that if it goes wrong I can stand in a corridor and repeat the words back to somebody and get the same answer."

Elias looked at him.

"Two conditions, then," Ivo said. "You go first, and you come out first, and you don't touch a single thing in there, and if I say *fault* you are already on your way out and you don't stop to ask why. That's it. I don't care about the door. I care about the ten minutes afterwards, when I'm standing in a corridor on my own with a fault on the panel that I've agreed not to write down, and a fault on a panel is the only evidence there is, and it evaporates at the end of the shift."

"Said out loud," Elias said. "In this room. Agreed."

"Twenty-two fourteen, then. That one's due about now. That one's actually due, look at the panel, she's off the top."

He was already on his feet and had the toolbag over his shoulder when he said it.

---

It was off the top.

Elias was at the panel, because that is where the fault announces itself and he did not want to be coming down a stair when it did. Ivo came up from the shop four minutes behind him and got as far as the fire door. At 22:14 the reader on door seven went to a red X and then to a yellow box, and the two of them went down the corridor together to have a look at a door, which is what two men do when a door stops working.

Eleven minutes, twenty-two fourteen to twenty-two twenty-five, and the door was shut and stayed shut.

That turned out to be the whole of what he had bought with somebody else's authority. The leaf had relocked a second and a half after the fault and never moved again, and the lamp beside it had gone from red to yellow and stayed yellow, and there was nothing behind that door for the entire eleven minutes except a door.

The first two of them were the worst of his life and the reason was that nothing happened. There was no opening. There was no room. There was a corridor, and the corridor was the corridor he had been staring at on a monitor for a fortnight, and now he was in it, and it was completely ordinary.

A fire door at the near end with a push bar. A cold radiator halfway along, the kind with the old bolted sections and a valve that had been painted over. A floor of grey terrazzo with a worn track down the middle where nineteen years of a hundred and forty-one pairs of boots a month had gone. A fire extinguisher on a bracket. A notice about the wing's access policy that he had read in the handbook.

And the bracket. Two holes, a stub, four screws, the shadow of a bell.

He stood in front of the bracket for a while. He could not have told anyone what he expected. A note. A bell. Something that would have made the eleven minutes worth breaking a rule for.

There was a bracket.

At 22:25 the yellow lamp went green by itself and nobody had touched it, and the door had never been open. Elias was out through the service door and up to the third landing before Ivo had finished putting his toolbag down, and Ivo came up forty seconds later, and neither of them said anything for about a minute.

"Right," Ivo said finally.

"Right."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"That was the stupidest eleven minutes of my working life and I'm going to be a systems technician for forty years and I will still be able to tell you exactly which minute I stood at that bracket."

He went to the panel. He looked at it, and the panel said *reader fault* in a yellow box, and the yellow box was the whole of the record.

Ivo did not open a ticket. He went into the event history for door 7, and there was nothing in it for tonight at all. Nothing. Not a fault line, not a start, not a restore, not a log. The controller had given up on the conversation a second and a half in, and everything after that had happened in a gap between two pieces of hardware that nothing in this building can see into.

He scrolled back. March. February. The history on that door was about nine entries in three years, and every one of them was a *restored* with no *started* in front of it.

"See that," Ivo said. "See what that is. That's the whole of what this door has ever said about itself. Things coming back. Nothing ever leaving." He closed the history and did not add a line, and Elias watched him not add a line and understood that he had just bought the only thing in this building that could not be taken back: somebody else's silence, given freely.

And then he understood the other half of it, which was worse. The silence had cost Ivo nothing at all, because there was nothing in the system to suppress. The silence was free. It would go on being free for as long as the door kept failing to finish its handshake, and Elias had been reading a free silence as a favour all night.

"There's no event," Ivo said, half to himself. "There's never any event."

"No," Elias said. "There never is. And that is not a gap in the system, Ivo. That is the design. Somebody, a long time ago, made a door that cannot say a word about a man standing in front of it, and they made it do that on purpose, and I don't know why, and I don't think anybody who did it to it knew either."

And then he went back to the desk and pulled the Hall up on the monitor, because there was a locker card in the basement that he had read over every night for two years without ever once reading, and a corridor on a screen that he had looked at forty times without looking at, and he was going to go back to that desk and sit in front of it with the only three things he actually had, which were three times in a notebook.

---

Because there was one more thing he had been carrying around since Tuesday, and it was a stupid thing, and it was the only thing he had.

Three times. 02:52, 20:55, 23:40. Three events in two nights, and the only theory those three supported was that it was happening more often, and more often is not a theory a man can take to anybody. But if it were on a cycle — if it were, say, every two hours and twenty minutes, or every five hours, or at some point every night — then it was a cycle, and a cycle could be written down, and a thing that can be written down can be handed to somebody.

He had a notebook with three times in it. He had a monitor and a swivel stand and a locked gallery he had a key to, and the rest of the night in front of him with nothing at all in it.

He put the monitor on plinth thirty-four at 23:40 and watched it. Nothing. He had not been watching at 23:40 on either of the two nights it had happened, and noticing that was a thing to do and not to do.

23:52. Nothing.

23:58. Nothing. He noted the time in the column, because a null result is a result, and the trouble with null results is that nobody ever believes them.

00:03. Nothing.

At 00:04 he wrote in the notebook, in capitals, in the flat block hand he used for things he wanted to be able to find again:

> NO SOUND, THU

And he shut the notebook, and leaned back in the chair, and turned his head, because at 00:05 the bell on plinth thirty-four gave its note.

The case was in the frame the whole time. He had not touched it. The case had a grey rectangle in it and a green telltale at the base and a seal Elias had checked at the start of the watch and could see across the room, and the note came out of it, and the hall did not get quiet first, and the hall did not get quiet after.

He never got an answer to that one.

That was the honest position and he held it for a week afterwards, and it is worth being exact about how he held it, because the shape of it is the shape of his week. He had three times in a notebook and no schedule. He had sat in front of a monitor on plinth thirty-four for twenty-five minutes and it had not come, and then he had written a null result in capitals, which is a thing you do to be fair to the record, and less than a minute after he had shut the notebook it had made a noise with the case in frame and nobody near it.

It could have been late. It could have been that a thing which only happens when you are not looking is a thing that happens when you are not looking, and there is no way on this earth to write that down in a column headed *time / location / observation / action* without inventing a reason a thing happened when the reason is that you wanted it to. It could equally have been coincidence, and he wrote that down too, and did not believe it, and left it in the notebook anyway because a notebook with only the conclusions you like in it is a list of wishes.

So the two possibilities sat side by side in him for the rest of the night, and neither of them was going to be resolved by a man with a swivel chair, and both of them had exactly the same consequence for Monday morning: there is no schedule to log. A schedule could be handed to somebody. This cannot, and a thing that cannot be written down cannot be reported, and that is the whole of his problem.

Seven minutes. He had seven minutes of the night in front of him and a notebook in his coat, and the only thing he wanted to put in it was the reason, and there was no column for that.

He wrote, at 00:12, on the line under the capitals:

> 00:05 Thu — 4th. First one I have sat and waited for. I cannot show anyone that these are connected. A timetable could be handed to somebody and a man could be shown it. This is not a timetable and I cannot show it to myself.

Then he sat for another three minutes, which he did not log, and thought about the other thing.

A man can be present, and paid, and unlisted for a year and a half. He can have a coat hook and a tin of tea in a basement in this building and be in a corridor at ten past one in the morning for ten nights out of a fortnight, and everybody who looked at the rota saw nothing, because the rota was *right*. The rota was completely right. The rota was a beautiful document. Somebody had gone into a system with a login and taken a line out of it, and after that the only true document in the entire building was the one that had been emptied.

Elias sat on the other side of his Wednesday-night fear, and found that it had a face and a shape and a name and a locker number, and that he could do something about it, and that the thing he could not do anything about was the fact that he had got here by taking a phone out of a tray in the middle of the night.
