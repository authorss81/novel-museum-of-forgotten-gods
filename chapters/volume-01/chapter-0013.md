# Chapter 0013 — The Fifth Card

He had eight photographs on a telephone and nowhere in the world to put them, and on Tuesday morning he understood that this was the same problem as the ninety-four, only quieter, and that he had been treating it as an administrative matter for nine days because it was one.

He had four of the erased card.

He had four more that he had taken on Monday night, in a shuttered gallery, standing up, with a scale rule off the desk by the door and a torch in his teeth: the drawer, open, with the eleven sleeves standing up in it and the fifth sleeve forward, and the card in the sleeve, and the object in the gap where the card had been, and the whole of it lit and in place.

He had not known he was going to take them. That was the part he could not get round for two days. He had gone into a barred room at ten at night with a bad card made out of a duty roster, and on his way out he had thought: somebody has to be able to look at where it was, and the tape measures everything except where it was.

The eight photographs were on a telephone in an inside pocket, and a telephone in a building that had a printed line about photographs nailed to a wall, and they were the only copy of the only surviving evidence that a person had been taken off a card in this building, and the man who owned them was barred from four of the five floors it was stored on.

At twenty past eight on the Tuesday morning, in the loading bay, with the bay door up on its chain and the rain coming in under it and the wind trying it, Ivo Pell came down the back stair in his systems-day coat over his night-shift shirt and sat on the edge of the desk with his boots swinging, which he did when he was too tired to stand.

"I've been up since ten o'clock last night," he said.

"You're on at seven."

"I'm on at seven." He put his head back against the frame of the shutter and shut his eyes. "So I did the inventory. Seven o'clock, systems office, four of us, forty minutes. And I want to tell you about it and then I want to go and lie down, and I want you to understand that telling you about it is the only thing I did today that I chose to do."

"Go on."

"Forty minutes. Six open items, a bin count, a fire test schedule, and a systems index of about four hundred items. It is the best work I have ever done and nobody in that room cared about one line of it, and I knew they wouldn't, and I did it anyway, because I made the presentation." He opened his eyes. "And here is the line. The schedule of collections, the document that says what this building holds and what it's insured against — it is empty. Nothing since the eleventh of September. It's the spine of the whole inventory and there is nothing on it. The Registrar told me on Wednesday it's with the assessor. Which means the Registry has it, and the Registry's register is a better record of this collection than ours is, and our collection is nine floors above a river and a stairwell away from two thousand people a day."

"Your eight weeks and the one document that mattered."

"Eight weeks for a bin count." He said it without any self-pity at all, which was worse. "And that's the good news. The bad news is the fire schedule, and you'll like the fire schedule."

"Tell me."

"Third compartment, west range, the Hall and the Gallery, four-hour rated, in date to March. That's been in date since 2011. And under it, a new line, in a different hand, dated the twelfth: *Schedule 12 carried objects in the west range are within a rated compartment; in date to 31 March next year.*" Ivo got off the desk and stood up, which took him longer than it should have. "Elias, they have taken the bell out of a public gallery and put it in a cage in a service corridor in the west range. They've moved it *into* the compartment. Containment just got measurably better. My compartmentation note just got better. The renewal just got better. In April this building is insured for more money than it was insured for in October, and the reason it is insured for more money is that a woman with a satchel stood in a room for five hours and watched a plinth lifted."

Elias looked out at the rain.

"Which is better," Ivo said, "for everybody except the claim."

"Yes."

"And for the claim it's worse, and here's the bit I can't get round, and I have been trying to get round it since half past seven this morning." He was looking at the desk, not at Elias. "I asked the assessor — no. I asked the H&S officer, who was in the room, and I asked her, straight out, whether moving a Schedule 12 carried object into a service alcove had any effect on the status of the objects' documentation, and do you know what she said?"

"What?"

"She said, *I wouldn't know, love, that's the Registrar's.*" Ivo laughed, one syllable. "Four words, in a meeting, with the whole thing on the table. And she's right. She's completely right. And that means the answer to whether the wage sheets in a box nine bays from a lamp are still wage sheets, or whether they've become papers in a carried object, is now held by a woman in Northglass who will never in her life open a box of a textile mill's accounts to find out."

He was quiet for a moment.

"They've made it *safe*, and safe is the same word as *closed*, and I don't think anybody in this building understands that yet, and I'm twenty-five and I've just worked it out over a bin count."

---

He spent the morning on the desk and did not get anywhere with the photographs, and the reason he did not get anywhere is that he worked out, over about four hours, with a pencil and a sheet of the back of a dead letter, exactly what he was entitled to do, and the list was as follows.

He could not put them in a Museum file. He had no signature, no post, and no right of access, and the only signature he had ever put to anything in this building in the last nine days was a false one.

He could not give them to Mara. That one took him the longest and it was the truest, and the reason it was the truest is that Mara was the person he had promised. He had promised to tell her everything, every time, and he had broken it, and the cheapest way for a man to appear to have stopped breaking a promise is to hand over the thing he withheld, and he understood that perfectly well and he did not do it, because handing over the photographs would have made the promise look paid, and it was not paid, and the cleaner's name was still in his coat.

He could not give them to Ivo, and this was not allowed, and he knew exactly why not: Ivo had a systems inventory to present for the last time on Wednesday and a name of his own that was on a fault report, and a photograph in Ivo's flat is a photograph that belongs to one man.

He could not simply keep them. That was the thing he had been doing for nine days and it had a price, and the price was a number in a coat and a name in a coat and a card a half-millimetre out of true in a drawer, and it had got him a suspension and a 1974 carbon receipt.

And he could not give them to the police, because there was no crime he could name in a sentence, and a man who goes to a police station with four photographs of a card with somebody's name rubbed off it is a man who is told, kindly, that there is nothing anybody can do.

So he wrote, on the back of a dead letter, a list of four, and then he crossed out three of them, and then he did not cross them out, because crossing them out was a way of not having considered them.

> Mara — no. Paid, not paid.
> Ivo — not allowed.
> Police — nothing to name.
> Nobody.

And then he sat with *nobody* for about a quarter of an hour, which was the most honest quarter of an hour of his week, and then he did the thing he should have done on Thursday, which was to go and ask somebody who knew more about the law of it than he did.

The truth was that he was not the owner of the photographs in any sense a court would recognise. He had taken them in a room he had no right to be in, at two in the morning, with a telephone he had signed out of a tray in his own name, in a building with a printed rule about exactly that, and the only way to turn them into evidence was to give them to somebody with authority, together with an account of how they had been obtained, and the account was going to be ruinous to him and to a woman in a label room who had said she heard the drawer.

And that was the point. That was the entire point, and he got to it at about a quarter past eleven with a headache starting behind the same eye, and the point was that an account was a thing he could give.

---

The label room is on the first floor, off the conservation corridor, and it has no windows, and it is entered by a card and it has a camera on the lintel that covers the doorway and the card cutter and about a metre of bench, and it does not cover the drawers.

He was not permitted on the conservation floor.

He stood at ten past four in the conservation corridor and asked, and it took forty minutes, and what he was given was a junior guard on his own round standing six feet outside a door with his hands behind his back, and the junior guard's name was not his problem and was not Elias's business, and the junior guard said, once, "I signed the book. I'm stood here. Whatever you're doing, I've got the time on it."

The drawers were all shut.

That was the first thing. Fenn was not in; it was half past four in the afternoon and Fenn was not in, because the label room worked nights. Somebody had been in during the day and had shut every drawer, and the Sillick drawer was not pulled out, and the stack of nine or ten cards was squared up with the top card level with the rest, and the room smelled of card stock and bone dust and the particular clean cold of a room with no window in it.

And the drawer was square.

Elias stood in front of it for the length of a cigarette he did not smoke and did not put his hand on it. He had put a card back half a millimetre out of true on Thursday night at five past two in the morning, on purpose, in the dark, and somebody had come into this room since and closed that drawer and squared the stack and taken the signal off it.

Somebody had read his message and answered it.

"Won't half do, will it," said a voice behind him.

Fenn was at the bench in the far corner with a tray of cut blanks and her sleeves pushed up, and she was fifty-five or so and she had been doing this for a very long time, and she had come in early, which was not in the rota and which she did not comment on, and she had a stack of already-carded objects in front of her and a bone folder and a bottle of glue and the look of a woman who has been waiting since two o'clock for somebody to come and be given something.

"Elias," she said. "Come and look at this properly, in the light, and then we'll talk about the other thing."

---

She took the sleeve out of the drawer herself, with two fingers, and put it on the bench under the task light and turned it round so the card was facing him, and then she stepped back and folded her arms.

"Now. Look at the stack. Count it."

There were eleven cards in the drawer. He had put his own back on Thursday, so eleven was right, and there were eleven sleeves and one object and the Sillick drawer.

"Now count from the front."

He counted from the front. One, two, three — the third sleeve was the plinth 34 card, and it was the card he had photographed, and it was the card with a maker's line gone off the back of it and a person's name gone at badly and not gone at all.

"That's the third from the front," Fenn said, "and that's the third from the back, because there are eleven and one of them's the middle. And it's the fifth from the front if you count the blanks, and the blanks are the ones I cut on Wednesday for the hall refit, and they're still in the drawer because the refit got held."

Elias looked at the stack.

"So the card that is in the case on the plinth is the third sleeve," he said, "and I am looking at it."

"You are looking at the card that was in the case when you photographed it. Yes." She let that sit. "And if anybody ever asks you which card that is, and you are a man who has written a great deal in a small notebook this week, then the answer is the fifth sleeve, and the two blanks in front of it are mine, and the four behind it are the Gallery of Unnamed Hands, because the Gallery was re-carded in the spring and they came out of the same run."

"Why are you telling me that?"

"Because you have got that face," Fenn said, "and everybody in this building has got that face when they first come in here, and it is the face of a man who has found something and is about to build a very tidy story out of it, and if I let you build it now then in three years there will be a person in this room who says the card in that drawer was the third from the front, and it will be a lie that a child told a child, and it will be in a Museum file for ever." She came back to the bench. "You wanted a drawer, love. This is a room. There is a difference and I have spent forty years telling it."

He stood there and did not say anything for a moment, and then he said: "The card was out of true."

"I know. I came in at two and it was out of true and I looked at it, and I did not think a great deal about it, because in this room a drawer out of true means a drawer, and a drawer out of true at two in the morning in the small hours means somebody came in and wanted me to know." She picked up the bone folder and turned it over in her hands. "And so I came in early, and I have shut the drawers, and I have squared the stack, and there is no signal on it now, and I am going to tell you what that is. That is me saying that whatever you did, I am not going to help you do it again and I am not going to stop you either, and I would like you to go away and think about how much nicer it would have been if you had asked me."

Elias Venn stood in a windowless room on the first floor of a building he was not permitted to be in, with a junior guard six feet outside the door who had signed a book, and found that he had been told no.

---

"Two things," he said. "And I'm asking, which I should have done on Thursday."

"Go on."

"The drawer. You've had forty years of these come through this room, and the maker's line on the back of that card was taken off by somebody who knew what a foundry catalogue is, and the number on the face was made up, and there's a name under the erasure that isn't the porter's. You know who cut it."

Fenn put the bone folder down.

"I know who cut it," she said. "And I'm not going to tell you."

"Mar—"

"Don't." She held up one finger, and it was the same finger Mara Iles had held up at half past twelve on Saturday afternoon in a different corridor, and Elias nearly laughed and did not. "Now. You are asking me to put a name on a piece of paper. I know exactly what happens to a name that goes on a piece of paper in this building, because I have been cutting cards in this room for forty years and I have watched it happen, and I will tell you what it looks like. It looks like a telephone call. It looks like a person coming in here on a Monday with a folder, being pleasant, and asking me whether I remember a particular afternoon. And then it looks like me remembering, because I do remember every afternoon, I have a good memory for days and a bad one for faces and it is the worst possible combination. And then it looks like a card going into a drawer in this building with a number on it, and that number is the number of the person I have just described, and the number is the only thing anybody will be able to find, and in forty years' time somebody will sit in a corridor and read the number and make a decision about a person I was wrong about in one afternoon in 1987."

"That's not what I—"

"You are asking me to do the thing you have been doing all week, and you have been doing it very well, and I have watched you do it from a doorway." She had picked her glasses off. "There is a woman in this building who has asked for the names of everybody who ever cut a card in this room. There are four of us who could tell her and there are four of us and we do not, and it is not because we are frightened of the woman. It is because a name on a card is a thing that follows a person. It is the only rule in this room. It has been the only rule in this room since before the building was made and it will be the only rule in it after everyone in it is dead, and you are not going to be the one who breaks it, not even for me, and especially not on a Tuesday with a junior guard in the corridor."

"Go and ask the Registrar," she said. "Go and ask her, and she will tell you what she wants to tell you, and it will be nothing, and you will have gone and asked."

And she was right, and he could not go and ask, because he was suspended, and she knew that he could not go and ask, and she had said it anyway, and it was the politest and the cruellest thing that had happened to him since Sunday morning.

He stood there for a second. Then he said: "The second thing."

"The second thing is the telephone in your pocket, and you want me to say I let you in at two o'clock in the morning on Thursday, and I didn't, and I am not going to."

"You did let me in. You told me the drawer was the right one."

"I said, *the Low Ward series are two drawers down on the left, and there's a spare box of the 1902 stock in the bottom if you've run out, and I haven't, so don't take mine.*" She put her glasses back on. "That is the exact sentence, and it is on no recording anywhere, and if you put it in a statement you will have to put it in a statement as *she told me where the drawer was*, because that is what happened, and there is no part of it that was permission and there is no part of it that was anything else."

"Mara would write it down exactly like that."

"Yes," said Fenn, "and that is why she is dangerous and why you are not going to get it out of me, and if you have any sense you will get it out of the Director, in daylight, in a room with a table."

---

He was out of the conservation floor by twenty past five and he stood on the loading-bay desk and looked at eight photographs on a telephone and understood the whole of what he had to do, and it took about a minute, and then he did it.

He went and got a sheet of A4 from the drawer under the desk and wrote it standing up, and it took him a long time, because he had to write it without anybody's help and it was four hundred words long and every word of it was a thing he was admitting.

> **STATEMENT — E. VENN, security, grade 1**
> made Tuesday 13 October, Museum of Quiet Saints, at 17:20, at the loading-bay desk.
>
> On Thursday 6 October, at about 02:05, I took a personal telephone from the numbered tray at the service entrance. I signed the book beside the tray, giving my name, 01:52 out and 02:41 back, and I did not write a destination, because the book has no column for one.
>
> I went to the label room on the first floor and took from the Sillick drawer the spare card for the object displayed on plinth 34 in the Hall of Former Faiths. I photographed that card four times, at four angles, on the personal telephone. I did not photograph the book, the lintel camera, the room or any other person.
>
> On Monday 12 October, at about 22:40, I photographed the same drawer, opened, in place, in the Gallery of Unnamed Hands, with the same telephone. Four exposures. That room is one I am not permitted to enter and I entered it without authority.
>
> The rule printed on the device book is that there is no photography of collections material, labels or records. I broke that rule twice. I was told the drawer was the Sillick drawer by the label-room finisher, whose name I am not giving, because I have not asked her permission and she has not given it. She was not present when I took either set of photographs and she did not open either drawer for me.
>
> I also wrote in the device book on Thursday an entry for the telephone that was not a device-book entry, being the same entry, and I put the card back into the drawer out of true, deliberately, on Thursday. The card is now square and the drawer is shut. That was me and not the finisher.
>
> On Saturday 7 October at about 07:30 I signed a fault report in which the words *access to the Quiet Wing corridor during the outage was not established* were false. I wrote them. My supervisor asked for one line and I chose it.
>
> These photographs are the only copy I have of a person's name erased from a card. They are not in any Museum system. I am giving them up because I am not able to hold them and because holding them is what I did last week.
>
> E. VENN, E. — signed, in my own hand, in the room, at the time above.

He read it twice. Then he wrote two lines at the bottom.

> *If this is evidence it is evidence against me first, and I have written it so that it is.*
>
> *I am not asking anybody to be dismissed. I am asking that the thing in the drawer is not moved again by anybody who is not named on a piece of paper.*

---

He sealed it with a Museum envelope, put the four Thursday photographs in, and wrote on the front of it, in block letters, because block letters are the only form that survives a corridor:

> **E. VENN — SECURITY, GRADE 1 — TO THE BOARD SECRETARY, MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS**
> **NOT TO THE CONSERVATION FLOOR. NOT TO THE REGISTRAR.**

And he wrote a second line, smaller:

> **Enclosure: four photographs, unaltered, taken 6 Oct approx 02:05. Registered post, receipt required.**

And then, at twenty past six, he took it to the duty manager at the service desk, and Priest read the front of it, and read the second line, and then read all of it, standing at the desk with a mug in her hand, and did not interrupt him once, and took about four minutes over it.

"That's the fourth one of these tonight," she said.

"Which one?"

"Dead letters." She put it down. "Elias, I've done this desk for nineteen years. I don't sign for things, I witness them. If you want this entered, I'll write the time in the day book and put my initials against it and that's the whole of what I can do, and it's a real thing, because a day book with a time and two sets of initials in it is a document that has to be answered."

"And the registered post."

"Registered post." Priest wrote **VENN, E. — 18:24** in the day book and initialled it and turned the book round so he could see it. "That'll go in the Monday bag with the audit. Do you want a receipt for it?"

"I want the receipt."

"You'll get the receipt, and the receipt will go to the Board Secretary, and if the Board Secretary opens it in front of somebody then the somebody will remember it, and if nobody opens it for four years then you'll still have it, because a registered post item is a thing that cannot be lost. It's the only kind of record in this building that isn't a piece of paper somebody can decide to re-file." She put the mug down. "Your grandmother had a receipt for a parcel for forty-one years, didn't she."

"My mother."

"Your mother." Priest drew the book towards her and squared it. "Right. Now go away, because I have to do the sweep at eleven and I have not got a great deal of respect for you this week and I am not going to pretend to."

---

He went out the service lane at ten past seven and stood on the pavement in the rain, and the Index put a sentence into him, and it was not a sentence about a bell or a room or a count.

> *A copy has been given to a person who was not named in it.*

It arrived out of nothing, with no wall behind it and no speaker, in a lane behind Civic Hill, in a man who had noticed that the print had gone out of the loading bay office across the road.

It was about the envelope. He was almost certain it was about the envelope, in the way the other ones had been about the thing he was standing next to, and he did not know what it meant, and for the first time in nine days he did not have anywhere to put it, because the one book he had been keeping for things that did not make sense had been the same book he had just spent four hundred words emptying in front of a duty manager.

He wrote nothing. He stood in the rain and let it be unexplained, and noticed that not writing it down was the first thing he had done in nine days that was not a decision.

---

Dael Ordway was in the loading bay at a quarter to nine with his hat off and the day book open in front of him on a sack of frames, and he had read what was in it, and he had come and found him, which he had not had to do.

"Elias."

"Mr Ordway."

"Don't." Dael turned the day book round. "Somebody's read that out. Not you. You wrote it and you handed it to a woman who does not have to sign anything. And that means it's on a Monday bag to the Board Secretary, and the Board Secretary's office is on the fourth floor, and there are four people on the fourth floor, and by Wednesday somebody will have said something in a corridor, and the first thing that will be said will be the word *label*."

"Yes."

"So you have started it." He shut the book. "Do you know what you've started?"

"I know what you've been protecting."

"I've been protecting a *building*." Dael's voice went up half a tone and came straight back down, which was the thing he had watched it do every Friday for two years. "That case is in an insurer's file now, isn't it. Not the Museum's copy. Yours."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"I know exactly what you're talking about, and I want to say this to you once and then I am going to go home." He took the hat off and put it under his arm. "I have a claims manager. I have had a claims manager for four years, her name is Cheryl Vosper, she is at Vail & Thwaite, and she is a claims manager and not a lawyer, and she has a drawer. The Board's got a solicitor and a solicitor can hold a thing for six years. A claims manager cannot. That's the difference, and it's the only reason I'm stood here telling you about it instead of telling you to keep your hands in your pockets."

"You've read it then."

"I've read the front of it and the day book, and the front of it says *not to the Registrar*, and that line is the whole reason I am not going to take the envelope off you." He put the hat back on. "Because you have just been in this building for two hours and worked out that the person who answers for the label room is the one person in it who cannot be got at, and you did it with a rubber stamp and a registered-post receipt, and you are better at this than I have ever been, and that is not a compliment and I want it on the record that I did not say it as one."

He was at the door.

"There's a second thing."

"Yes."

"Your hours on the restriction this week are logged as *scheduled leave in lieu of notice*." Dael said it to the door. "It is not on the paper. It is in the system, and the system is what a payroll clerk reads on the last Thursday of the month, and if it says that, then you are not suspended, you are on leave, and leave is unpaid and un-dismissible. It means I cannot sack you and I cannot pay you and those are the two powers I have got." He stopped. "Which means somebody is going to have to make a decision about you on the fourth Monday, and it will not be me, and it will be the Director, and you should spend the rest of the week understanding what it costs her to make one."

He went out into the rain and shut the door behind him with the care of a man who does not slam doors in a building he is insured for.

Elias stood in the loading bay with the wind coming in under the roller and the dead letters still in a manila envelope on the corner of the desk, and the cage four feet away with the bell in it, and the phrase from the day book, and the sealed envelope in the Monday bag, and he thought, with a clarity that arrived from outside him and that he would have given a great deal to argue with:

*Nobody in this building is protecting the thing. Everyone in this building is protecting the floor they are standing on, and the floors are all different heights, and one of the floors has four drawers' worth of somebody's wages in a box, and every single person in this building has gone to a great deal of trouble to make sure that box stays on the floor it is on.*

And then, because he was a man who could not help it, and because it was the only thing in eight days that had gone right, and because the man who had done it had told him on a stair landing at eight o'clock on a Friday night that if he did not have to ask then it was not a favour:

*And Ivo did it anyway. And I did not ask him.*
