# Chapter 0027 — The Man in the Good Coat

Northglass is four minutes from Civic Hill on a tram and the hearing was at ten o'clock on Wednesday the twenty-eighth of October in a first-floor room above a firm of auctioneers in Cattlemarket, and Elias Venn was not allowed in it, and he was in the building for nine hours.

The claim was not his. The claim was the estate of a firm of solicitors in the same street as the auctioneers, acting for a freehold purchaser, and the mortgagee's solicitors were separately represented and had their own room on the same corridor, and there was a company called Hallowgate Holdings whose entire contribution to the proceedings was to be a name at item four on a schedule of documents, which was where companies keep their names when the thing they own has been emptied.

The claim was about a site. It was about a site that had been cleared on Sunday the twenty-fifth of October and had had a man of seventy-four go into a canal on it on Saturday the seventeenth, and it was brought under a section of an Act that nobody in the building could name and that everybody in the building had read, and its subject, in one line on a printed sheet on a table at the door, was *damage to a leased site and consequential loss, and the works of a third party*.

Elias Venn stood in a corridor from ten o'clock in the morning until about ten past seven in the evening with a folder under his arm that he had not opened, in a building with a fire stair and a lift and a coffee machine that had a sign on it about the price of the coffee, and he was there because a claims manager in Northglass had told him on the nineteenth of October that she was going to have to ask him not to be in a room on this date, and he had said *that's fair*, and she had said *it is not fair, it is my job*, and had then rung him at eight o'clock that morning to say where the room was, in case he came.

He was the last of the witnesses on a list of nineteen.

The first ten were the mill. Eight of them were dead. One of the two who were alive was a woman of eighty-one who had come in a taxi with a niece and had given her evidence for eleven minutes and had been asked four questions, and the other was a man of eighty-two in a nursing home in the north of the city who had given evidence by letter and whose letter had been read out, and whose name was on a *schedule of dependants* and not as a witness, because he had not walked out of a house since 2019 and a company does not put a nursing home in a witness box in a private hearing.

The eleventh witness was Cliff Sowerby. He was seventy-four and he had come out of the hospital on the Monday, five days after a temperature of about thirty-four, and he came on his own feet with a frame, and he gave evidence for about forty minutes, and the man from the freehold purchaser asked him one question, which was: *did you understand at the time that the site was under a clearance programme?* — and Cliff Sowerby said no, and then said, before he was asked anything else, that he had had the pump housing lifted off the slip on the Saturday with a crane his nephew arranged, and that he had a key to the site office because he had had one since 1974, and that he was aware, in the end, that he ought not to have been there, and that he would not be at the site on the fourth of November, and that he was not going to say that he was a trespasser in a room with a tape in it, and if the tape wanted a trespasser it could have one, and he was seventy-four and had been in a canal.

Then the eleventh witness's evidence was over and there were eight left and they came out of the room in ones and twos and stood in the corridor and said things to each other, and Elias Venn listened to all of it, because he was a man on a corridor and men on corridors are the best source in the world.

---

They came out in this order, and the order is the shape of the day.

At half past eleven, a young woman of about twenty-six with a lanyard and a folder came out and telephoned for about ninety seconds and said, to nobody, "No, I can't say that on the phone." At ten past twelve, a man of about fifty in a very good coat came out with a solicitor and said, twice, in a voice that carried about four feet: "The lamp is plant." And then, at half past one, the thing that had been running in the room since the morning came out into the corridor in four words.

"The gate book is a line in a list."

Mr Rhys, of Quill & Frayle, was standing with a document wallet under his arm and he said it to the man from the freehold purchaser and not to Elias Venn, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has said eleven times in eleven weeks.

"It is item three. *Gate book, leaves 1 to 96 present.* It is a line in a list. It is a description of a category of object that came off a site, and it does not identify a document, and it does not state a date, and it does not state who wrote it, and it is not a document. It is an entry in an inventory, and an inventory is evidence that a box existed." He had the wallet open now and he was enjoying himself in the way a man enjoys himself when he has been right for eleven weeks. "And the lamp is item nine. *Lamp, fitter's, electric, navy enamel.* It is a line in a list, on the same sheet, in the same hand, and it is a fitter's lamp, and a fitter's lamp is plant. It passes to the mortgagee and it passes to the purchaser and it is in Northglass or it is on a shelf on Civic Hill and it is a lamp. And a bundle of wage sheets tied with tape and marked pay office is item ten, and a bundle of wage sheets is not plant, and a bundle of wage sheets is nothing under the Act, because a bundle of wage sheets is a document and a document is not lost, it is *mislaid*, and the section does not deal with mislaid things, it deals with damage, and there is no damage to a document that was picked up in good order on a working day by a fitter's mate with a handcart."

"That's a very narrow construction," said the man in the good coat.

"It is an ordinary one. It is the construction a court would take on Friday afternoon without any help from anybody." He shut the wallet. "And it comes down to an address, gentlemen, and it always comes down to an address. It comes down to *who was at the works on the thirtieth of October in 1974*, and there is not one document in this claim that puts anybody at those works on that day. There is a list of objects. There is a receipt. There is a bundle of paper with a man's name on it in a hand that nobody can place, and that name is P. LOACH, and the address under it is Sillick Lane, Low Ward, and that is a *delivery* address written by a solicitor's clerk in Northglass who was writing down what the man said when he asked him where he lived."

"So what happens," said the man in the good coat, "when a company's property is removed from a site in the ordinary course and the company cannot say whose it was?"

"What happens," said Mr Rhys, "is that a compensation claim is not available to a person who cannot connect the object to themselves, and so the claim fails, and so the estate of the freehold purchaser is left holding a site, and the site is clear, and the inventory is in a file, and everybody in this corridor is entirely within their rights and has behaved correctly throughout."

He said that last sentence to Elias Venn, who was four feet away with a folder he had not opened, and Elias Venn understood that he had been looked at and identified and had been told, out loud and in front of a witness, that the entire three weeks of his professional life had been, in the correct opinion of an honest man, a matter for the compensation section of an Act and not for a hearing at all.

That is the whole of what a well-meant procedure does to a claim. It does not have to lie. It only has to be a procedure.

---

Doreen Sarn was in the building all day and was not a witness.

She was at twenty-five past ten with a plastic tumbler and a blue folder and a cardigan over a pinafore, and she was on the claimant's side, and she had every right to be, and she was not called, and she was not in the list of nineteen, and at half past two in the afternoon she said so herself, in a corridor, to a solicitor who had asked her, in a voice with genuine embarrassment in it, whether she wished to be added.

"No," she said. "And I'll tell you why, and then you can put it in your list of reasons."

"The tribunal may—"

"The tribunal has got the list. The tribunal has got a woman of eighty-one in a taxi and a man of seventy-four with a frame and a woman of fifty-four with a folder and a list of nineteen." She shifted the tumbler from one hand to the other. "I'm not a witness, because the second I stand up in that room I become a piece of paper, and I have outlived four institutions in this city that wanted my paperwork, and every single one of them got it, and not one of them ever asked me a question."

The solicitor looked at her for a moment.

"Ma'am, the tribunal would—"

"I know exactly what the tribunal would do. I've been a pay clerk for forty years and I have watched about nine hundred men go in and out of a room about a number, and the number goes in and the man's name goes on the statement and the man's name goes on the schedule of assets and the man's name is in a file in four rooms for fifty years, and about eleven years after he dies somebody writes a book about it and calls him *a name*. I've had that. I've *been* that." She looked down the corridor at Elias Venn, who had been in a front room in Ferry Bank on Wednesday the fourteenth of October and who had, in fourteen days, become a person with a folder on a corridor. "I've got my own copy. It's the only copy. And on the day it's read out in a room, with my name on it, then it's in the world and it belongs to the room. And I'd like it to be read out. But I would like to be in the room when it is, and I would like to be allowed to say things that have nothing to do with a number, and I can't do that as a witness, so I'll be a member of the public."

"Members of the public are not—"

"I'm seventy-eight, love, and I have a bad chest, and if you tell me I'm not a member of the public in my own claim I'll stand in Cattlemarket and shout."

---

The tide was out at four o'clock in the afternoon, which is a thing about a tidal city that nobody who has not worked in one believes, and from the first-floor corridor window at Cattlemarket you could see the river going brown and slow past a quay where a container with a seal on it was standing in a bonded yard about four hundred metres away, on bay four, and had been standing there since Friday.

It had been there since Friday. That was the thing about it. It was a steel box with a seal on it and a bay number chalked on the end, and it would stand there for a fortnight and then a month and then it would be broken open, because a bonded container that is not broken open in ninety days goes to a bonded warehouse and the duty is paid, and nobody was going to break it open in ninety days, and if they did, it would be because somebody had paid for it to be broken open, and nobody in this city was going to pay for that, because the only people who knew what was in it were nine people, and one of them was standing in a corridor holding a folder.

Elias Venn stood at the window and looked at it and worked it out, standing up, the way he worked everything out.

The box had been filled on the Thursday before last. He had watched it: twenty men, a foreman called Upton, two slings, a tag read out loud, the doors shut at twenty past two, a signature, and a form on a clipboard that said *site contents — general, as per schedule*, and the schedule had not been on the site and was not going to be. It had gone over a wall on the Friday morning at nine, on a low-loader, and it had been in the bonded area of a store on a quay since that Friday, five days, and it would be there for a hundred and twenty.

The other thing in that store was four feet away from it and nobody knew. A museum case, oak, plate glass, a lead seal with a number on it, holding a dead fitter's lamp that had been off a plinth in a gallery on Civic Hill by mistake at six o'clock on the Saturday morning, and into bond at a quarter past nine that day, and sealed, and sitting on a shelf because a man in an office had put a destination into a transfer form at seven o'clock that morning and a clerk had written four lines about it and ended in the two words *put away*, and *put away* did not mean a room, it meant a cage, and a cage was a thing you did not touch.

And on the other side of the river, about eleven hundred metres away, on a hill, with its lights on at twenty past four on a Wednesday afternoon in October, was a building he had worked in for six years and was not allowed above the ground floor of, with a room in it that had a small bronze bell in it under a seal, and the classification on that bell said that it was to be displayed and under no circumstances removed from public view by a museum acting alone, and it could not get there from the quay, and it could not go to the quay, and there was a case cover standing against a wall in an office on the ground floor of it that had never been used on anything, and there was a form in a safe in Irving Quay with a blank in it, and there was a woman in Ferry Bank with a hardback cash book with a rubber band on it that was the only copy in the world, and there was a taped conversation on a disc somewhere with his own voice on it saying *ninety-four*, and every one of those things was in a different building in a different part of a city, and not one of them was in the same room.

He wrote it down on the back of the folder he had not opened, at four o'clock, in pencil, with the time, because a fact with no time on it is a fact with no end on it.

---

At half past four a man came up the stairs.

He was seventy-eight or thereabouts, and he was in a good coat, and he had a bad chest, and he had a frame in one hand and a plastic carrier bag in the other, and he came up the stairs of a building in Cattlemarket in Northglass on a Wednesday afternoon in October and asked the woman at the desk, without any preamble at all, whether he was in the right building, because there was a notice in the paper about a mill.

Elias Venn put the folder down.

Doreen Sarn had said, on the fourteenth of October, in a front room in Ferry Bank, that the estate's agent was a firm, that she had never asked its name because she had never had to ask anybody's name at that mill, and that it was a firm in the North End with a man in it who had a bad chest and a good hand.

The man on the stairs had a bad chest and a good coat.

"My name's Neve," he said to the woman at the desk. "Arthur Neve. I was the agent. Ravenscourt and Hale, in the North End — that's gone, it's been gone since ninety-one, my partner died in eighty-eight, and I have got no firm, I have got a bag." He put the bag down on the counter and took out a plastic wallet. "I read the paper five weeks ago. Sunday the eighteenth. The weighbridge." He put a hand flat on the frame of the door. "I've been to see that gate in a photograph about nine times in fifty years and I never once thought I'd see it in a newspaper, and then on the Monday there was a piece about a man going into a canal on a slipway, and I read that and I thought: that's the site. That's *our* site. I was the one who emptied it. I wrote to them on the Tuesday. Nobody answered me, because nobody ever answers anybody, and I have been sitting on that plastic wallet since the Wednesday."

Doreen Sarn came out of a chair by the window at about ten past five and stood and looked at him for a long moment.

"You're the one with the chest," she said.

Arthur Neve turned round.

"Aye."

"From a firm in the North End."

"Ravenscourt and Hale, 6 Northgate Steps, and the chest was a pleurisy in 'sixty-nine and I have had it on and off ever since." He looked at her. "You're Doreen Sarn."

"I am."

"I signed a receipt on your office table on the thirtieth of October in 1974 at about ten past four in the afternoon, and I put the lamp in the van myself, and I did not take a shilling off anybody for any of it, and I have thought about that lamp more than I have thought about most things." He held the plastic wallet up. "And I've brought the other one."

Elias Venn came across the corridor.

"What do you mean, the other one."

"Quill and Frayle were the solicitors. They had the file, because their client was the receiver, and the receiver had a clearance to complete and a schedule to certify, and there was a carbon in their file and a carbon in mine, and the one in mine is the *buyer's* copy." Arthur Neve put the wallet on the window ledge and opened it. "It's the same eleven lines. It's the same van. And it's got my hand on it, and there's a line at the bottom that Quill and Frayle's does not have, and the line says *received, all, subject to weight*, and the word *subject* is the most expensive word in that wallet."

He put a single sheet of paper on the window ledge, and it was fifty years old, and it was a carbon, and it had a list of eleven items on it in a different hand from the Museum's, and a different order, and a number at the bottom.

Elias Venn did not pick it up. He stood four feet from it and read it.

> 3. Gate book — leaves 1–96 present, **with entries to 30 Oct**
> 4. Posted wage sheets, loose, approx. 2 dozen
> 5. Envelope, sealed
> 9. Lamp, fitter's
> 10. Wage sheets, bundle, tape, **pay office**
> 11. Clock, works office, **stopped 05:54**

Elias Venn read the last line twice and then sat down on the windowsill, in a building in Northglass, on the twenty-eighth day of the month, in a coat that had not been dry since the previous Wednesday.

*Stopped 05:54.*

Six minutes to six. The number in the reading. The white enamel dial on the far wall of the pay office with a black hand and a second hand, and a time on it, and it was a time and not a figure, and there had been a second in which he thought it was going to be a number, and it was not a number, it was a clock on a wall in a works office that stopped six minutes to six on the morning of the thirtieth of October in 1974, and the clock is item eleven on a list of eleven in a van in a store on a quay, and somebody's hand had written what time it stopped at, and the hand had not known it was writing evidence, and it had gone into a buyer's carbon and a seller's carbon and a Museum drawer, and it had taken the number out of a bell about six hundred times over, a shift a day for fifty years, and it was sitting in a plastic wallet in Northglass.

"That's the mill's clock," he said. "That's item eleven. *Clock, works office, stopped.* Six minutes to six."

Arthur Neve looked at him.

"How do you know what it says?"

"Because a bell said it," said Elias Venn, in a corridor, in a building in Northglass, at twenty past five on Wednesday the twenty-eighth of October, to a man of seventy-eight, and then heard himself, and did not take it back, and did not say anything else.

---

They put the claim back to five o'clock, and a solicitor came out of the room at ten past seven and found Elias Venn on a corridor with a folder and a name on a list of nineteen and his own name at the bottom of it.

"Mr Venn."

"Yes."

"I'm instructed to object." She had a wig on and a bundle and no expression whatsoever. "I want to say three things and I want them said in front of you and not in a letter, because you will get a letter in about a fortnight and a letter is a document and a document can be re-read forty times and a conversation can only be had once."

"Go ahead."

"First: you are the last name on the list of nineteen, and my client's objections to the admission of your evidence are taken out of time and will be served properly, and I am telling you now so that you are not surprised and so that you do not think it is a surprise, because it is not, it is a list, and there are nineteen names on it and you are number nineteen and there has been a good deal of correspondence about you."

"Then say the reason."

The solicitor glanced down the corridor. There was nobody within twelve feet of them.

"The reason is that you are the person who took the photograph of the card," she said. "And if you give evidence, then the photograph is evidence. And then the drawer is evidence. And then the night you were in that corridor is evidence. And I am not going to sell that to anybody, including you."

Elias Venn stood in a corridor in Northglass and did not say anything for about four seconds.

"You have never seen me," he said.

"I have never seen you."

"You have never seen the photograph."

"I have never seen the photograph. I know that it exists because there is a reference to it in a document that came into my client's file from a claims manager who has been extremely fair to everybody, including my client." She squared the bundle. "And I want to say the second thing, which is that I have read the four hundred words you sent out of a museum on the fourteenth of October, because it is in a file that came in by registered post to a claims address and not to a building, and therefore it is not privileged and it is not subject to anything, and I read it in a taxi."

"And?"

"And it is the reason I am standing here instead of being in there, and it is the reason I read it in a taxi, and I would like you to know that I read it in a taxi because I did not want to have read it in a room where I was going to argue about it." She did not soften it. "You broke yourself first. In writing. In your own hand. To an insurance company. And the effect of that on a case is not a moral effect at all, it is a *probative* one, because a man who has put four hundred words of his own wrongdoing into a file has made himself a witness to his own conduct, and a witness to his own conduct is the only kind of witness this tribunal will take, and so you are the best witness in the building and you are not going to be allowed to be one, and I have had to make that argument to a man who wanted to hear it and I have never had to make it before and I found it extremely unpleasant."

"Third thing?"

"There isn't a third thing."

"There's always a third thing."

"There is a third thing and it is not evidence, so you will not like it." The solicitor put the bundle under her arm. "The hearing has been going since ten o'clock and there have been nineteen names and eight of the dead, and every single person who has gone in there has been a person who *worked at the mill*, and they have all been asked about a number and none of them has been asked a single question about the day. Not one. Two hours, and the only evidence about the morning of the thirtieth of October in that room has been about a wage. And Mr Neve — who is a witness, and who came today of his own accord, and who has brought a carbon that nobody asked him for — has been on my list since half past four this afternoon and he is not on the witness list, because he is not on anyone's list. He came because of a photograph of a weighbridge in a Sunday paper." She looked at the plastic wallet on the window ledge. "That is the third thing. When that carbon is admitted, everything this hearing was about becomes a footnote to it, and the hearing will be over in a morning instead of a year, and it will be over because of a man of seventy-eight with a bag, and not because of anything any of the rest of us did."

He got about as far as a window and understood, in the time it takes to walk to one, that a solicitor had told him the single most useful fact he had received in a month, and had told it to him as a professional obligation to tell the other side, and that this was what the profession actually was, and that the profession was not on his side and had not been on his side at any point since the tenth of October and had been, on two occasions, entirely correct.

Dael Ordway came down the stairs at twenty past seven and did not go out of the front door, and stood at the bottom of them for a moment with his hat in his hand, and Elias Venn had not known he was in the building.

"You've been here all day."

"I've been here since one."

"You were a witness."

"I was a witness at three o'clock for about nine minutes and I gave them the cage and the docket and the paragraph nine letter and the date you all keep putting the clearance on, and I did not mention your name once, because the moment I mention your name in a room with a transcript in it you are a witness in a claim you are not a party to, and you would be, for the rest of your life, a man in a claim he was not a party to." Dael turned the hat over. "And then at about twenty to four a gentleman in a very good suit came out of a room and had a word with me in the corridor, which I am not going to tell you about."

"That's all right."

"That's not all right, it's a matter of record and it will be in a transcript, and you are not to ask me now and you are not to ask me later, and I am telling you now so that you don't." He put the hat on. "It was about money. It was a quantity of money, and it was not a sum offered, and I have been in this business for eleven years and I know the difference, and I am not going to say the figure to you, and if you ask me what the figure was I will tell you that I do not remember it, and it will not be a lie, and we will both know that it is not a lie, and that is the arrangement."

Elias Venn looked at a man of forty-four standing at the bottom of a staircase in Northglass on a Wednesday evening.

"Mr Ordway—"

"Eleven years," said Dael Ordway, "and in about six weeks one of you is going to write a sentence about me on a piece of card and put it in a drawer, and I have been trying since about half past three this afternoon to work out whether I am frightened of that, and I am not, and I am frightened of the thing on the fourth of November, when that cage has been furniture in a bay in a basement for three days already, and the proposal is dead, and the company that wanted it has to start again, and a company that has to start again in the first week of November is handed ten working days it cannot possibly use, and the cage goes back into bay 9 on Friday the first of November, which is furniture for the second time in a month, and Mr Nunn knows that, and I know that, and Mr Nunn is a careful man."

He got to the door.

"And Elias." He stopped. "I have not told Mara Iles that you were not allowed to give evidence today, and Mara Iles will find out on Friday, and Mara Iles is going to want to know why a man's name was on a list of nineteen and not in a room, and I am going to have to write it down."

"You don't have to warn me about that."

"No," said Dael Ordway. "I don't. I am doing it because you did it to me on the twentieth of October at four o'clock in the afternoon, and I have been thinking about it for eight days, and it cost you something to do it and it cost me more to be told." He went out into the rain on a Wednesday evening in Northglass and did not put his hat on for forty feet.

Elias Venn stood in the entrance hall of a building in Cattlemarket until the door shut, and then went out onto the pavement, and looked up the street at a first-floor window with the lights still on in it, and then at the river, and then at a building on a hill on the other side of the water with its lights on as well, and there was a bell in it, and there was a box on a quay, and there was a form in a safe in Irving Quay with a blank in it, and there was a carbon in a plastic wallet in a man's bag in a taxi rank on Cattlemarket with the word *subject* on it, and there was a tape of his own voice in an office on the ground floor of the building on the hill, and none of them was in the same room, and every one of them was a document.

He got the tram back at twenty past seven and worked until nine, because it was Wednesday and there were two hundred and fifty-eight dead letters on a desk behind him, and a clerk's is a slow job, and a clerk's is the only trade he has ever had that has never once required him to be right about anything.
