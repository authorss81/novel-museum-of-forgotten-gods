# Chapter 0041 — The Line in the Gate Book

"Neither of us has done anything about it." He straightened up. "But if a caption is a lie, then a card is a lie. And a card is what we put the object on. And the object has been on plinth thirty-four with a card on it that says a thing about a bell which a man of twenty-eight admitted in a public room is not true, and I wrote four conditions in October at six in the morning, and one of them took that bell off its plinth in a public room for a period that nobody in this city can name, and I would like to know before the end of this month whether I did that because a woman in a laundrette is right about a letter, or because I read a caption and believed it."

Nobody answered him, because there was nothing on the sill to answer with.

"Two minutes," he said. "And then we come off that hardstanding, because it is about to come in again and my shoulder has not been right since a Tuesday in August."

Here is the whole of the injury, and **nobody is to blame**, and that is the sentence.

It was four minutes to one in the morning. It was raining hard enough that the lamp at the gate made a cone about nine feet long and stopped at the edge of it. The hump runs the width of the hardstanding and divides it into two. The kerb is along the side where the concrete was poured badly. The compound road on the far side of the hump is where the cabin is, and there is no light on that side.

Dael Ordway went over the hump.

That is all. He put his left hand out on the iron, which is what a man of forty-four who has done eleven years of nights does automatically and has never once had to think about, and the iron was wet, and the crown is eighteen inches, and his foot came off the near edge, and he went down on his left side and put his hand out late.

The sound was not a crack. That is the thing Elias Venn has said about it more than once, and it is in the notebook, and it is the only entry in a hundred and fifteen pages that he has read back to himself: **it did not sound like a snap. It sounded like a boot coming off a stair.**

Dael Ordway got himself up onto his knees, which took a while and which Elias Venn watched and could not help, and said, "That's the first one," in a completely ordinary voice, and then about four seconds later, in a different one, "That's the first one I've had."

He was in a car at about twenty past one and in a corridor at about half past, and the corridor was outpatients at the city's general hospital, and there were four people in it at that hour and he was the fourth. A nurse took his coat.

He asked, and this is on the page, and Elias Venn wrote it at ten past four in the morning standing up in a car park on the back of the risk assessment sheet: he asked whether the site had anybody on the sheet who knew he was there.

"Two names and a vehicle, and time off's blank because we never wrote it."

"Then write it. Now, while it's in your head. Time on, time off, and if anything happened to me in that compound, you write down in that book that two men were on the site, and the second man's name is on that line, and if anybody ever asks you who they were, you say you don't know — because you won't, and you'd be guessing, and a guess in a gate book is a lie with a date on it."

The man wrote it in, in pen, and initialled it, and gave the page to Elias Venn and not to Dael Ordway, because Dael Ordway was on a trolley by then and had said about four words in four minutes and the last one was *don't*.

The X-ray was at twenty past two.

> **LEFT CLAVICLE — FRACTURE, MID-SHAFT. DISPLACED.**
> **Mr M. GREEN, FRCS (Orth).**
> *Mid-shaft left clavicle fracture following a fall from standing height onto a wet surface in an outdoor compound. Displacement at the fracture site. No surgical indication. Analgesia, sling, district fracture clinic 8 Dec. No chest injury. No head injury. No admission.*

Eight lines, and a surgeon's name, and *following a fall from standing height onto a wet surface in an outdoor compound*, which is a sentence a competent man wrote in about eleven seconds, and which is **more accurate than anything anybody wrote about it afterwards, including the man it happened to.**

**Nobody is to blame.** It is not a formality and it is not the way institutions talk when they have decided to pay something. It is four separate things and every one of them is true on its own.

The hump is a weighbridge, eighteen inches at the crown, and it is in the hazard line of a risk assessment a night supervisor wrote out on a Monday **before anybody went**, and read out in a car at half past eleven, and has not crossed out and is not going to.

The concrete was wet because it had been raining for three hours and it was November.

The first hazard line says *unlit external area, single lamp at gate*, and it was written by a man who was not being ironic.

And Dael Ordway said *two minutes* and then went over a hump he had been looking at for half an hour.

Nobody on that compound pushed anybody. Nobody was told to do anything by anybody. Nobody was on that site who had any business being there except two men and a witness, and each of the three was there on a piece of paper with a tick in box four or a name in a column, and the papers were correct, and the risk assessment is correct, and the gate book is correct, and a man is lying in a bed on the fourth floor of a hospital with a collarbone broken eighteen inches below a shoulder he has used to shift eleven years of nights, and the paperwork that got him there is four pieces of paper and every one of them was right.

And Elias Venn put his own name in the fourth column of a line in a gate book in Low Ward at about twenty past eleven at night, in a hand that leaned, on a line that is in a book a solicitor can produce, and it is his name and not his Director's and not his employer's, and it was the correct thing to do, and the man in the hut asked him whether he wanted it that way, and he said yes.

They were out on the frontage at ten to four with the rain coming down in a way that made the sodium lights look like the ones in a dream, and the gate motor still humming, and Dael Ordway in the back of the car with a sling already on and the blanket collar up, and the sling was on because they had been to a chemist at twenty to three because a man had asked for it in a car.

"Three things," said Elias Venn, "in a minute, because the Book is out at a quarter past and there is nothing on the timetable that says suspended."

"Go on."

"One. That gate book has your name and mine in it and no job, and it says *time on* at twenty-five past eleven and *time off* at twenty to one, because a man wrote it in, in pen, at a quarter to one in the morning, because you asked him to while you were on a trolley." Dael Ordway turned his head against the headrest, which is not a thing you want to do at ten to four. "Two. It says two men were on the site and one of us came off a hump. When Ashby's people take a statement on Friday — and they will, it is a workplace, you are an employee and I am a man who was on it — that book is the first document they have, and it has got your name in it, and not mine."

"Two's right."

"Three. The clearance book. You read it."

"I read it."

"You read *gate hut to compound, one crate, fifty-eight kilos.*"

"I read it."

"Then you know what I am going to do, and I would rather say it here than in a corridor." He put his hands flat on the wheel. "I am going to write a letter to a company that has a fence and a compound and a freehold and a telephone that a human being does not pick up, and it is going to say: *there is a crate on your compound, numbered, fifty-eight kilograms, cleared out of a gate hut on 22 October; the Museum would like to know what is in it, and if the answer is that it is a load of nothing, the Museum will say so in a file and be finished.* And I am posting it on Monday."

"It's Tuesday."

"I'm posting it on Monday and it will arrive on Wednesday, because it is a letter and letters do not arrive on the day they are posted." He did not look round. "And I am not having you send it, and I am not having Mara Iles send it, and I am not having the Director send it, because the Director's name on that letter turns it into a Museum matter about an object under a statutory stay, and it is not that. It is a crate on a compound and a man in overalls remembers moving it."

"Fine. Do it Monday. And you are not to sign it as a matter of the Museum, because that is precisely what I have just spent two and a half hours in a car and a clinic and a chemist's not doing." He shut his eyes for a second and opened them. "And I am not your friend, and I want to be very clear about that, because we are going to be in the same building for another month and because everybody who has looked at this for six weeks has been extremely generous about what this is. I read your suspension and I signed a false cause once and I said a true thing about you to a transfer clerk in a loading bay and I have not said it to you and I am not going to. I do not like what you have done to my gallery. I do not think the object on plinth thirty-four is a bell, and I have never said so to you and I am not going to start at half past four in the morning on a wet road with a broken collarbone. And I would put my name in a gate book beside yours any night of the week, and I could not tell you to your face why those two things are both true, and it took a night in the rain to find out that they are, and I would have liked about ten more minutes on that hardstanding to work it out properly."

"You were on it for twenty minutes."

"I was on it for twenty minutes and the rain got worse at the end." Dael Ordway looked out of the window at a fence. "Go and post your letter on Monday. And let the book be the book."

He was on the front desk of the Museum of Quiet Saints at ten past seven on Tuesday morning the twenty-fourth of November with a hospital parking ticket in his coat and a paper cup of the coffee they sell in the corridor, and there were about nine people in the lobby, and one of them was on the telephone to a bonded store on a quay in Northglass, and one of them was a man of fifty-three going into a lift with a folder, and one of them was a woman of twenty-eight going into the same lift at the same moment and not looking at anybody.

At twenty-five to eight Miss Arkwright telephoned the front desk herself, which she has never done in six years, and said four sentences.

"He's not in the employ of that building and he is telling the truth about it, and that is going to be the only thing that saves this. Hold the pallet line. Properly. Today, on my system, in my hand, with a time and a reference. It is a proper hold now, not a note and not a telephone call, and it holds seventy-two hours, and after seventy-two hours it goes to a manager in an office in Northglass that I have spoken to nine times in six years. So it holds until Wednesday the twenty-sixth at ten to five in the afternoon and not one minute longer, and the collection is at eight on Thursday morning, and that is the arrangement and I have not got a way round it and neither has anybody else."

Then she said: "And the item is not yours. I have been trying since ten to seven to work out a way of saying a thing to a bonded goods clerk in the plainest words available to me, and here it is. The item is not yours. You do not own it, you cannot sign for it, you cannot give an indemnity, and you cannot give an undertaking, and if you turn up at a bonded store on Wednesday morning with a letter off a director you will be turned away by a woman with a headset and she will be right, and I am the woman with the headset. So do not come here on Wednesday with a letter off a director. Come here on Wednesday with a person."

"Who," said Elias Venn.

"I do not know," said Miss Arkwright. "I have six hundred loads a year and I have never once known what was in a bay in my life and I am not going to start, and there is a person in that building who is on nobody's payroll and that is not an answer, that is a man shouting, and I am saying it because I have been up since half past four and because a woman telephoned me at ten past five yesterday morning and asked me whether she could have until Friday to see whether anybody wanted to kill her, and I said no." A pause. "Tell her no was the wrong answer. Tell her it is Wednesday."

The line went.

Elias Venn put the receiver down on a public counter in a public museum at twenty-five to eight on Tuesday the twenty-fourth of November, and the Board was sitting at ten, and a Registry assessor nobody had ever met was coming to the Gallery of Unnamed Hands at two o'clock that afternoon, and the paper merchant's fortnightly collection was at eight the next morning, and he had a notebook in his coat with a page torn out of it and a page torn out of him.

He wrote one line on the back of the hospital parking ticket, because there was nothing else to write on, and it is the only thing on that ticket and it is in the notebook as well:

> **It is not ours. It cannot come here with a letter off a director. It has to come out with a person, and the person has to be somebody this building cannot put a hold on, and I have known who that is since about ten past seven this morning and I have not said it out loud, and I am not going to write it down either, and if I write it down on this ticket then it is a fact on a piece of paper and I will have made a decision in a queue.**
