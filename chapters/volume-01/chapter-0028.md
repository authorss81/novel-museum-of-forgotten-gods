# Chapter 0028 — Somebody Opens the Box

The transfer schedule came down onto the loading-bay desk at nine o'clock on Thursday the twenty-ninth of October, and it was two pages, and it was the correct document, and it was the document that finished the claim's last chance of being a claim about a box.

> **MORROWGATE MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS — MANIFEST TRANSFER SCHEDULE**
> **Matter:** one (1) box, sealed, accessioned **1961/LW/44**, Low Ward series, held in cage 2, west range service corridor (ground floor) under Custody Disc 41 and padlock tag QA/1187.
> **Purpose:** transfer to a quay-side secure store, in bond, pending re-issue to purchaser, on a docket under Standing Instruction 14 clause 1.
> **M. ILES, Senior Conservator and Accession Registrar. 29 October 2024, 08:40.**
> **CUSTODY:** the box stands in cage 2 under Custody Disc 42, cut at 16:00 on Saturday 24 October, the disc it replaces having been off the box and up a tailgate ramp and back again inside thirty minutes, and a disc that has been on a lorry is not a disc.

And under the heading *CONTENTS*, the Registrar had not written *office papers*. She had written eleven lines, and she had written them in the order and in the hand of a carbon of 1974, and under each of them there were three further columns, and the three columns were:

> **NO OPENING** · **CONDITION AS RECEIVED** · **DATE OF COLLECTION: 30 OCT 1974 — LAST WORKING DAY**

> **1.** Weavers' time book
> **2.** Dye-house time book
> **3.** Gate book (leaves 1–96 present)
> **4.** Posted wage sheets, loose, approx. 2 dozen
> **5.** Envelope of loose papers, **sealed, contents not examined**
> **6.** Tea chest
> **7.** Monthly wages book
> **8.** Withdrawn, soiled
> **9.** Lamp, fitter's, electric, navy enamel — *see note: 9 is in Quay 3, Broomhead Reach, in bond, seal intact, under return*
> **10.** Bundle of wage sheets, tied with tape, marked **pay office**
> **11.** Clock, works office, stopped

He read it standing up and then read the third line and the fifth line and the tenth line again, and then he put it down on the desk very carefully, the way you put down something that is about to become a different thing from what it was.

Because the box's own label said *Sillick Mill: administrative papers, disposal*. Eleven inches of card, printed, twenty years old, on a box in a cage, and the box was on a shelf in bay 9 of unprocessed storage from 1974 to October of this year, and the label said *disposal*, and everybody in this building — Mara, the Director, the Director's solicitor, the insurance company, a claims manager in Northglass, and a suspended night guard with a notebook — had treated it for a month as a box of rubbish that a firm of solicitors was going to weigh.

And the correct document, drawn up in the ordinary way by a registrar who was doing her job on a Thursday morning, said in line three **gate book**, and in line ten **bundle of wage sheets, tied with tape, marked pay office**, and in line four **posted wage sheets, loose, approx. 2 dozen**.

You cannot sign a transfer schedule for a box of *administrative papers, disposal*. A receiving party who signs for a gate book and a bundle of wage sheets has signed for a gate book and a bundle of wage sheets, and a receiving party who has signed for a gate book cannot then say that the gate book was nobody's.

"You did this on purpose," he said.

"I did this on Wednesday night," said Mara Iles, "and I have been awake about it since, and I am going to tell you the whole of it because you will otherwise spend a week deciding whether I did it on purpose, and you will get it wrong, and I would rather you got it right."

"Go on."

"Clause one of the standing instruction requires a written docket stating the destination and the date, and requires a countersignature from a person who is not an officer or employee of this Museum. Clause two of the Director's conditions of the fifteenth says that no Schedule 12 carried object shall be removed except on a docket countersigned by a person who is not an officer or employee of this Museum. Neither of those can be satisfied and I know that neither of those can be satisfied and the Director knows and Mr Nunn knows." She put her hand flat on the schedule. "A transfer schedule is not a docket. A transfer schedule is a schedule of contents. And a schedule of contents is a thing you sign for, and I have to draw it up, and I can draw it up in one of two ways. I can draw it up from the box's label, which says *administrative papers, disposal*, which is a description of a category and not of a thing. Or I can draw it up from the receipt in drawer six B, which is a receipt this building made in 1974, in its own hand, on a carbon, in its own office, and which names eleven items."

"And the receipt is right."

"The receipt is *evidence*. It is not right, it is not wrong, it is a document that a person made at the time, and it is the only document in this business that says what came into this building on the seventh of November in 1974, and I am the Registrar of this Museum and it is my duty to schedule what leaves it." She took her hand off the page. "If I schedule it from the box's label, then the box is a box of rubbish and the object is a chattel of the works and Hallowgate's man signs for a box of rubbish and it goes to a quay and nobody ever has to think about it again. And if I schedule it from the receipt, then the box is eleven named objects, and one of them is a gate book, and one of them is a bundle of wage sheets tied with tape and marked pay office, and one of them is an envelope, sealed, contents not examined."

"And the box does not go out on a lorry tomorrow."

"The box does not go out on a lorry tomorrow," said Mara Iles, "because the man who signs for it on Saturday morning is going to read line ten and stop, and he is going to ring me, and I am going to tell him the truth, and he is going to have to decide whether he is the man who took a gate book and a bundle of wage sheets out of a museum, and I do not know what he will decide and neither does he and that is his job and not mine. And whatever he decides, the schedule exists, and it is dated, and it is on the file, and it is signed by me, and it names a gate book."

"You have just made the Museum incapable of disposing of its own box."

"I have just made it impossible for anybody to say that the Museum did not know," she said. "There is a difference and it is the whole of the difference between this building and the last four institutions in this city that wanted Doreen Sarn's paperwork, and it is a difference of about forty years of not being cruel, and I am not going to be sentimental about it either, because what I have done is not an act of courage. It is a piece of administration. It is a schedule. It is what I am for."

---

He found Nunn in the west range corridor at twenty past ten, with a clipboard and a strap wrench and a good coat under a high-visibility one, and the cage was behind the mesh with the box in it and a new red disc on it, because the old disc had been on the cage since the twentieth of October and had been up a tailgate ramp and back on it inside thirty minutes on Saturday morning, and Custody Disc 42, cut at four o'clock on Saturday afternoon by a woman in the post room with a pair of shears, was on it in its place.

"Mr Nunn. I want to ask you a question and I want to ask it properly and I want you to be allowed to say no in about four seconds."

"Go on."

"There's a box in that cage and it's got eleven things in it, and one of them is item five, and item five is an envelope of loose papers, sealed, contents not examined." Elias kept his hands in his pockets. "That envelope is the last physical copy of a wage shortfall sheet made by a woman called Doreen Sarn in 1974, and it has been in a cage for fifty years, and it is the only thing in this building that would tell anybody what was on the pay run."

Nunn did not look at the cage. He looked at Elias, and then at the corridor, and then at his own clipboard.

"I'm going to say no," he said, "and I'm going to say it in about two seconds, and you're not to argue with me, because if you argue with me I'll have to explain myself to a coroner eventually."

"All right."

"A man who does not open a box he has signed for is a man who has signed for a box." Nunn said it the way he said everything, which was as though it had already been settled in a yard in Northglass and he were merely repeating it. "And a man who opens a box he has signed for is a man who has opened a box. And a man who has opened a box has an account of what was in it, and the account is a document, and the document follows him. I've been doing this nineteen years and I've never opened one and I've never been asked to, and I have signed about eleven thousand of them, and I have a clean sheet, and I am not going to have a *conversation about a wage sheet* in a corridor at half past ten in the morning on a Thursday with a man who's suspended and can't be in half the rooms in this building."

"That's fair."

"Is it?"

"It's correct," Elias said. "And I'd say it differently, but not better, and I'd not have thought of it in nineteen years."

Nunn looked at him for a second.

"Different how."

"A man who does not open a box he has signed for has signed for a box. And a man who opens it has opened it. And the only difference between those two men is a pair of scissors and about nine seconds." Elias Venn did not move. "But you're not the only one, Mr Nunn. You're the last one, and there's a reason you're the last one, and the reason is that everybody above you has been *told* not to. The Director's written four conditions. The Registrar's written a schedule. The Director's solicitors wrote a paragraph nine. And your office wrote a line on a transfer form on Saturday morning at seven o'clock saying *put away*. And every single one of those is correct, and every single one of them is a document that says do not open it, and not one of them was written by a person who wanted a wage."

He stopped, because he had about four seconds' worth left and he had already said the thing that was not about the wages.

"Is there anybody here who can open it?"

"Not me and not the Director and not your registrar and not Mr Ordway and not you." Nunn said it without any drama. "That's the whole point of the clause, isn't it. You want me to tell you that the clause is the thing stopping the wage sheet, and it is, and it's the only thing in this building that is stopping it, and if a director had ever written that clause differently, mate, you'd have had a shed full of opened boxes in October and nobody would have the faintest idea what's gone past. I've had eleven thousand sheets signed and I have never known what was in one of them and that is not a failing, that is the job, and the job is only possible because of a piece of paper one of you lot wrote."

He put the clipboard down on the mesh of the cage.

"Wrong answer, though," he said. "You know it's the wrong answer."

"I know it's the wrong answer."

"Right." He picked it up. "Well. That's Friday's problem, then, and it's not going to be my problem, because this box does not move on Friday, does it."

"No," said Elias Venn. "It doesn't move on Friday, or on any day, and it's not going to move on the twenty-ninth, and the twenty-ninth is today, and the proposal in the corridor lapses at the close of business today, and after that the box returns to its last certified condition and its last certified place and a cage in a corridor becomes a piece of furniture."

Nunn stopped.

"Say that again."

"Clause three of the Director's conditions of the fifteenth of October. A proposed disposition not executed within ten working days lapses. The proposal was posted at ten o'clock on the fifteenth. Ten working days off the fifteenth is the twenty-ninth." He looked at the clock at the end of the corridor. "It's just after eleven on the twenty-ninth. The post has been. If anything is to be signed it is signed in the next four hours, and if it is not signed by five o'clock this afternoon then it is furniture, and I am ringing the office at four to tell them the lorry is cancelled, and I would rather ring them at four than at six."

"Christ," said Nunn, and then, after a moment, "Christ, that's brilliant."

"It's not brilliant. It's a clause she wrote in a hurry at six o'clock on a Thursday morning, and she told me about it on the Tuesday in a loading bay and said she was not going to be the woman who said a thing like that out loud in a building before it had happened." Elias Venn looked down the corridor at a handrail that was eight days old and capped at both ends. "It's the only good news anybody has got out of an institution in a month and it came out of a schedule, and by Friday morning a company that has to start again will be starting again in the last week of October, and the cage comes out of this corridor and the schedule is still on the file, and on Wednesday the fourth of November that company will be holding a new docket with a new ten days on it, and there is no version of the next ten working days in which a gate book goes to a quay."

"You've just told me a company's got ten days it can't use."

"I've told you a clause has been read correctly by four people in five days and the fourth one of us has a clipboard."

Nunn put his hat on.

"Right," he said. "Then I'll ring the office at four o'clock and tell them the lorry's cancelled, and I'll tell them the reason, and then I'm going to have a very interesting conversation with a man in a good suit who is going to ask me whether the *conditions* changed, and I'm going to tell him the conditions did not change, and I'm going to enjoy it, and none of that is any of your business." He got to the end of the corridor and turned round. "And I never opened it. And if anybody ever asks me, and somebody will, in about two years, in a room — I never opened it, and it was never in my gift, and it was never anybody's gift in this building except the one woman who's written the schedule."

Mara Iles produced the letter at two o'clock in the afternoon.

She did not produce it out of a drawer. She produced it off a terminal, and she printed it, and she read it out loud in a loading bay with a door shut, and it was four lines long and it had come into the building at ten past nine that morning and it was the answer to a request that had been made on a Sunday in October by this Museum's own registrar, in the margin of a standing instruction, in ballpoint, initialled and dated, forty minutes' work, and she wrote it on a Sunday and she is not an officer and she did not sign it as one.

> **MUNICIPAL PAYROLL BUREAU — REMITTANCES AND DEDUCTIONS**
> *Ref: MPB/R/8841. 29 October 2024.*
> *In reply to the Museum's enquiry of 24 October under clause 7 of Standing Instruction 14 (2024), the bureau confirms that it holds a remittance record for each person administered at cost centre M.Q.S. 4, comprising surname, forename, grade, weekly hours, pension deduction, union subscription, and payment made, for each pay period since the person's first engagement, and that such a record is a record of the person and of money paid and is not a record of the employer.*
> *The bureau's records for individuals who have ceased employment are retained for the period required by its own retention schedule and are not destroyed on the cessation of engagement.*
> *H. LOUND, Chief Clerk, Remittances.*

Elias Venn read the third line three times.

*Is not a record of the employer.*

"Two hundred and six years of filing cabinets and no disposal policy at all," said Mara Iles, "written down on a Thursday morning in about forty seconds by a chief clerk who has never heard of this building, and it is the single most important sentence that anybody outside this case has written since the tenth of October. A person's deductions are the bureau's record. They are not ours. So when a person is not on our books and is on theirs, then it is not that a person has been deleted from a payroll. It is that a person has been deleted from *one* of two payrolls, and the other one is not ours, and the other one does not know, and nobody told the other one, and that is the whole crime and it fits in a sentence."

"You wrote that clause on a Sunday in forty minutes."

"I wrote that clause because a woman in Ferry Bank told me that the day she started recording what a person said to a guard, in a guard's handwriting, she had stopped keeping a record and started keeping a rumour." She squared the printout. "And I have been waiting three weeks to find out whether the clause would do anything, and it has done something, and it has done it by accident, because I asked the bureau for an *account of a record* about a man named Case, and a chief clerk in a municipal office with no interest whatever in the Morrowgate Museum of Quiet Saints wrote me a general rule about who owns a payroll, and a general rule is a thing that applies to more than one man."

---

Doreen Sarn was in the building at half past three on the Thursday afternoon because Elias Venn had telephoned her at two o'clock and had been told, in the first four seconds, what would happen if he asked her to come.

"I don't come to you," she had said. "You come to me, and you come to me with a reason that isn't *I want to show you a letter*."

So she came, because Elias Venn had said, on the telephone, that there was a document from the municipal payroll bureau that said whose record a person's deductions were, and that a sentence in it appeared to answer a question she had been carrying since 1974, and that he wanted to know whether the sentence was any use to her.

She read it at the loading-bay desk. She read it four times, which is what everybody does, and she had her glasses off and cleaned them on her hem, and then she read the third paragraph again with a finger on it.

"**H. Lound.**" She said the initials out loud. "H. Lound. That's a man's name. He's put his initials to it."

"Yes."

"Nobody puts a name on a thing like that." Doreen Sarn took her glasses back on. "A chief clerk in a bureau with a hundred and forty thousand remittances a year doesn't put his initials on a general rule unless somebody upstairs made him, and that means the rule is his and he wrote it, and that means it isn't a form letter, and it means he has thought about it." She put the printout down. "Now. What is this for."

"You said in September that you could never find out whether the sheet the mill sent to the bank was the same sheet you sent yourself, and that neither of you was a liar, and that it had eaten your life." Elias did not put his hands on anything. "Mr Lound's bureau doesn't care about that. But that paragraph says a bureau's record of a person is the bureau's record and not the employer's. And that is the answer to a question you've had for thirty-one years, and it isn't the answer you wanted. It says: the person was in a book, and the book was not the employer's, and the employer's book and the bureau's book disagreed, and both of them were true, and the one that got you was the one that was kept by a person who was not told."

"Go on."

"So the method works," said Elias Venn. "That's all. The method I have been doing since the tenth of October, in a corridor, with a notebook, and getting it wrong every time because I kept it in a coat — the method is that a record is a record and a memory is a memory and you do not add them. H. Lound has just written it down in three lines and he has never met anybody in this case and he does not know that he has done anything, and the thing he has done is that he has made a rule that a payroll clerk can put in front of a tribunal and win on. And you're a payroll clerk."

Doreen Sarn looked at the letter for a while.

"On the basis of a record of that kind," she said slowly, "I will not treat a sealed item as destroyed."

"Ma'am?"

"That's what it means." She squared the printout against the desk. "On the basis of a record of that kind, a sealed item is not a destroyed item. That is the ruling. A bureau's remittance file is a record that says a person existed and money moved, and it survived, and it was never destroyed, and it was never in the employer's gift, and if the employer cannot produce a record, then the employer has not got a destruction — the employer has got a *hole*, and a hole is not evidence of anything except that you have one. And I have been waiting thirty-one years for somebody in an institution to say in writing that a thing not being in a book is not the same as a thing not having happened, and on the seventh of November in 1974 I would have said that to a bank manager and he would have laughed at me, and now there is a chief clerk with a reference number on it." She looked up. "Get me a copy of that. Not a photocopy. Get it to me and I'll put the date on it myself in my own hand and I'll write underneath what I think it means, and that is a document, and a document of mine in my own writing is the only kind I have ever been able to stand behind."

"Then somebody opens the box."

"Somebody opens the box," said Doreen Sarn.

Elias Venn said, "Somebody opens the box."

And they both stopped, because they had said the same sentence at the same time in a loading bay in a building on Civic Hill, and it had a sound to it that neither of them had put there, and it was the sound of a thing that has been avoided for three weeks by four competent people and has now been said out loud twice by two people who were not talking to each other.

Elias Venn wrote the time on the back of the folder. It was 15:41.

"What I am not going to do," he said, "is open it. Not because of the standing instruction and not because of the padlock and not because I'm on a restriction. Because if I open it, then I am a man who opened a sealed item, and I will be a man who opened a sealed item in every document in this city for the rest of my life, and the first person anybody will think of when they ask *who opened the box* is the man with a notebook and a police number and a reading. And that is the last thing in the world this business needs. It is what a coat pocket looks like from the outside."

"Then it isn't you."

"No." He put his hands flat on the desk. "And it isn't a registrar, and it isn't a director, and it isn't a director's solicitor, and it isn't a transfer clerk who signs for things, and it certainly isn't a claims manager in Northglass or a man in a good coat with a bad chest or a solicitor in Cattlemarket, because if a lawyer opens it then it is a lawyer who has opened a box and it is privileged or it is disclosure and it is going to be a fight about the privilege for two years. And it isn't a journalist and it isn't a filmmaker. And it isn't Ivy Case, who has a father in a hospital and a letter she wrote herself, and it isn't Hattie Bramm, who is owed more than anybody in this city and who would be asked about it in a kitchen for the rest of her life."

"Who, then."

He did not answer for about four seconds, and the reason he did not answer was that there was one answer and it was the worst one, and the answer was *her*, and everybody in the room knew it, and it was the reason he had not said it.

Mara Iles said it from the doorway.

"Her," she said. "And not in a room with a camera in it, and not in a corridor, and not in an office in this building, and not on a Saturday. And not with me and not with you and not with Mr Nunn." She had a folder under her arm. "And not because she has the right to see it. She has got no right to see it whatever. She has got a *claim* on it, and a claim is not a right of access, and the reason the form she is being asked to sign says *may be left blank* is that the same principle applies to a sealed envelope, and I have thought about this for three days and I have not found an argument on the other side and I am not going to look for one."

"What argument," said Doreen Sarn.

"The argument on the other side is that she is seventy-eight and it is her handwriting and she should be in the room." Mara Iles came the rest of the way into the office. "And the answer to that argument is that she has been in a room about a document of her own making once in her life, in 1974, in an office with a gas fire, and the man she asked was *you'll have to ask the clerk for that*, and she has been the only copy ever since. If she is in a room when that envelope is opened, then the last forty years of her life are justified in front of a camera. And the envelope is a wage sheet, and the wage sheet is a piece of paper, and the paper is not the point. The paper is the excuse."

Nobody said anything for about a minute.

"She has to be in the room," said Doreen Sarn. "And I'll tell you why, and it's got nothing to do with being owed. If it opens without me in it, then the first account of what's in it is somebody else's, and I'll be seventy-eight and I'll have to live in a world where a thing I put my name on was described by a man in a high-visibility vest. And I'd rather be in the room and say *I never touched it* and have that in a document, than not be in the room and have it be true."

"Then it's you and me and a supervisor and two people from the conciliation service and a camera," said Mara Iles, "in a room in Ferry Bank, on a Friday, at four o'clock in the afternoon, with a fire on, because it is her flat and it is not a hearing and there is no procedure, and the conciliation officer will minute it because she minutes everything and because I have asked her to, and Mr Lound's letter will be on the table with my own handwriting on it, and nobody in this building will be in that room, and Mr Nunn will sign a form afterwards saying that the seal on QA/1187 was intact at eight forty-five and intact at four twenty and that he did not open it and did not witness an opening."

"And the lorry on Saturday," said Elias Venn.

"The lorry on Saturday is cancelled, and the proposal has lapsed, and the box is a piece of furniture, and on Wednesday the fourth a company will be holding a new docket that cannot be executed inside ten working days, and the cage comes out of that corridor on Friday because I have told Facilities to take it out and put it in the old frame store in bay 9, where it sat for fifty years, and nobody will think about it again for a fortnight." She put the folder down. "And on the twenty-sixth of November at six o'clock there is a hearing in a room, and a man of seventy-eight with a plastic wallet is going to be a witness, and a retired pay clerk is going to sit in the front as a member of the public. And on the Friday after that there is an envelope open in a woman's own front room in Ferry Bank with a fire on. And those are two different things and they are going to be confused with each other by about eleven people in the first ten minutes, and I am not going to be the person who confuses them. So: not in this building, and not this month, and the Friday after the hearing, and not in a gallery."

She picked the folder up.

"And Mr Venn is not in the room in Ferry Bank. That is not a kindness. If he is in the room, then the room is a man and a lamp, and I have spent a month getting him out of that."

Hattie Bramm was in the loading bay at ten past eight on the evening of the twenty-ninth of October because she had telephoned at six and had said *I'm coming up the lane and I'm not coming to the office, I'm coming to see the box*, and Elias Venn had met her at the roller door with a torch.

She looked at the cage for a while. She put her hand on the mesh and did not say anything for about a minute.

"Friday," she said. "So it's furniture on Friday."

"It's furniture on Friday."

"Right." She took her hand off the mesh. "Then you can have the six."

"The six?"

"I've been carrying a number since March and I've not given it to anybody and I've not given it to you, and I gave you a *queue* in October and a folder in a plastic wallet and a name in the back of a list, and that was the right way round and I want to say so, because in a fortnight's time everybody is going to say I should have led with this." She put the folder down on the loading-bay desk and did not open it. "I can put six of them to a street and a date. That's what I can account for. Six, with a street on them, that I've been to or that a woman in my queue has been to. And I'm not going to tell you what six out of a hundred and forty is, because the moment I do that it stops being six people and it starts being a percentage, and I have spent nine years stopping that."

"Which six."

"I'm not giving you the six. Not today. You'll have the six on the twenty-sixth of November or you'll not have them at all, and that's not a threat, it's a queue, and you're not standing in it yet." She put her hand flat on the folder. "What I'll give you tonight is the rest of the shape, and I want it written down, and I want it written down exactly, because if it's written down it stops being a woman talking."

He got the notebook out and the pencil and wrote the time, 20:12, and then wrote down what she said, and she watched him do it and corrected him twice.

"There are about a hundred and forty people. I'll say about a hundred and forty, and I mean about a hundred and forty, and that is a number I have got from nine years of letters and a folder and a queue, and it is *mine*, and it is not a mill's, and it is not a gate's, and it is not a pay run's, and it is not in any book in this city, and it is a number a woman made up from letters, and if anybody prints it without those words on the same line as it then they have printed a lie."

"Understood."

"And of that hundred and forty: there are nine dead. I know they're dead because I have been to the register and I have paid for copies, and I have got the certificates in the folder, and nine of them went between seventy-four and the present, and there have been two more since Christmas, and every single one of them died broke, and that is not a figure of speech, love, that is what happens, that is what always happens, there is nothing in a person's life that keeps the money coming after there's no job."

"And the other ones."

"Two are in a home. One is in a hospital." She stopped. "And *that* is what I can account for, and no more, and I'm telling you the numbers in that order because if I tell you the hundred and forty first you'll all go quiet, and if I tell you the nine first you'll all be kind at me, and I don't want either."

Elias Venn wrote it down in the flat block hand, with the time, and read it back to her, and she made him change one word.

"'Went between seventy-four and ninety-four' — change that to 'between seventy-four and the present.' I've had two letters this year."

He changed it.

"Right," said Hattie Bramm. "And now the other thing, and then I'm going home on the tram because I've got a queue on Friday and there's a woman coming from Broad Street who hasn't been in since the spring." She took the folder off the desk and did not open it. "The rest of them are a list of names on a piece of paper in a drawer in my front room, and I have had it since the day it happened, and I am not giving it to anybody, and you can all have the rest of it."

"Ma'am—"

"Not the list. Listen to me, because you're going to say *I understand* in about four seconds and then you're going to go and tell a man in a museum that I said I understood." She put the folder under her arm. "I've had that list since the Friday morning. Thirty-one years. It's a piece of paper in a drawer in a flat on Sillick Lane, and it is not in a solicitor's safe and it is not in a museum and it is not in an envelope in Northglass with a stamp on it. It is a piece of paper in a drawer, in a house, where it can get burnt, and I know that, and I have thought about that every single day for thirty-one years, and I have not moved it, and I am not going to move it now, because the day I move it, it belongs to somebody."

She got to the roller door and stopped.

"You've all got a copy," she said. "The Museum's got a receipt in a drawer and a box in a store and a schedule. The woman's got a cash book. The man's got a wallet with a carbon in it and a bad chest. You've all got a copy. And nobody's got the hundred and forty, and I have, and it's in a drawer, and the reason I'm not giving it up is not pride and it's not leverage and it is not that I want to be the woman who has it." She opened the roller door and the rain came in. "It's that a list of a hundred and forty names is the only thing in this business that can't be a receipt and can't be an exhibit and can't be a schedule of contents, and the second somebody has it in a file, it stops being a list of people and starts being *evidence*, and then it gets used. That's all. That's the whole of it. That's thirty-one years."

She went out into the service lane and Elias Venn stood at the roller door with the rain coming in and held it for about a minute and a half, and then he shut it, and went and did the dead letters, because it was Thursday and there were two hundred and sixty-one of them and a clerk's is a slow job, and the rain coming in through a roller door a woman has just walked out of is not a thing anybody can make a mistake about.
