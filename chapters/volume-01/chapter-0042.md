# Chapter 0042 — Seventy-Two Hours

The hold went on at nine minutes past nine on Tuesday the twenty-fourth of November and the reference number was **BQ4/2511/L2/04**, and Miss Arkwright read it out over the front desk telephone to Elias Venn and to nobody else, and then she made him repeat the last four figures back to her, because she is a bonded goods clerk and has a procedure.

"You have written *hold* on my sheet in block capitals," she said. "It is a typed field. It has a menu. It says *item, quantity, reference, time, initials, basis*, and you have put one word in a box and a reference and a time and your own initials, and the box called *basis* has got a telephone number in it."

"Yes."

"That is not a basis. That is a man in a shed. I have put *description presented by party, ref CR/1187* in the basis field, because that is what happened, and because the reference on it is a public register number with a date on it and it is in a book in Northglass that anybody may read, and I would rather my basis field had a number a regulator can look up in than a mobile telephone." The line clicked. "And I have written *PART HOLD — pallet, line 2, 2 pallets strapped* in the notes, and the word *part* is doing a lot of work in that line and it is the correct amount of work."

"How long."

"Seventy-two hours, and then it goes to a manager in an office in Northglass that I have spoken to nine times in six years. Seventy-two hours from nine minutes past nine this morning is nine minutes past nine on Friday, and Friday is the day your room is shut, and that is not a coincidence and it is not me being clever, it is seventy-two hours."

"And the collection is at eight tomorrow morning."

"The collection is at eight tomorrow morning and Mowbray will be on the bay at a quarter to eight and Renwick will sign for the weight and they will take everything in bay four except the pallet, and the pallet is two strapped pallets of books and the wage book is on the top one in a sleeve, and it will stay where it is on Friday at nine minutes past nine and it will be referred, and after that it is a manager's decision and I am a clerk." A pause, and then a thing that was said slightly differently from the rest of it. "Elias, the claim on Friday's paper. Tell me honestly whether it went in."

"It did not go in."

"No," said Miss Arkwright. "I did not think it had. It wanted a description and you had not got one and I was the one who told you that and I have thought about it every day since, and I am not going to pretend I did not." The line went quiet for a second. "So the two things are this. You have got a hold with a deadline on it. And you have not got a claim, and a claim had a deadline, and that deadline was Friday the twentieth at five o'clock, and it went, and the four hundred and ninety-four kilos of loose paper in a skip in Northglass was at a pulp mill by the twelfth of this month and there is nobody in this city who can give it back to you, including me, including the Director, and the only person in this business who lost anything on Friday the thirteenth of November is you, and you did it standing on a hardstanding at twenty to nine in the morning with a notebook, and I told you at half past seven that it would not work and I was right and it was not anybody's fault."

"I know."

"You do not know, because nobody has said the other half of it yet and I am going to." Her voice did not change at all. "On Friday the thirteenth of November, a registrar in a museum could not describe her own collection. And on Monday the twenty-third of November, a night officer in a car with a broken man in the back of it got a description out of a weighbridge hut, in the rain, at half past eleven at night, off the back of a kettle. And the two facts are nine days apart and they are the same fact, and it is the only thing I have learned in six years that I did not know in the first one, and I am going to write it in my own book tonight and it will not go anywhere."

The laundrette is on the corner in Low Ward and it is open until ten every night and there are eleven machines and a boiler and a shelf about five feet up above it, and there is one thing on the shelf, and on Tuesday the twenty-fourth of November at about twenty past nine in the evening Elias Venn sat down on the bench by the door, which is where he has sat since he was a boy, and did not get the stepladder.

His mother was folding a sheet on the trolley and did not look round.

"You've told somebody."

"I told Mara Iles at twenty-five to nine on Monday morning, in her office, with the door shut, and she fetched a document out of a file on the fourth floor that I have had in my knowledge for four days, and I gave her a page out of the notebook, and it is a page, and it is one page out of a hundred and fifteen."

"Huh." She put the sheet on the stack. "Is that the growth?"

"No," said Elias Venn. "That's the thing before the growth. The growth is the other hundred and fourteen pages and I have given her a date for them and she has written the date down so that I can't move it, and I did not tell her the date."

"When."

"I don't know."

"Then you didn't give her a date, you gave her the fact that you'd given her a date, which is a lawyer's answer and it's a good one, and I have taught you that." His mother turned both hands over on the trolley. "Now. Are you going to open it?"

"No."

"Say why."

"Because I have not finished," said Elias Venn. "I have not told her the readings. There are four of them in there and a corridor and four seconds of a name I cannot produce, and I have had a hundred and fifteen pages in a coat for six weeks and the reason I have not handed them over is not that I don't trust her, it is that a hundred and fourteen pages of it are work and a Museum record, and about a dozen of it is me and is not, and if I hand over the whole book in one go on a Friday afternoon then it becomes a thing that happened, and it stops being two books. It has to be two books, in that order, in a room, and the second one is going to be the hardest thing I have done since the sixth of October and it is going to cost me nothing at all, and I am saving it up."

"That's a very good answer and it's half true."

"The half that's not true is that I'm frightened of what she'd do with it, and she wouldn't do anything with it, and she said so to my face on Monday, and she said she'd put it in a drawer and not read it in front of me, and she put a receipt on the back of it and initialled it, so I've got a receipt."

"Then I can't say any more about that." She turned a machine round. "You going to tell me what page it was?"

"No."

"Was it the one with the two sentences on it?"

Elias Venn sat on a bench in a laundrette in Low Ward and looked at the front of a machine with a woman in it going round and round.

"You don't know there's two sentences on it," he said.

"I don't know anything, love," said his mother, "I know you've been not-telling me things for six weeks and you've been not-telling me in a different way since Monday, and there's a difference, and I can hear it from here. And I'm not asking, and I have got about eight years of asking left and I'm going to spend them on things I haven't thought of yet."

She started the machines down, one at a time, at ten, which she does at ten, and which she has done every night for about thirty years, and which he has watched about two thousand times.

At five to ten, with the last machine still going behind her and the boiler ticking, she said one more thing, and she said it to the machine, not to him.

"There's a man in a sling at number nineteen who's got a broken collarbone and a police file and an accident book and four pieces of paper, and there's a woman of seventy-eight who's been asked for a form four times this year and has said no four times, and there's a man in a bed somewhere with a cloth in his pocket that nobody will tell him about, and there's a woman of fifty-four who's been in your building twice and has never once been asked for anything by a person who's paid to ask her." She shut the last machine off, and the noise went, and the room got very quiet, which it always does. "You've spent six weeks being the only one who can get in a room. And there's four people in this city who could get in a room tomorrow and can't get in anything, and none of them can get in anything because nobody's asked them. And you know that, because you've written it in that book of yours, and that's what item seven is."

He did not answer that, and she did not require it, and it is the last thing she said to him in November.
