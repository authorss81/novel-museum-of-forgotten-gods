Spence has done since the twelfth of October and which nobody has asked her to stop.

**The inquiry was opened on Tuesday the first of December 2024 and it is the fourth thing that has happened to this building in six weeks and the first one that anybody in it had asked for.**

It is not a disciplinary inquiry. It is not a hearing under the standing instruction. It is a Board sub-group with a chair, a terms of reference, a standing invitation to the conciliation service, and a power to require any person to produce a document, and it exists because of what a registrar said in a room on Friday morning in front of eighty people.

Its terms of reference are two pages and item one is a door.

> **BOARD SUB-GROUP (INQUIRY) — INQUIRY INTO THE HALL OF FORMER FAITHS AND THE 1974 ACQUISITION OF 1961/LW/41–52. Terms of reference, 1 Dec 2024.**
> **1. The unlisted room at the east end of the Quiet Wing corridor, level 4.** *The room is shut and has been shut for eighteen years. A work authorisation dated 2 November 2006, unsigned by any counter-signature and marked not completed, instructed that its door be made good, sealed and re-hanged, that a secondary latch be fitted to the corridor side, and that the primary latch be left engaged. A card-access instruction of 11 October 2006 to which that authorisation is attached bears a typed name at its foot, and the sub-group has established that no officer, employee or former employee of this Museum has ever borne the name at the foot of that instruction, and that the two initials on the authorisation are the initials of a person on the Museum's fixed-positions file of 16 September 2024, in a grade, on a payroll. On 27 November 2024 at 12:04 the secondary latch on that door released itself. The door has not been opened and has not been touched.*
> **The room is not to be opened by the sub-group. Item 1 is reserved to the Director, as the first act of this inquiry, on the morning the Hall of Former Faiths reopens to the public. The sub-group has resolved that it does not need it and has asked not to be given it.**
> **2.** The series *Sillick Mill: administrative papers, disposed*, accessioned 1974, and the sheet accessioned under it.
> **3.** The number of Schedule 12 carried objects displayed in this Museum's public rooms, on open plinths, with a rope line, and the mechanism by which that provision operates.
> **4.** The Museum's acquisition record, its schedule of collections, and the circumstances in which that schedule has been empty since 11 September 2024.
> **5.** The night incident log of 18 October 2006, and the structural-accident file of the same month.
> **6.** Any other matter the chair directs.

Item five is the one that broke the fiction, and it broke it on the first afternoon, and it broke it because of a phrase.

The Casket file is four pages. It has a date and a shift and a structural surveyor's letter, and the letter says the failure was in a floor, and the whole of it is a building losing a piece of itself on a Wednesday night in October 2006 in a way that four men on the floor lived through and about nine hundred people did not.

**There is no damage record anywhere in that file for the fourth level.** There is no damage record for the fourth level because there was no damage on the fourth level, and a sub-group member found that out on the first afternoon by asking a question that cost her eleven minutes, and she was **Mrs Angela Vessey**, who is sixty-four and has a right of access to Board papers and reads headings.

"There is a work order on the fourth level three weeks after the incident," she said, "for a door, and it is not countersigned, and it is marked not completed, and a man closed it on the third of November and wrote *bracket not re-sited, lamp not returned, left as found*, and the word *left as found* means he went to a door expecting a job and found it already done, and the person who had done it was not on the paperwork, because the paperwork says *leave the primary latch engaged*." She put the authorisation down on the table. "And the file in front of you says a wall came down. If a wall came down on the fourth level there would be a damage record, a contractor's report, a make-safe, and a door somewhere would have had to be secured. There is not one. **There was no structural event on the fourth level of this building.** And that means the file of the eighteenth of October 2006 is not a description of a structural accident. It is a description of a thing that was covered up, and the covering-up is a document, and the document is four pages long and it is signed by a man who was not on a shift that night."

Nobody in the room argued with her and nobody needed to, and the minute records that the sub-group **resolved that the file of the structural accident of 18 October 2006 is not a record of a structural accident and is to be treated as a record of a concealment, and that the concealment is the matter now before the sub-group**, and that the file is preserved unaltered and is not to be amended, and that a copy of it goes to the conciliation service and a copy goes to the office of the Quiet Accord Registry and **a copy goes to the family of every person on the 2006 night roster, of whom there are four, and two of whom have been traced in eleven days and one of whom is alive and living in Low Ward and one of whom is not.**

It is not Wendel Case's family. It is a different family, and it is on a list of four names, and Elias Venn has read it exactly once and has not written it down, and it is not his to write down.

And the second thing the sub-group did on the first afternoon was to ask the Director to say, in the record, what she had assumed for eighteen years.

"I assumed," said Anja Ward, "that the name on the face of that instruction was a contractor's signatory. I have assumed it since October 2006. I have never read the attachment, because in nineteen years nobody has ever asked me to, and because a Director's access to a card-access instruction is a right and not a duty, and I have about four hundred rights in this building and about nine thousand duties and I have not once in nineteen years got to the bottom of the four hundredth one."

"And is that assumption correct."

"No," said the Director. "It is not. It was never correct. A contractor's signatory would not be on an employee list, and I knew that when I wrote the assumption down, and it is exactly why the assumption looked right on the paper. I said so to a member of my staff in a corridor in October and he went away and looked and came back and told me he had found nothing, which was true, and it did not help me at all, and I believed him, and I was wrong, and the reason I was wrong is that the assumption did the job it was built to do, which was to stop a director from opening a file." She squared the minutes. "I would like it recorded that I built that assumption on purpose, in about nine seconds, in a corridor, in October, and that I have not forgotten doing it."

He is not a friend of Elias Venn and he is not going to become one. He came to the inquiry on the fourth of December, on a crutch, with a plastic folder, and he was a witness for two hours and ten minutes, and what he gave the inquiry was this.

He gave them the night log and the amendment and said that he had written the first version at about ten past seven that night and the second at about twenty to eight and that he had been in the building for nineteen hours when he wrote it, and that a log entry is read by a Director and an auditor and sometimes an insurer, and that *readings taken in the Hall of Former Faiths* on a Saturday in November in a building with a public tour in the diary would have been read by about nine people by Monday, and that he had not decided to conceal anything. He had decided that a sentence in a log that described a public session with fifty-one people in it would be a sentence that got him into a room with a lawyer in it, and that he had a liability position in a transcript in Northglass that he could not carry by himself.

Then he said, and this is minute 4.6:

"And I want it in that this is the second time I have said that. I said it first in a loading bay in this building on the thirteenth of November to Mr Nunn, a transfer clerk, because he is the only person in this building I have ever been able to say anything to, and I did not say it to the member of staff it was about, and I have not said it to him, and I am not going to, and I want the sub-group to have it because Mr Nunn carried it down two flights and gave it to him and the two of us have not discussed it and are not going to."

Nobody in the room asked Dael Ordway a question about Elias Venn. There was not one. It is the only thing in four days of inquiry that nobody asked, and it is in the minute that nobody asked it, because the minute records who said what, and the absence of a question is legible on a page.

And then he produced the two pieces of paper that saved him, and he produced them himself, unasked, in his own working capacity, out of a plastic folder, and asked for it to be minuted that way, which is a phrase he has heard somebody else use in a room in October and has never mentioned that he has heard it.

The one-page risk assessment, dated the twenty-third of November, hazard line three: *cast-iron weighbridge, 18 in. hump, kerb at side. Trip and fall hazard.*

**The families were sent a documented claim on the second of December and an interim payment, and it is not a settlement, and it is not enough, and nobody has pretended otherwise.**

The claim is a document of nine pages. It is headed **MUNICIPAL CONCILIATION SERVICE — CLAIM NO. CC/26/1104 — IN THE MATTER OF PAYMENTS FOR THE FINAL TRADING WEEK OF OCTOBER 1974, SILLICK MILL, LOW WARD**, and it is drawn on the Museum's own acquisition record, on the certified comparison of two 1974 carbons, on the bound wage book for October 1974, and on one sheet of thin carbon folded in three, in a pay clerk's own hand, made at her own kitchen table in November 1974.

The interim payment is **eight hundred pounds per named claimant**, paid on account, and it is not an admission, and the letter offering it says so four times, and it is signed by a claims manager in another city who has proved right about everything in this business and has not been thanked once.

**It does not go to the people.** It goes into the conciliation service's account, per name, as a schedule of interim payments, and the schedule is a public document, and the reason is given in a letter from Mara Iles that is in the file and that says: *a sum of money handed to a person in a public room on a Saturday is a photograph, and the Museum has spent six weeks learning that a photograph of a payment is a receipt for the payer and not for the person paid. These are to be scheduled, and the schedule is to be public, and a person may come to the conciliation office on any weekday between ten and four and ask whether there is a line with their name on it and what is against it.*

Fifty lines are in the schedule on the second of December. Nobody knows how many lines there ought to be, because the schedule of contents of this Museum has been empty since the eleventh of September, and there is no instrument in this city that can say how many people worked on the closing shift of a mill on a wet Friday morning in 1974 except a book on a trestle table and a woman of seventy-eight and a woman of eighty-one who came on a bus.

And the fifty-first line is this:

> **51 — LOACH, P. — Sillick Lane, Low Ward. Fitter. Collected, 30 Oct 1974, per Museum accession 1961/LW/41–52. Date of death not established. Estate traced: none. Unclaimed. Held pending.**

Nobody in this building wrote that line. It was written by a clerk of the conciliation service working from an accession record and a name on a collection line, and the *Unclaimed* is a box on a form and *Held pending* is a box on a form, and the whole of it is a row of four boxes and a reference.

Elias Venn read it on the second of December at a table in a loading-bay office on Civic Hill, at about ten past nine in the morning, and read it twice, and Mara Iles was standing at the other side of the table and read it over his shoulder and did not say anything at all.

In the Hall of Former Faiths on the twenty-seventh of November there had been a receipt on an easel, in a plastic sleeve, with a collection line on it, and a man in overalls had been forty feet from it in a room of eighty people and had not moved, and the panel said *a receipt's a document* and *a name under a receipt is a saint*, and nobody turned the Museum's own card over.

**On Friday the twenty-seventh of November at four o'clock in the afternoon, in a front room at 12 Ferry Bank, second floor, first door, with a gas fire on, two conciliation officers in the room and a supervisor and a camera, and no member of the Museum of Quiet Saints in it, a cage came off a padlock in a building four miles away and was carried to that room by two people from the conciliation service, and eleven items came out of a box in a polythene bag in front of a camera, and a woman of seventy-eight watched.**

Item five is an envelope of loose papers, sealed, contents not examined, and it was put into this Museum on the seventh of November 1974 by a man in overalls called P. Loach, and it had been in a box in a cage in bay 9 for fifty years.

Inside it is one sheet, folded in three, and it is a **carbon copy of a pay sheet**, and it is in the pay clerk's own hand, and it was made at that table in November 1974 off an office carbon by a woman who did not know that the envelope would be posted to a firm of solicitors who would sell her office to the building that would keep it for fifty years.

It agrees with the bound wage book. Field Officer Marsh has said in the record that the two documents do not conflict on any name and on any figure that the pay column contains, and that where the carbon differs from the book the difference is in the *hours* column and the hours column of the book is the one that has been right, and that the reason is that the carbon was copied from the weekly sheet and the weekly sheet was the pay clerk's own working copy and the book was the firm's.

And there is no longer any corroboration in this city for either of them, and there never will be. The mill's own posted wage sheets for the last eight weeks of trading in 1974 went into four yellow skips on a quay in Northglass at twenty to eight on Friday the thirteenth of November and were at a pulp mill by the twelfth. The final pay run now exists in exactly two forms: **a bound book in the custody of a conciliation service in a hired room above a shop, and a sheet of carbon in a front room in Ferry Bank in a woman's own hand.**

And the shortfall is on the sheet, in total, in a pay clerk's figure, in her own hand, in 1974 money, and it is in a file in a room above a shop, and it is not in any Museum file, and it is going to stay in a room above a shop for a long time, and the only people who will ever be shown it are the people whose names are on the sheet, and about thirty of those have not come forward yet.

And it was never going to be proved by a bell. It is going to be proved by a woman of seventy-eight and a woman of eighty-one who came on a bus and a man of fifty-four with a hard hand and a hundred and forty names in a drawer in Low Ward, and by about four hundred people who were in a room, and by a man who could not say his own name for fifty-seven minutes and did not lose a minute doing it.
