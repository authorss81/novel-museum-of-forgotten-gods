# Chapter 0012 — The Gallery That Is Not Keeping Its Own Count

The dead letters came in on the Sunday in a manila envelope with a number in biro and, in the two afternoons of a suspended man, they were the only thing in the building that nobody was going to look at.

They went the way dead letters go. Return to sender where there was a sender. Circulars, and parish magazines, and a charity that had been sending the same appeal to the same address since before Elias worked here. Three envelopes from a firm of solicitors in Lantern Row about an assessment, opened, read, and re-tied. One from a school. And a great many, an embarrassing great many, from people who wanted to give things to a museum because they had read somewhere that the museum wanted things.

On the Monday morning, twenty to nine, with two hundred and ten of them done and a stack of six left, Elias found one that had not come through the post.

It was a photocopy of an envelope, which told him it had come through the post, because somebody had written *not delivered* on it in pencil and then had it photocopied at a branch library. The original was a Museum envelope with the crest on the flap. The addressee was a Mrs D. Sarn and the address was a first-floor flat on a road off Sillick Lane, and the whole of the line had been struck through in biro, and above it somebody had written a forwarding address in a different, older hand, and beside the forwarding address a postcode, and under the postcode, in a harder pencil, two words.

*Deceased — family.*

And underneath the envelope, folded in three, the letter itself, dated the ninth of October 1976, on Museum letterhead, and it had been folded in three so many times that the folds had gone through the paper and worn, and the ink had been pressed into the creases so hard that it was legible on the outside.

> We acknowledge receipt of your communication of 3 October concerning the disposal arrangements of the former textile works, Sillick Mill. The material described has been received for the Low Ward series and is retained for completeness. We are unable to assist further.

There was no signature. The letterhead carried a job title, an office and a telephone number, and the office was the Registrar's, and the telephone number was the number of the building Elias was standing in.

He read it the way he read everything, all the way down and then all the way back, and then he read the pencil note on the envelope, and he did not do the thing he wanted to do, which was to photograph it. The printed line over the tray said what it said, and he had signed that book with his name in it at two minutes to nine in the morning for the last time on Saturday, and he was not going to be a man who found a new way to break the same rule in the same week.

So he copied it out. Address, forwarding address, the office, the date, the words, and the two words in pencil, in the flat block hand he used for things he wanted to be able to find again, and then he sat back and looked at the six-line entry and understood that he had been doing this for two years and had never once given the count of anything away.

---

He was in the building from seven in the morning to eleven at night on the Monday, which was sixteen hours on an unpaid day, and the reason for it was not a rule and was not a decision so much as a fact about what a man does when the only place he is permitted to be is a loading bay.

At 08:55 he went and stood in the ground-floor corridor where the security room door is, because at nine o'clock the door of the Hall of Former Faiths was going to be opened by three people and he was going to hear all of it, and because he wanted to see who went through it.

They went through it at 08:58. A tall woman in a coat he did not know, going first, with a document wallet. A short man in a conservation apron with a case of instruments in a wheeled case, who was humming. And then a woman of about fifty in a plain coat with a leather satchel and no case at all, who stopped at the door, and read the plate on it, and did not go in.

Elias Venn stood in a public corridor forty feet from a door he was not allowed through and watched a woman with a satchel read the plate on it, and she did not go through, and he understood that she had not gone through because it was not her inspection yet; it was the Museum's conservator's inspection, and a Registry assessor stands to one side of a conservator and watches a case being opened and does not touch anything, and everything that is in the file afterwards will be in a document clerk's hand rather than an assessor's.

Then they went up and the door shut and the Hall was on the other side of it for five and a half hours.

---

Mara Iles came down at ten past eleven and stood at the loading-bay desk with a folder under her arm and did not sit down, and put one sheet on the corner of the desk flat, squared to the edge of the wood, which was a thing she did and which he had learned to find steadier than a conversation.

"Six minutes," she said. "I have to be back up at half past. I gave the Director my number twice and she gave me hers once, which is the first time that has ever happened to me in this building, and I am not going to think about what it means until I have slept."

"Tell me."

"No. First: what it was." She turned the sheet round. "It's a Registry inspection, not ours. Ours is the minute. Ours is three lines and a signature and it says the case was opened, the interior was examined, the plinth and the label are as previously recorded, nothing has changed, and Spence signs it. That's a fact and it's a good one. Dael minute-takes and Dael is the one person in that room who is glad it's over."

"And theirs?"

"QA/INSP/4, revised. One page. Serial QA/4/1187." She put her finger on it. "Section three, and I've read it until I could do it with my eyes shut, and I'd like you to read it as a person who has spent a week being told what a form will not carry.

> *Object: 34. Hall of Former Faiths. One bronze tally bell, cast 1902, with later staple repair to a shoulder fracture, incomplete.
> Classification: Schedule 12 — carried object, no activity observed in the period of inspection.
> Note: activity reported to the assessor by museum staff prior to the date of this inspection was not observed. Assessor makes no finding as to prior report.*
> *Grange, O., assessor, quiet accord registry, Northglass, 12 October*"

Elias read the note twice.

"They've written down that we told them it was sounding," he said.

"They've written down that a Registry assessor did not personally hear a sound in a hall where a sound had been reported. That is a *true* sentence. It is the most careful piece of writing I have read in this building and it is completely true, and it is a sentence about what a person in a room heard and not about what happened, and if somebody opens that file in fifty years they will find that on the twelfth of October an assessor attended Morrowgate, examined an object, and observed nothing, and concluded nothing, and recommended nothing." She took her finger off it. "Which is exactly what a Registry is for. It's the *note* that frightens me. Not the false thing. The note."

"You think it's wrong."

"I think it is correct in every particular and that correctness of that kind is a thing you build, Elias, not a thing you stumble into. Nobody in that room lied. Every one of them did their job. And the result of six honest people doing their jobs is a document that says there is no problem here, and I have four photographs in a man's coat that say there is, and I do not know which of those two things is going to survive contact with a Board."

He let that sit, because she was right, and because she was the second person since Sunday morning to say a version of it to him in a better form than he had managed.

"What's the classification mean?"

"Schedule 12 is the old Municipal Works schedule. Carried object. That means it carries a saint-pattern — that's their word, saint-bearing — and that no activity has been observed. That is not a safe classification. That is a *live* classification with a note on it, and it means it stays where it is and it gets looked at." She turned the sheet over.

"What's on the back?"

"That's why I'm here." Her voice did not change at all, which was how he knew. "They opened the case. Not the interior — the interior is Spence's and the interior is a small bronze bell with a staple through a crack. They opened the *frame*. They lifted the case off its feet, because the isolation pads under it had perished, which is true, they had, and the assessor stood there and watched Spence fit four new ones, and then they ran a lead from the case surround to the building earth, which is in the schedule as a requirement for a Schedule 12 carried object, and then they drilled a small hole in the terrazzo and bolted down a brass plate."

"Read me the brass plate."

"Reference QA/A/2. Four characters and a digit, stamped. Two of them, one under the case, one under the plinth, so that if the case is ever lifted the plinth cannot be moved and called an accident." She looked up. "Elias, there is not one other anchor plate in that Hall. There are thirty-one plinths and there is no brass on the floor of any of them. They have done a thing to that object that this building has not done to anything, and they have done it in ninety minutes, and I have stood next to a brass plate with a number on it and watched a man torque a bolt into a floor that is a hundred and forty-one years old."

"What does the plate do?"

"Nothing," Mara Iles said. "That's what I want you to understand. It does nothing. It is not a ward mechanism and it is not a lock and it is not on any schedule of the Accord's that I have ever seen. It is a piece of brass with a reference on it, and its entire function is to make the object *located*. Every document that comes out of here from now on says *object 34, QA/A/2, level 2, in situ*, and a thing that is *in situ* is a thing that has not gone anywhere, and the sentence will be true and it will be doing an enormous amount of work."

She squared the sheet again.

"And the other two things are on the second page and I have to go."

---

"The card," she said. "The assessor photographed the card. Through the glass, with a scale rule and a reference board, four exposures, on a Registry body camera, and the description is in the file in the assessor's own hand, and I am going to read it to you and I want you to notice what it says.

> *Card, printed face, hand-cut, ink, recent. Maker's line present, name illegible. Second line erased, partial. Accession reference on face reads 4471/B. No accession file located under this or any reference. Number is not in the form of a Museum reference. Photograph 1187/1 to 1187/4, QA registry body camera, not for reproduction.*"

Elias had to put his hand on the edge of the desk.

"They've got it," he said. "The erasure. Off a card, through glass, on a camera nobody in this building can reach, with a scale rule."

"They've got that the second line was erased, and they have not got what is on it, and there is a photograph of it in Northglass in a Registry file that no ordinary member of this building's staff will ever read." Mara's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "So say it, because I have read it too and it took me a second."

"Doreen Sarn," Elias said. "It's Doreen Sarn. It's the erased name. It is not Wendel Case."

"And I know that, and I know it because you have not told me that you know it, which means you found it between last Saturday and this morning, which means you found it somewhere I do not have, and I have not asked you where, and I am going to keep not asking you where, and I would like you to sit with how that feels." She picked the sheet up. "Two documents. Two. One of them has a card in it with a *made-up accession number* on it and the second line gone at badly, and the other has an assessor in it saying that the number is not in the form of a Museum reference. Two documents from two institutions that have never spoken to each other, and they say the same sentence, and I could take them both to a hearing tomorrow. And neither of them says the second line. Not one. In forty-eight hours a Registry camera and I have photographed the same erasure and we have both stopped at the same word, because *partial* is what it is, and we will both spend the rest of our lives at *partial*."

She was already at the stairs.

"What else is on that page."

"One line and I have to be back up at half past." She put her hand on the rail. "The object has been entered on the Museum's schedule of collections as *unaccessioned, carried, on deposit*, and a depositor's agreement has been raised, and the clause that matters is clause nine, which says the Museum shall not exhibit, lend, remove from the building, or dispose of a deposited object without the prior written consent of the depositor, and the depositor's address on the agreement is the Registrar's office."

"So the Museum doesn't own it."

"The Museum does not own it, and cannot prove it does not own it, because the ownership transfer is dated 1974 and the ledger page for 1974 in the Low Ward series has been out of the audit for four years because of a water incident on level B that nobody has ever had the money to write up." She said it the way a woman says a thing she has said to herself four times this morning. "That is a good clause. It is a well-drafted clause. It is a clause that has been on the Museum's depositor's template since 1998, and the Registry has now, with a scale rule, brought this building's most useful piece of paper to bear on that bell, and it will be argued over by four firms of solicitors for a decade, and there is not one person alive who can say who put that bell in this building or who took it out of anywhere."

"And the schedule also carries a flag."

"It carries a flag." Mara Iles stopped on the third step and turned round and looked down at him, and there was nothing in her face at all, and he found that much harder to be in the same building with than anything the other characters had done. "**Duly exported. Set the twelfth of October.** It has not been exported. It is four floors above us in a case with a brass plate in the terrazzo. And that flag is now on our object in the Museum's own schedule, and that flag is what a porter lifts a case out of a building on."

"Then somebody could—"

"Somebody could put a note on a docket, say *duly exported*, and the docketing would behave exactly as it is designed to behave, and the flag was set by an assessor who was in the building for five hours this morning and inspected the bell, and it is *correct*, because no sound was observed, and it is the single most effective piece of work this building has had done to it in a hundred and forty years." She went up. "I'm sorry. That was more than six minutes. Go and do your desk."

---

Ivo rang at twenty to one, from a workshop, with something with a compressor on running behind him.

"Read your own catalogue," he said. "You've spent a week reading a building. The spine of the whole inventory is the schedule of collections — what we hold, what it's made of, where it sits, what it's insured against — and it is *empty*. Nothing since the eleventh of September. The Registrar says it's with the assessor. So the Registry now holds a better record of this building's contents than the building does, and your friend's *exported flag* sits on the Museum's own schedule, and the schedule is the only thing a porter is ever going to look at before he lifts something out of here."

"Can you get in the security room?"

"I'm standing in it. I know exactly what that sounds like, and I also know what it costs you to have somebody do something you didn't ask for, and I did it anyway, and I'm not coming back to it." A compressor cut out. "And the schedule comes out on the table on Tuesday morning with everything else. The renewal's in April. There is not one human being in Northglass tonight who has a copy of what this building holds."

He spent the afternoon on dead letters because it was the only thing in the building that was his.

At half past three he found the remaining thing in the pile that was not circulars. It was a leaving card, printed, that had come round the whole building on a wire tray, and somebody had filled it in badly in biro on the back.

> To all — I'm off on the 30th. Two hundred and forty-one hours in and my own kit's still in the cage, which I've told the shop three times. They'll get it. I'd not bother. — *MALLOY, J. — Grade 2*

Two hundred and forty-one hours. And *nil*.

Elias read it until the print stopped being print, and then he read the word *nil* over again, and understood that he had no idea what it was nil of and was not going to find out from a card, and that a man in this building had said his own hours out loud to the whole staff in a form that would be thrown away, and that the form had said *nil* without saying what it was nil of, which was — he thought, and then stopped himself thinking, and then thought it anyway — the same disease as a result field with *a number* in it.

It was the only joke in his day. He took it upstairs at four o'clock because Ivo was in the systems office and the piece of paper was going to be on a bench somewhere for a year, and Ivo read it and laughed for about four seconds and then stopped laughing and said, "Two hundred and forty-one," and then said nothing else for a while.

---

He came in at six and the west range was on the emergency circuit and the Hall of Former Faiths was on the emergency circuit, and the whole building had the flat dry sound a building makes when it is being kept warm by a plant room rather than by a boiler.

At seven o'clock, a little before seven, two men from facilities came down with a cage.

It was a proper cage, floor-standing, on a dolly, with a mesh front and a hasp and a padlock that was new and had a paper tag on it. They had brought it down the service lane on a sack truck and they had a docket, and Elias read the docket because it was on a desk in front of him.

> HALL OF FORMER FAITS — RELOCATION, OBJECT 34
> FROM: hall, ground, plinth 34 (QA/A/2) TO: caged alcove, west range, ground
> REASON: AWAITING TRANSFER — works, Northglass
> **PLACED BY TRANSFER: 20:00, Sat 16 Oct.**

Elias read the date four times.

It was in the correct column, in the correct pen, with a signature under it that was a squiggle of the kind a signature becomes after thirty years, and it was Dael Ordway's, and the initials beside it were **D.O.** and the time was **20:00** and the day was Saturday.

It was half past seven on Monday.

"Saturday," he said. "It's Monday."

The taller of the two men checked his docket. "It says Saturday, mate."

"It's Monday. It's Monday night. That docket was written on Saturday and signed with a time on it, and the time is in the past, and that is either a man doing a job he has not got round to, or a man putting a date in a form, and I do not know which and I would like to."

They looked at each other. They were not Museum staff. They were Civic Works building maintenance under a schedule of charges, and they had a cage and a dolly and half an hour, and this was not their paper and not their day.

"Saturday, right," said the shorter one, and picked up the dolly.

The cage went across the ground-floor corridor and up four steps to an alcove beside the west lavatories that Elias had walked past a thousand times and had never had any reason to look into, and it was a dead end with a window that had been painted over, and the mesh front of the cage faced out into the corridor, and the padlock went through and the tag came round, and the tag said **QA/1187**, and the assessor had been in the building for five hours this morning.

Elias stood in the corridor and looked at a small bronze bell in a cage three feet from a lavatory door.

He could not see the case seal from there. He could not see the label. He could not see the plinth, because there was no plinth, and he could not see the anchor plate, because the anchor plate had been bolted to a terrazzo floor from which the plinth had been lifted whole and carried away. He could see a bell in a cage, through mesh, in a corridor, and a padlock with a paper tag on it, and he had a right to see nothing whatsoever and he was seeing all of it.

And the thing that made him put his hand on the mesh, and stay there for a second longer than a suspended man should, was not the bell.

It was that a person who put a date in a form had not moved it, and that a person who moved it would have had to destroy the form.

---

The headache that had begun at twenty-one forty-one on Saturday night came off him at ten to ten on Monday night, in a loading bay, over a table of a charity's circulars, without any warning at all.

He had been carrying it since Saturday. It had been the two-day one, the new one, and it had been a solid weight behind his left eye since Sunday morning, and it went at ten to ten and the first thing he felt was not relief.

It was the specific vertigo of a man who has been leaning on something for two days and has just had it taken away, and there is a second or two — he knew this, he had had it twice now — where you cannot tell whether you are all right.

He stood up and held the edge of the desk for a moment, and the loading bay was exactly where it had been.

Then he went and looked at the lamp, and he read its card twice more, and then he went up into the Gallery of Unnamed Hands with a torch, and he was barred from that room, and he had known that at seven o'clock in the morning when he read a piece of paper with his own name on it, and he had said *I know*, and he had said it out loud, and he had gone to the laundrette and to a bench outside a building and to two hundred and ten dead letters, and he had stopped at seven o'clock, and it was now ten at night.

---

Here is what the room was, and he had walked past the door of it every second week for two years and had never once been inside it.

The Gallery of Unnamed Hands was a long low room off the far end of the Hall, and its name was the joke the whole city made about it, and it was officially an unfinished research section, and it was shut. The window along the outside wall was shuttered inside and had been for years. There were nine plinths down the centre, and on eight of them there were cased objects under low light, and on the ninth there was no case at all: it was a plinth with a felt top and a card on it that said **SEE NOTE ON DRAWER 6B**, and the drawer was in one of the six flat files that ran down the other side of the room, and the drawer was open, because the case had been taken off it.

He put the torch on it.

It was an oil lamp that had been converted. Navy blue enamel over pressed steel, a cradle handle with the paint gone at the grip, a short lead ending in a bayonet cap, and inside the reflector a filament that had been dead for a very long time. On the base there was an oval brass maker's plate — **BIRCH & SLOANE, ENGINEERS, NORTHGATE** — and next to the plate, on a brass record strip that was fixed into the base with three small screws, there was a number and a line, and the line was very short because it had been cut.

> 1961 / LW / 51 — FITTER'S LAMP, ELECTRIC, NAVY ENAMEL.
> *Presented to the Museum, 1974.*

And in the margin, in ballpoint, in the same ballpoint as a date that had been entered at the same time:

> *Purchased at the closure of the works, 30 Oct 1974. — D. SARN.*

The Museum had had the lamp since 1974, and the accession said 1961 because the Low Ward series was opened in 1961 and the year on the front of a reference was the year the series was opened, and it was a fitter's lamp and it had been bought on the last morning the mill ever ran, by the pay clerk, for a lamp that was sold off off a bench with the rest of the shop, and her name was on the object in this building, and nobody had ever read it. It had come out of the low end of the same catalogue run as a box of Sillick Mill administrative papers that now had a hand-written label on it and was in a cage at the end of a corridor.

He had been inside the room for four minutes.

He stood there and counted the files, because he was not going to think about the lamp yet. Six units. Eight drawers to a unit.

Forty-one drawers. Nine objects. Forty-one and nine, and one of the nine had no case on it because its case was on a cart in the loading bay, and the room's own printed sheet — pinned inside the door on a clipboard, so old that the plastic had gone amber — was headed **GALLERY OF UNNAMED HANDS — OBJECT NUMBERS** and listed nine entries and nothing else, and had listed nine entries since 1988, and there was a room under his feet with forty-one drawers in it and a piece of paper on the wall that said the room held nine things.

Nine was the number the suppression had been using for thirty-six years.

He wrote it in his head and not in the notebook, and he went to the door, and he checked the corridor, and the loading-bay desk was ten metres away and the desk was empty and there was a chair and a card-index tray on it and a logbook, and the ground floor was nine people on nine floors of a building where the galleries had shut at six, and there was no procedure in this building, in any document, in any of the four books he had been trained in, that covered what he was about to do.

No Mara. No Ivo, who had been gone since half past three and who had a *systems inventory* to present in seven and a half hours and who could not afford to be on a corridor list. No named cost said aloud to a witness. No salt-and-ash card, because the last one had been scraped into a conservator's palm at twenty-two minutes to ten on Saturday night, and what he had in his coat was the jar and a sheet of stiff card torn off the back of a duty roster, and he had never in his life made one on his own.

No exit that anybody else was going to hold open.

He had the stop word in his own mouth and nobody to say it.

He said it to himself. *Fault.* Out loud, once, in an empty room, and heard how it sounded, and understood that this was the difference between a protocol and a habit.

He made the card badly, at the flat file, with the ash thin and blotchy because his hands were cold and because he was spreading it in the dark with a finger. He wrote the question on it in pencil before he asked it. He did the five things he could do and skipped the sixth.

---

The bound was fifteen minutes, and it was nothing, and it was ten past ten on a Monday in a shuttered gallery on Civic Hill with a bad card on a flat file and nobody in the building who knew he was in it.

> *Who carried this lamp out of the last building it was in.*

The first three seconds were nothing. There was a shuttered window, and a row of dark cases, and a dead plinth with an open drawer in it, and the smell of old felt.

The fourth second was a table.

Not a museum table and not a mill table. A kitchen table, scrubbed pale, with a cloth folded over the end of it, and the lamp was on the table, and the lamp was lit, and that was its act, that was the thing it had been made to do and the last thing it had ever done, and so the room it brought was the room it had last lit.

A hard hand, on the left, and a pen. A sheet of ruled paper, ruled in columns, and the columns had been ruled by somebody who cared about columns, and the head of the sheet had a title on it, and he could not get the title.

And on the right, at the edge of the light, because the light was on the table and the table was in the middle of the room: a man.

Not a face. He understood that he was not being given a face, and he understood it the way a man understands a thing about a photograph: not because the photograph was damaged but because the camera had never been pointed at it. The lamp saw the man from a metre and from the side, and a man at a metre and from the side is a shoulder, and an arm, and a cap on the back of a chair, and a hand coming into the light and stopping short of the paper and going back, and coming in again, and going back.

He was doing arithmetic on the sheet. That was what the hands were doing. Pen on a figure, back along the line, pen on another figure, and the two figures were the same figure, and he did it again.

And the woman said, "You'll have to ask the clerk, love, you'll have to ask the clerk for that," and the woman's voice was the voice Elias had been listening to for two minutes, and it was not a voice he had ever heard in this building.

He had four seconds. He knew that they were four seconds, because his watch was on a mark he did not remember passing, and because the second time this had ever happened the arithmetic had also come out right.

The last four minutes of the thing were the woman's hands, and they folded the sheet, and they folded it in three, because a sheet that has been folded in three can be put in an envelope, and the lamp did not see the envelope.

It saw the cloth, and the cloth covering the table, and the chair with the cap on the back of it, and the woman's hand going across the table the way you go across a table when you are not thinking about it, and the line she said with her hand out and a lot of it after the part about the clerk.

*Put it in the post. Second post, it's got to be the second post.*

And then the lamp's own last act, which was to be a light on a table, and a woman, and a sheet folded in three under a cloth that smelled of lavender, and a sentence that was not addressed to a lamp and that a lamp had kept for thirty-one years because a lamp cannot do anything else with a sentence.

**"That'll do, then."**

Elias Venn came off the question in a shuttered gallery on Civic Hill at twenty-five past ten on a Monday night, standing up, with the taste of hot iron in his nose and both palms hot and the rope gone from his right hand. He stood in the middle of nine objects and forty-one drawers for a minute and a half without putting a light on anything.

Then he went to drawer 6B, which was open, and which he did not have to open because it had been open since the case came off it in the spring, and which he was allowed to look at because it was in a public gallery and he was a member of the public in the only sense available to him that night.

It was the gallery's own working drawer. Object cards, one a sleeve, in alphabetical order by the reference, and behind them the folder everybody forgets, which was the acquisition correspondence, and in 1974 every object in this room had come in on the same afternoon out of the same van.

The docket was a single sheet on a carbon, and the carbon had made two of everything, and it was the office copy.

> **RECEIPT OF MATERIAL — 7 NOV 1974**
> Received into the Low Ward series from Quill & Frayle, solicitors, 14 Lincoln's Lane, acting for the estate.
> Schedule of items collected at the former textile works, Sillick, by the estate's agent, at the works' own request, 30 Oct 1974, being the last working day. Eleven items. No disposal fee payable to the Museum.
> 1 — Time book, weavers
> 2 — Time book, dye house
> 3 — Gate book (leaves 1–96 present)
> 4 — Posted wage sheets, loose, approx. 2 dozen
> 5 — Envelope of loose papers, sealed, contents not examined
> 6 — Tea chest, office
> 7 — Wages book, monthly
> 8 — — withdrawn, soiled
> 9 — Lamp, fitter's, electric
> 10 — Bundle of wage sheets, tied with tape, marked *pay office*
> 11 — Clock, works office, stopped
> *Collected by: P. LOACH (fitter), Sillick Lane, Low Ward.*

Elias read line ten four times and then read line eleven, and then went back to line ten.

A bundle of wage sheets, tied with tape, marked *pay office*, taken out of a mill on the last morning it ever ran by a man in a fitter's trade off Sillick Lane, delivered to this building in a van with a lamp, and put on a shelf.

And it had never been opened. Fifty years. It had been accessioned as item 44 of a Low Ward series, described in a catalogue entry as *administrative papers, disposal*, and it had been written off as damp, and it had been lifted out of a bay by somebody who wrote a live personnel file number on it in biro, and it was three feet from a lavatory door behind a padlock, and the whole of the difference between the thing Hattie Bramm had been writing letters about for nine years and the thing that was going to happen to it in thirteen days, when the last of the site went over a wall, was a piece of tape and a mark in a dead man's hand.

And *Q. F. — 14 Lincoln's Lane*, on a 1974 receipt, in a Museum file, in a room with nine objects and forty-one drawers in it.

He did not photograph the docket. There is no rule that would have stopped him and no line printed over any tray about a carbon in a public gallery, and he stood in a shuttered room with his phone in his hand and did not do it, and he knew exactly why, and the reason was that a thing in a Museum file in a room, on a night, in the hands of a suspended man with a lamp, is not evidence and never will be, and the only way it became evidence was if somebody else had a copy. He had four exposures of the drawer itself in the telephone in his pocket, taken on his way in, and he understood while he was standing there that a photograph of a piece of furniture in the dark is worth a great deal less than a photograph of a document, and that he had done the one that was worth less, and that this was also a decision.

He put the drawer back the way he had found it, which was open, and went down.

He wrote it all down at the loading-bay desk, and then he wrote down the two things that made it a fact, and then he sat and looked at the three entries for a long time.

The first was the reading. A table, a cloth, a sheet folded in three, a hard hand, a pen going onto a figure twice, a man at a metre and from the side with no face on him at all, and a woman's voice saying *you'll have to ask the clerk for that* and *put it in the post, second post, it's got to be the second post*, and a lamp that had been lit exactly once in fifty years and had kept a sentence for thirty-one years because it had nothing else to do with one.

The second was a carbon receipt in a drawer, made by this building in 1974, that said a bundle of wage sheets tied with tape and marked *pay office* had come out of the mill in a van with the lamp, and had been put on a shelf, and had not been opened in fifty years.

And the third, which he wrote last, and underlined, and which was the actual difference between Saturday and Monday:

> The lamp was on the table and the man was at the side of the table. I asked who carried it out of the last building and I got a kitchen. That is not the object failing to answer. That is the object telling me the truth about where it was standing.
> So: the object is not wrong. The object is *somebody's*. Everything the man did in that room, we are seeing from a lamp on a table, and a lamp on a table cannot see a face, and a sheet of paper cannot know what is on the other side of it.
> And it repeated her sentence back at me, word for word, after thirty-one years, because it was in the room when she said it and it is the only thing in this city she has ever been able to say out loud.
>
> **Which means the sheet is telling the truth about a woman, and lying about everything else. And I cannot fix that from inside it. There is no room a man can stand in where both of those are true at once.**

Then he wrote the times, and the time limit, which was fifteen minutes and not eleven, and the door he had gone in through, which was a barred door, and that he had been in the room alone, and that there had been no card worth the name and no witness and no one to say the word.

He had been in the building since seven. He went out at eleven.

And on the step, in the rain, with a door closing behind him, there was a sentence in his head with no wall and no speaker behind it, in a room with eight dead lamps in it and one more that was not dead on Monday and had been lit exactly once in fifty years:

> *The room is not keeping its own count.*

He did not write it anywhere, in either book. He had two lines in his own notebook that were longer than the sentence and shorter than an account of it, and a number in an envelope in Northglass, and a name he had read four times that morning in a letter dated 1976, and he stood on the step in the rain and found that he did not want to go home, and did not want to be in the building, and did not know what a man in Low Ward was supposed to do with a day that had started at seven o'clock in the morning.
