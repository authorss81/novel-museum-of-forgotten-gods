# Chapter 0004 — The Card With Two Names

The card on plinth thirty-four was not a printout. Everything else in the Hall of Former Faiths came off a printer, and that one had been cut by hand out of a sheet, and squared to the eye, and the four corners were sharp in the way that only a blade makes them.

Elias stood in front of the case at one in the morning with a headache that had not moved a degree in an hour and a bit and thought about that for a while.

The Museum made labels in one room. He had worked out that much in his first year, from the trays of finished cards that went up on the conservation floor every Monday and came back down with corrections, and from the fact that nobody in the galleries ever seemed surprised by a label. They were cut in the label room on the first floor, by conservation staff, on a card cutter, by people whose names were on a list somewhere. A guard could not go in there. A guard could stand in a doorway and ask, and get told, in a voice like a closing door, that the room was not his.

He had been doing the round for two hours. He was not going to get a file. He was not going to get a file tomorrow either, because the only person who could produce one had told him in a corridor that some objects came in without a file at all, and had told it to him in the specific tone of a woman naming the last time she guessed and what it cost.

Then, because a card that opens nothing is a thing somebody has to report, he went back down to the Hall door at twenty-six minutes past one and put his card on it out of pure habit.

The lamp went red. The Hall is a public gallery and it is locked after twenty-two hundred, and his key was in his pocket, and the reader had no business being awake and was awake anyway.

It wrote the refusal down. It was the first thing that night that had put him in the building's record, and he had done it to himself, for nothing, on the way past.

So. Somebody had cut that card by hand. Somebody had cut it in the label room, and somebody had been told what to cut, and the number on it meant nothing, and there was a name underneath it somewhere, because you do not put a new name on an old object without the old name being on the back of the card where the next person can find it.

And if there was an old name, it would be on the back, and the back of a card that had been used before would still be on the shelf, in a drawer, in a room with no windows and a card cutter and an alphabet on forty-one drawers.

Elias went up to the service door.

---

The tray was where it had always been: a grey steel cupboard on the left as you came in, unlocked, with two hundred and six numbered slots in three columns and a sign wired to the front of it that said PHONES. Beside it on the same table was the book, a ruled pad on a clip, with a column for name, one for time out, one for time back.

Above the columns somebody had printed a line in capitals, and it had been printed there for the two years Elias had been signing the pad every shift like a man signing a form for the bin.

> NO PHOTOGRAPHY OF COLLECTIONS MATERIAL, LABELS, OR RECORDS.
> THE BOOK IS THE RECORD. THE TRAY IS NOT.

He had read that line every night for two years. He had read it the way you read a fire exit notice.

He wrote in it:

> Venn, E. — 01:52 out

He did not write a destination, because there was no column for one, and because a destination is the only part of this that could be a lie. He stood there and looked at the two empty columns for a second longer than he needed to, and then he took the line as it came and left the rest of it blank, which is the shape of a man who has decided not to be a thief and has not decided to be anything else.

Then he unlocked the tray, which was not locked, and took his phone out of slot twelve, and the duty manager came down the service lane on her round at about two, the way she does on a quiet night, and he did it in front of her.

"Everything all right?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"That's a late one."

"Yes, ma'am."

She went up. Elias put the phone in his coat and went up the stairs with it, and he was aware, the whole way up, of the fact that he had just made a piece of paper in a building say that Elias Venn wanted his telephone in the middle of the night, and that he had chosen to make it say it in ink, in a column, with his name spelled out, rather than to have taken it out of an unlocked cupboard and put it back and told nobody anything at all.

The drawer was the right one. Sillick. Nine or ten cards, all of them cut the same way, all of them squared to the eye, and thirty-four was third from the back.

Every plinth card in the building has a spare in a drawer, because cards get torn and water gets on them and somebody in the label room is responsible for the fact that this Museum can replace any label in the collection inside a day. The card in the case on plinth thirty-four was sealed behind glass under a public no-touching rule, and Elias was not going to get that far tonight and would not have been entitled to. The spare was not sealed. It sat in a drawer in a windowless room, and it was the same card, the same cut, the same text, and the text was the name.

He had it in his hand and the light of the room raking across it before he had decided to be the kind of man who does this.

Fenn was at the far end with a batch of four hundred and she was not going to come and look at his face for a while.

He got the card up to the bench light. New card stock, which he knew because there was no acid in it and this building still ran on paper with acid in it, and the ink on the face of it had the particular shine of a pen that had not been used long enough to sink in.

Then he did what he had come to do, and he did it with the flat of a bone folder against the back and the light coming across it, and the shine came up, and there was writing under it.

The card had been used before. That was the whole finding, and it was a stupid one to arrive at at two in the morning in a room with a woman at the far end of it. The back of a card in a drawer is not blank, because the back of a card is where the maker goes.

There were two lines. The first had been taken off with something dry and hard, pressed hard enough to lift the fibres and leave them standing up like frost on a window, and what was left of it was a shape and a place and a date with the last figure gone: a foundry name, *Low Ward*, and a year nobody could read any more. The second had been gone at by the same rubber, less carefully, in a hurry, on a different line, and it had not gone at all.

*Wendel Case.*

Not a maker. A maker on a Low Ward card is a foundry and a place, and a foundry can be written to, and a foundry that answers has a catalogue, and a catalogue has a name at the top of it. That is why the first line had been taken off, and it had been taken off thoroughly, and it had been taken off by somebody who knew exactly what a catalogue is.

The second line had been missed.

On the front, in the same recent ink, the number. Acq. 4471 / B. A number standing in the place where a maker, or a mill, or a date of acquisition ought to be — and that is the name this object has carried in every public document the Museum owns.

Then he put the card flat on the bench and pushed his thumb gently along the half-millimetre of dust that had been sitting on the top edge of that stack of cards since somebody filed it, and the mark came up on his thumb like a line on a chart, and he was, for about a second, standing in a building with a bell on plinth thirty-four and a card on a bench and two names on the same piece of paper. They were not a name and a correction. They were a name and a name, and one of them was a number pretending to be a maker, and the other one was a person, and a person does not get written on the second line of a card for a foundry.

He took the phone out.

He did it standing up, quickly, with the card on the bench under the light and his body between the door and the shelf, and he took it four times: flat, at a low angle, raking, and straight on, and then he put the phone away and looked at the door lintel.

The camera was up there, a small dome on the smoke detector's mounting plate, and he had checked it from the doorway before he had touched the drawer, because he had checked it. Its field took the doorway and the card cutter and about a metre of bench. It did not take the drawers. It could not take the drawers, because the drawers were behind a solid partition and the card cutter had been installed in 1998 without consulting anybody about the room's angles.

Fenn said, from the far end, without turning round, "If you're looking for the Low Ward series they're two drawers down on the left, and there's a spare box of the 1902 stock in the bottom if you've run out, and I haven't, so don't take mine."

"I wasn't after the series."

"No? Then you're welcome to tell me what you were after and I'll tell you if I'm allowed." She turned round. She was perhaps fifty-five and she had been doing this for a very long time and she looked at people the way a person looks at a bird that has come in through an open window. "You don't have a badge on."

"I'm security. I've been up here two hundred times."

"You've been up here for the tea and the machine and the printer, which I know, because you always say goodnight." She put her hand flat on her batch of four hundred. "You're the one who's been in the drawers. I heard the drawer. That's a good drawer, that one, and it doesn't want slamming."

"I opened it gently."

"You opened it the way you open a drawer at home, which is the way you open a drawer when somebody's watching." She gave him one long look and went back to her batch. "Whatever it is, it'll come round. It always comes round to the label room eventually, because there's nowhere else in this building for a name to be."

Elias put the card back.

He put it back a half-millimetre out of true, with the top edge sitting a finger's width proud of the rest of the stack, and he stepped back and looked at it, and the reason he did it was fully considered and he did it anyway: because a man who takes a card out of a drawer and puts it back straight has not done anything at all, and a man who puts it back crooked has left a thing on the table for whoever comes looking.

It was the worst decision of the week and it took him a second and a half and he made it standing up.

---

It was five minutes past two. He had the four photographs and the name and about twenty minutes in which the duty manager was somewhere else in the building, and there was one more thing he could do with the twenty minutes, and it was the worst one.

He went up one floor.

There is no camera on the level-two landing. He knew that the way he knew there was a camera on the label room lintel: because he had walked past both of them two hundred times, and because two years ago he had asked the systems tech why the one on the landing was a blank plate and been told, not unkindly, that nobody had ever asked the Board for a second camera and that he should be careful about what he asked the Board for.

At the end of the landing there was a restricted door with a card plate on it, and a card plate writes down who opened it and when.

Elias stood in front of it for a while with a name in his hand.

Then he put his card on, because a card that reads a card is the most honest machine in the building, and what it would write down was a true fact, and he had spent two years being paid to be the man who writes true facts down.

The door let him in. The building wrote it down. Both of those happened within about a second of each other and neither of them was going to happen differently.

---

Inside there was a corridor eight feet wide with a window at the end that had been painted over, and a fire plan in a frame on the left, and behind the fire plan, on a steel shelf, the personnel record.

He had assumed it would be a system. It was a shelf.

One ring binder a year, going back as far as the shelf went, each one with a printed spine and a year on a gummed strip, and a card at the end of the shelf that said 1974– and nothing else. Inside each one the same thing: surnames down the left in alphabetical order, and against each surname a grade, a start date, a department, and a leaver's column with a date and a reason code in it where the leaver's column was filled in.

He had read enough of these to know what he was looking at, and it took him about four minutes to stop being excited and start being frightened.

There was no W. Case.

He did it properly, because he had a name and a job and an hour. He took the C's. Callaghan, R., grounds, 1991 to 2003. Cunliffe, A., porter, 1994 to 2011. Coverley, D., porter, 1979 to 2002. Crowther, M., conservation, 1986 to 2009. Four C surnames in thirty years of a building with two hundred people on its books, and not one of them had ever been a night porter except two, and neither of them was called Case.

Then he went back to the front of the binders and did the thing that took him the longest and told him the most.

He read the year strips.

He read the leaver's column. He read the spine of every binder from 1974 forward and put his thumb on the shelf and walked the whole run of it, and there was not one year missing, and there was not one binder out of order, and there was no gap in the shelf where a binder should have been and no sign anybody had ever taken one off the shelf to do a job at a table and put it back slightly wrong.

There was no seam.

That was the thing he stood in front of for the rest of his twenty minutes. Somebody, at some point, had gone into a complete record and removed a line, and had done it so well that the record was not merely lying — the record was *better* than it had been. There was a regularity about it now that had not been there before. Every other grade in the building had a leaver's date. Every other grade had a reason code. Grade 2 porter, the largest grade in the building, had a leaver's column and eleven names in it and one of them had a reason code that read *personal* and the other ten read *left service*, and the ten were spread across nine years, and there was nothing wrong with that at all, and if anybody had put it in front of a panel it would have gone through in four minutes.

Elias did not photograph that one. There was no camera to avoid, and a photograph of a shelf of binders in his own coat would have been worth nothing, and standing in the eight-foot corridor he understood that the useful part of this was not the record at all.

The useful part was that he now knew the shape of the hole. It was not a hole in a list. It was a hole in a *series* — nineteen years of grade-two night porters in a building that runs a bell shift, and one of them had gone out of the middle of it and left a tidy column behind.

He wrote two things in the notebook and shut it.

> Thu 02:20 – level 2, archive corridor. Restricted door, card-read, will be in the log in my name. No camera on the landing.
> Record on the shelf is annual ring binders, 1974 forward. Every year present, every binder square, nothing disturbed. Columns are surname / grade / start / dept / leaver's date + reason code. The C's: Callaghan R grounds, Cunliffe A porter, Coverley D porter, Crowther M conservation. No W. Case, and no leaver's record for Case either. Whatever came out of this was taken out properly.

And underneath, because it was the sentence he had been in the building for:

> The record is not damaged. That is the worst thing about it.

He put the binders back square, which was the only thing in that corridor that was going to be exactly where it had been when he arrived, and he went down.

Then he went down to the service door and put the phone back in slot twelve, and wrote his name and 02:41 in the book, and stood there for a moment with the pen resting on the line.

The office above him was quiet. The Hall of Former Faiths was a floor down and locked and dark with one bronze bell in it. His head had not moved a degree since 23:41 and there was a continuous note under his left ear, thin, patient, exactly like the note the case had made.

---

He got back to the security room at three and did the last of the round with his notebook shut, because he had understood something on level two that he did not want to test.

He had crossed a line. Not the line with the word *photography* printed over it — that was nothing, that was a letter, and letters get broken every week by people with worse reasons. He had crossed the line of a man who takes the thing somebody has hidden and keeps it, and then, a quarter of an hour later, a floor up, he had put his card on a restricted door in order to find out how deep the hole went, and the door had written his name down, and he had read a complete personnel record and found nothing in it, and had understood that the nothing was the finding.

He thought about telling Mara in the morning. He worked the whole idea out, standing at the desk with the round done, because he was good at this part: *I found an erased name under a false accession number, on a hand-cut card, in a drawer, and I have got four photographs of it on a telephone that I signed out of the tray in the middle of the night and I did not write down where I was going.* That was a good sentence. It was even, he thought, a decent sentence.

And then he imagined Mara's face while he said it.

Because Mara would ask him what he had done with the card, and he had put it back crooked on purpose, and he would have to say that too. She would ask him why he had taken the phone out of the tray at all, and the answer was that a man had told him a fortnight ago that a sound with a time on it is a document, and a document is a thing you can hand somebody — and he had handed it to himself, in a locked cupboard, in his own handwriting, where a man who wanted to protect himself from his own memory would put it.

And then Mara would do what Mara does, which is put it in writing. And the file would go into a system she did not control, on an index that a person had already demonstrated once this week could produce nothing, and a card that had been sitting in that drawer for however long it had been sitting there would be re-homed, or the drawer would be locked, or the person who had cut it would be told that a guard had been asking after their work.

And then in a fortnight the card would be gone, and the name under it would be gone, and the number 4471 over B would be nothing on a case in a locked hall, and the only remaining copy of *Wendel Case* would be four photographs on a telephone in the inside pocket of a coat, belonging to a man who could not produce them without admitting that he had broken a printed line to get them.

The line in the book was a warning. Somebody, in this building, a long time ago, had printed a warning about exactly this and nailed it to the wall by the tray, and the warning was not about the cameras at all.

Elias wrote in the notebook:

> Thu 01:52 – 02:41 — phone out of tray, slot 12, book signed both ends, no destination given.
> Thu 02:05 – label room, first floor. Cards for the hall are cut by hand, not printed. Card for 34 is recent. Two lines on the back: a maker's line gone at properly, and a second line gone at badly and not gone at all. W. Case. Card put back crooked. Deliberate. My decision.
> Thu 02:20 – level 2, archive corridor. Restricted door, card-read, will be in the log in my name. No camera on the landing.

He read the third line. He left it.

At four he did the second sweep of the service lane, and the wet came in under the service door the way it always did on a wet night in the autumn and stopped at the mat, and the building ticked, and the note under his ear went on being thin and patient, and there were three hours of the shift in front of him and one name in a coat pocket, and he went up the hill in the rain at seven to sleep for four hours before Thursday.
