# Chapter 0036 — The Man on the Floor

Sunday the fifteenth of November is the one day of the week that a building of this age is quiet in a way that is not suspicious. The plant does its work. The letterboxes on level two are empty. About nine hundred people came through the doors yesterday and about three hundred of them came through them between eleven and one, and there is a Sunday in a museum's week where there is genuinely nothing happening, and Elias Venn has learned in five weeks that the days when nothing happens are the days that get you, because there is nothing to count.

He was on the desk from ten because the front desk is open on a Sunday and the front desk is the only place in the building where a man who is not on the payroll may sit without a hat on.

He did the dead letters. There were three hundred and one of them and it was a Sunday and a clerk's is a slow job, and the great majority of them were circulars and parish magazines and one from a charity that had been sending the same appeal to the same address since 2016.

At about half past eleven the front-desk screen — which is a booking and stock screen, and which anybody at that counter can read, because the counter is a public counter and the screen faces the room — came up with a line on it that had not been on it at eleven.

> **REGISTRY ATTENDANCE — BOOKED. TUE 24.11. 14:00. GALLERY OF UNNAMED HANDS — RE-CERTIFICATION ON RETURN OF 1961/LW/51.**
> **ATTENDANCE: ASSESSOR (ROTA) — NOT ROTH, NOT GRANGE.**
> **REQUESTED BY: PELL, I. — SYSTEMS.**

Elias Venn stood at a counter in a public museum on a Sunday morning and read the second line four times, and then he rang the front desk's own internal extension, which is a thing you can do, and Ivo Pell answered it on about the second ring from somewhere a long way off.

"Was that a booking?"

"Course it was a booking, I made it at twenty past ten on Friday, it's been sat there since." A pause and a keyboard. "You can't book a specific assessor. That's not how it works. You book the slot and the slot gets whoever's on the rota that day, and I've had the bulletin since Monday and the bulletin has the rota on it, and Roth's on the twenty-fourth and Grange's on the eighteenth, and neither of those is the twenty-fourth, so I asked for the slot and the slot's gone to the rota, and the rota on the twenty-fourth is a name I don't know and have never asked about because it's none of my business who's on the rota."

"It's my business. It's a Gallery."

"It's *your* Gallery in the sense that you got a stay put on an object in it in October, and I'm not being funny, this is a systems matter, and I'll tell you what it is and then I'll tell you the funny bit."

"Go on."

"Two hundred and forty-six exposures." The keyboard stopped. "There are four assessors on that rota. Two of them I've had in this building and one of them read a document into a record and took a bell's hands off it in about four minutes, in front of fifty-one of us, on a Saturday, and she was right about every word of it, and I have not been able to forget it, and I don't think she's ever been thanked by anybody including me."

"Mrs Grange has been thanked."

"Mrs Grange has been *proved right*, which is not the same and is worse, because being proved right costs you something and being thanked costs them a stamp." He came back on the line. "And the other two, I don't know, and it does not matter, and I want to say something about that and then I want to tell you a joke, and if I tell you the joke first you'll think the first bit was a joke, and it wasn't."

"Go on."

"In March I photographed two hundred and forty-six exposures of six flat files for the insurance schedule, on a tripod, with a scale rule, under *schedule of collections, condition, Gallery flat files*, and it went in the systems index on the sixth of March with my name on it, and I did that because the schedule said *surveyed annually* and nobody had surveyed them since the year before, and I said all of that out loud in a room on the twenty-third of October and asked for it to be written down that way."

"I know. I was in the room."

"I handed four of them to a policeman. Not four of the two hundred and forty-six. Four. Of drawer 6B. Open, in place, with the card in it." A breath. "And a woman in a house in Sillick Lane with a laptop has a piece of A4 with the word *reading* on it in a paragraph six, and a man of seventy-eight is going to produce a carbon at a hearing in eleven days, and there is a file on the fourth floor of this building with a suspension in it that is not a suspension, it is a form that says *any further incident attributed to the same cause is to be referred to a hearing*, and I have been in this building three years and I have thought about clause five every day since the twenty-third of October, and it has not gone away, and nobody has decided it."

"Has anybody asked you to explain it in writing?"

"No."

"Have you offered?"

"Twice. Once in writing on the twenty-eighth of October, which is in Mara's file, and once out loud to a Director on the sixth of November, in a corridor, in about nine words, and she wrote it down both times and neither time has come back with anything." A pause. "So there's a man on a rota in Northglass that I've never spoken to coming into our Gallery in nine days to look at a room with eight objects in it against a wall sheet dated 1988 that says nine, and there's a woman on the same rota I've spoken to twice, and both of them are competent, and I cannot arrange which one I get, and that is what a rota is, and it is the most frightening document in this business and there are about forty of them in this building and I read all forty of them on Monday."

"That was the joke?"

"The joke's coming. The joke's the last thing." The keyboard came back. "Right. The joke. There's a poster of a weighbridge in our own lobby at four foot six off the floor and it's been there since the fourth of November and it's a picture of a gate with a hut behind it and there's no caption on it, and the campaign that put it there has put thirty-one of them round Civic Hill and every one of them is a picture of a thing with nothing written on it, and a girl of about eleven asked Mara on Saturday why there wasn't a label. And Mara said *because it's a photograph of a place, not of an object.* And the girl said *that's not the same thing.*"

"And she isn't wrong."

"She is not the same thing and that is the joke," said Ivo Pell, "and it is the only joke I've got this week, and I'm going to be at the front desk at eight on Friday morning, and you can have it then if you've got a better one."

---

Ivo Pell was on the floor at about half past one, which is a Sunday, on a systems round, with a torch and a printout, and the Gallery of Unnamed Hands is on the second floor and is open to the public on a Sunday from eleven to four, and there were four people in it.

She was at plinth 9.

Elias Venn was not there. He was at the front desk on the ground floor, two floors down, at ten past two on a Sunday afternoon, and he found out at about ten past four, and he found out from Ivo Pell, and that is the correct order of it and it is the order it happened in.

Hattie Bramm had come up at half past one on a Sunday with a bag and her coat on and had asked the front desk whether the Gallery was open, and the front desk said it was, and she had gone up, and there was one member of staff on that floor, and he was twenty-five and he was on a systems round and he was standing in the middle of the room with a printout.

He could not leave. That is the whole of it. He has a rule, on a plywood board in a corridor, four lines, screwed up on the twenty-first of October, and the fourth line says *if you are not sure whether you are a party, you are a party*, and a member of the public alone in a public gallery is not a party of one, but a man who has been in a room with a woman for four minutes is, and the rule is not about capability, it is about who is in the room, and so Ivo Pell stayed.

Plinth 9 is a felt-topped plinth with no case and a card in a plastic sleeve on the front edge, and the card says the lamp is in stores, and it is empty, and the third drawer down in the west unit of six is open.

There is a sheet of thin grey carbon paper in drawer 6B, folded once across, with eleven lines of typing on it, and a ballpoint entry in the right-hand margin in a hand that leans.

She read it standing up.

It says, in a typewriter face, on a form headed **RECEIPT OF MATERIAL — 7 NOV 1974**, that eleven items were received from a firm of solicitors at 14 Lincoln's Lane in Irving Quay on the seventh of November 1974, collected on the thirtieth of October 1974 being the last working day, no disposal fee payable to the Museum, and it lists eleven things, and the fourth of them is *posted wage sheets loose, approx. 2 dozen*, and the fifth of them is *envelope of loose papers, sealed, contents not examined*, and the seventh is *monthly wages book*, and the tenth is *bundle of wage sheets, tied with tape*, and the ninth is *the fitter's lamp*.

She is an unpaid-bills organiser and not an archivist and she runs a Friday queue off a printed grid, and she had never seen a document from that mill in her life, in nine years of writing to a company that had bought the freehold from the receiver in 2019 and had a sign on a fence, and she read a 1974 receipt standing up in a gallery on a Sunday afternoon with a man of twenty-five next to her.

Ivo Pell says that she read it twice. He says that he did not offer to read it to her and did not have to, and that he stood about a foot away and looked at the ceiling for the first half of it.

Then she asked him a question, and it was not about the lamp, and it is the sentence the whole of this business is going to need and Elias Venn did not have it and has still not got it, and a man of twenty-five had it in his own head by three o'clock that afternoon, and had carried it down two flights in his coat pocket to a man at a counter, and had written down not one word of what she had not said.

She said: "You're the one who does the systems round."

"That's right, love."

"Are you the one who's off the rota on Saturdays?"

He said he was.

"Right." She put her hand flat on the edge of the plinth, which is not a thing you are allowed to do, and Ivo Pell did not tell her, because the object was not there and the plinth is furniture. "Then can I ask you something that isn't about the lamp."

"You can ask me anything you like."

"You can't, because you're not the one who can do anything about it, and I know that, and I'm asking anyway." She did not look round. "I've got a hundred and forty on my list. That's mine, that's nine years of letters, and every one of them I can put to a street and a door, and I've got about thirty-one still to get to, and about forty of them are dead, and about nine are in a home, and the rest I have had letters off or I have had nothing but a person who knew them. That's the part I can do. I can *do* that part."

"Yes."

"And there's a second thing and there's no list of it." She turned round. "The ones I can't get are the ones who never had a letter. There's no version of that list. There's no list of the people who were never written to, and there never will be, because you can't take a list of a thing that nobody kept, and I'm the only person in this city who has ever tried, and I have got a hundred and forty, and the hundred and forty is everybody I could find, and everybody I could find is everybody there is as far as I can prove, and it is not."

Ivo Pell, who is twenty-five and who has a clause-five question hanging over him and a sister with a chest, said:

"I don't know."

"That's all right," said Hattie Bramm. "I know you don't. I didn't come for an answer, love, I came because you're the only one on this floor and I have got about nine more days of not being able to ask anybody who is paid to answer, and then there's a hearing."

"It's the twenty-sixth."

"Then I'll come to the hearing," said Hattie Bramm, "and I won't be a witness, and if anybody stands me up there I'll say what I said to you, and then they'll have to write it down, and then it'll be in a file and it'll be a thing anybody can point at instead of a thing I said in a gallery on a Sunday, which is the whole reason I've been not saying it for nine years."

She said that last part with no weight on it at all.

Then she asked him one more thing, which was whether the Museum was writing to the hundred and forty, and he said he did not know, and she said, "No, they won't," in a voice that was not disappointed, and then she put her hand on the case of nothing at all on plinth 9 and said, "That's a shame for them, not for us," and went down.

Four minutes. Ivo Pell had a watch on and it was four minutes, and afterwards he wrote the number down on a printout, which is not a thing anybody does, and then he wrote what she had said underneath it, in longhand, on the back of a systems sheet, and then he put it in his pocket and carried it down two flights to a front desk where Elias Venn was standing at ten past four on a Sunday.

"That's a printout for Tuesday," he said. "Ignore the printout."

"I'm not ignoring anything."

"Read the back of it."

He read it. It was in Ivo's hand, in ballpoint, badly, because it was written on a knee:

> **H.B. 15/11, 14:00 approx. Four minutes. Gallery, on her own, came up off the desk, came to me because I'm the one on the floor.**
> **She has a 140 and she can put a street to every one. The 140 is everyone she can find. The ones she can't get are the people who never had a letter. There is no list of those and there never will be, because you cannot take a list of a thing nobody kept.**
> **She said if anybody stands her up on the 26th she will say that, and then it will be in a file instead of a gallery, and that is the reason she has not said it for nine years.**
> **S. PELL. (she asked me if I was off the rota on Saturdays. I am.)**

Elias Venn read it twice.

He had had four minutes of Hattie Bramm in a corridor in October, on the sixteenth, outside a library, on his own initiative, and he had used them to say a room and a row and a plinth and a date and a case to a woman who was standing at a queue and did not have four minutes and did not ask for them, and she had given him four seconds in return and a sentence about a name in a room costing a room, and then she had gone back into her queue.

She had given the four minutes on a Sunday afternoon to a systems technician because he was the only person on the floor.

"She didn't come to the desk," said Ivo.

"No."

"You were at the desk."

"I was at the desk," said Elias Venn.

He did not go and find her. He thought about going and finding her until about ten past seven that evening, on a tram, on the wrong side of it, and he thought about it properly, and he worked out — he is not proud of this, and he wrote it down that night, and then crossed most of it out — that he was not going to go and find her because he wanted the sentence, and because she had just handed it to somebody else for nothing, and because if he went to her on Monday morning and asked her for it she would give it to him, and it would be in his notebook, and it would be *his*, and it would not be a thing a woman said in a gallery and had to be argued with.

That is the sixth time.

He wrote it down at half past eight, on a Sunday, at a table in a flat in Low Ward above a laundrette that shuts at ten, and it took two lines, and the two lines are the only thing in a hundred and fifteen pages of that notebook that he crossed out.

Nunn came in at about half past four on the Sunday, which is not a thing a transfer clerk does, and came to the loading bay, and stood at the desk with his coat still on.

"Two things," said Nunn. "One's work and one's a favour, and I'll do the work one first so you can tell me to go to hell on the second."

"Go on."

"Mr Nunn, transfer clerk, about the docket." He put a folder on the desk. "Hallowgate Foyston. Re-proposed on the fourth of November, signed, in time, with the new ten working days on it. New docket, new number, and I've had it in the system since the Wednesday, and it is the second one, and there is nothing wrong with it, and it is going to lapse."

Elias Venn did not look up straight away. He finished a line, and then he did the arithmetic, which he could not help doing, and which he has now done about six times since the Wednesday, and which is this:

> **Served: Wednesday 4 November. Ten working days, weekends out, the day of service not counted, as in October.**
> **Thu 5 — one. Fri 6 — two. Sat/Sun out. Mon 9 — three. Tue 10 — four. Wed 11 — five. Thu 12 — six. Fri 13 — seven. Sat/Sun out. Mon 16 — eight. Tue 17 — nine. Wed 18 — ten.**

He checked it against the last one, which he has checked about nine times in five weeks.

> **October: conditions issued Thursday 15th. Fri 16 — one. Sat/Sun out. Mon 19 — two. Tue 20 — three. Wed 21 — four. Thu 22 — five. Fri 23 — six. Sat/Sun out. Mon 26 — seven. Tue 27 — eight. Wed 28 — nine. Thu 29 — ten.**

And that is exactly right, and he checked it against the docket and against a sentence in a corridor and got the same answer both times: the tenth working day is the last day on which anything at all can be done, the office shuts at five, and it does not die at five, it dies in the small hours of the next working day, at four minutes past midnight on Friday the thirtieth of October, because a transfer clerk was sent for at half past four and told to be there at five and was there at five and had nothing to write on the form, and wrote *did not travel* when the day turned over.

Same clause, same arithmetic, same kind of clerk. Which means the last moment at which anything at all can be done about the Sillick box is **five o'clock on Wednesday the eighteenth of November**, and that at about four minutes past midnight on **Thursday** the nineteenth it is furniture for the second time in eleven weeks.

And he put the two dates next to each other on the same page, because he could not help it, and then he sat looking at them for about a minute, and then he wrote one more line, and it is the only line in that notebook that is a piece of arithmetic and nothing else.

> **The Board sub-group sits on Wednesday the eighteenth, eleven o'clock to six.**
> **The box dies at seventeen hundred on Wednesday the eighteenth.**
> **The material-change inspection is at eleven o'clock on Thursday the nineteenth.**
> **Three documents, three dates, one building, and nobody has put them in the same room, and I have just done it on the back of a clipboard in a loading bay at four o'clock on a Sunday, and it has taken me five weeks and it is the first useful thing I have done all month.**

"Five o'clock on Wednesday the eighteenth," he said. "Then it goes at about four minutes past midnight on the Thursday, like the last one."

"It'll be whatever minute it is," said Nunn. "It's not a date I've got to hit, it's a date I have to be *here* for, and I'll be here, and I will write on the docket exactly what the last one says, which is *did not travel* and *no removal took place* and the time, and I will do it in the same hand and with the same pen as the first one because it is the same form and I am the same man." He straightened up. "And the box is in bay 9 and has been since the first of November and I have not touched it and I have not looked at it, and I have said to two people this week that I have not looked at it, which is not the same as not looking at it and is a thing I have started doing and do not recommend."

"Say that again," said Elias Venn. "The bit about the room you are not allowed to be in."

"I've been doing it for four years," said Nunn. "It's a good trick. People stop asking." He was already walking. "It does not work on you, for what that's worth. You asked me the same question on the Friday and again on the Sunday and you are the only person in this building who has ever asked me it twice."

"Mr Nunn, on the second thing."

Nunn did not sit down.

"There is a man of forty-four called Ordway, who did nights until the sixth of November and has not been the supervisor since the seventh, and on Thursday I had a conversation with him in this bay at about half past four, and he said a thing about you, and I'm going to give it to you word for word because you asked me once not to improve on anybody and I have decided that includes him."

"Go on."

"He said: *there is a man on a seven to three and a six to nine rhythm who is not on the payroll and not on the rota, and who has been inside more barred rooms in this building this month than any member of staff, and there are days when I have to write a thing down and the thing I have to write down is whether he was in the building, and I have started writing it, and I have started writing it because a suspended man is not a thing I can put in a report, and I do not want to be the man who has started writing it. I do not want to be the man who has a column with his name in it.*"

Elias Venn did not say anything.

"And then he said, and this is the bit I've been carrying down two flights for: *I am not saying it to be fair to you. I am saying it because I have got a liability position in a transcript in Northglass that I cannot carry by myself, and if the day comes that somebody wants to know how many times that door opened in November, the answer is going to be in a column, and I would rather it was in a column with my name in it than a column with nobody's.*"

Nunn looked at the floor of the loading bay for a second.

"He was not being kind to you and he was not being unkind," he said. "I've worked with that man for six years. He wrote four of the conditions that took your bell off the plinth in October and he thinks that's the worst thing he's ever done and he has never once said so. He's not a bad man. He's a man with a building on his name and a form in a file in Northglass, and he did the thing, and I thought you ought to have it before somebody else does."

"Where is he?"

"Day off. He's on days now. He hasn't been on nights since the sixth of November and he hasn't been the supervisor since the seventh." Nunn put his hands in his coat pockets. "That's all of it. I've told Dael Ordway that I've told you. He'll be in on Monday and he will not mention it and neither will I, and if you go up to him on Monday and make a speech about it you'll have wasted the only good thing anybody's done for you this month."

"That's fair."

"That's not fair. That's me being practical." Nunn went to the door. "Right. The docket's the work one. The other one's the favour. A woman rang the office at about eleven this morning and asked to speak to whoever did the systems index and Ivo wasn't in and Priest took the message and put it on the board in my office, and it says *a lady from Low Ward, on the Gallery flat files, wants to know if the drawer is meant to be open and if not would somebody shut it, and she says she has read it and she is not asking for it.*"

Elias Venn stood very still in a loading bay.

"What did Ivo say?"

"He hasn't seen it. It's still on the board. It's a Sunday." Nunn stopped in the door. "That's a real thing, is it. That's a real thing that's just happened."

"It's a real thing that's been happening for a month," said Elias Venn. "Mr Nunn, thank you for the favour."

"Don't thank me, I passed it on a board." But he was pleased about it, visibly, and Elias Venn has never in five weeks been glad of a piece of news and had two in an afternoon, and neither of them was about the ledger.

He shut drawer 6B himself at about ten past five on the Sunday, and he should not have, and he did it in front of four people, and the fourth person was a man of about seventy on a bench by the window who had been in the room since half past one and had not asked for anything and had not read anything and had a folded newspaper on his knee.

He shut it because a woman on Low Ward had telephoned the front desk on a Sunday morning to ask whether the drawer was meant to be open.

He did it without permission and he put his initials and the time on the back of a systems sheet and put the sheet in the day book at the front desk, which is where a record of a thing that has happened goes, and he could not stop himself doing it, and it is on the page, and it is the first thing he has done in this building as an unauthorised act since the twenty-fifth of October, and the day book says:

> **17:12 — VENN, E. (member of public, signed in 10:04). Drawer 6B, Gallery of Unnamed Hands, closed. Contents undisturbed. No item removed. Reason: drawer was open in a public room.**

And that is the first of four entries he has now put in four separate books in four separate buildings about a drawer, and the last of them is his own initials in the Museum's own day book on a Sunday evening, and the reason he did it is that a woman of fifty-four read a document in a drawer on a Sunday and could not get anybody to answer her, and he was in a museum on a Sunday and he is the man with the notebook and he is the man who is not permitted in the room.

He thought about that for about a minute, standing at a counter, and it came to him on a Sunday evening in a form that will not go away:

He has spent five weeks getting a bell off a plinth by a lawful door, and the lawful door worked, and every single thing he has been allowed to do this month has been a thing a form let him do, and the only thing he has done today that anybody might call brave is shutting a drawer that was open, with a pen, in a room he is not allowed to be in, on the word of a woman who telephoned a front desk and was put on a board.

He went up the hill at eight. The dead letters were in a box under his bed, because the front counter had stopped being his on Monday. He got through about forty of them and then stopped, because a man cannot do three hundred and one circulars and think about a woman in Northglass, and he had a telephone in the flat and it was a telephone he had paid for himself out of about what was left of a night guard's hours, and he did not use it.

He had a notebook with a hundred and fifteen pages in it and a hearing in eleven days and six families in a room and a determination on Thursday morning and a man's initials in a drawer, and he had spent the whole of a Sunday shutting a drawer and writing down what a woman said to a man of twenty-five, and that was the correct day's work, and it was the first day in a month on which nothing in this building was worse on Monday morning than it had been on Sunday night, and he noticed that he was pleased about it, and he was suspicious of being pleased.
