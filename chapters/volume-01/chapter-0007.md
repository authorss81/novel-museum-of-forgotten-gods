# Chapter 0007 — The Roster

Ten past seven on Friday, and Elias Venn put the words *Sillick Mill* into a terminal and the Museum gave them back to him in four seconds, which was faster than the tram had managed in six years of both of them taking it.

> SILLICK MILL (LOW WARD): ADMINISTRATIVE PAPERS, DISPOSAL.
> Accession 1961 / LW / 44. Location: unprocessed storage, level B, bay 9.
> Condition: damp. Not for issue. Not for exhibition. Retained for completeness of the Low Ward series.

Bay nine. He read it four times. It was on the level he had walked past twice a week for two years, under the floor he had been staring at since Wednesday, behind a door he had personally tested for a working lock in his first fortnight.

He read the second line again. *Not for issue. Not for exhibition.* Somebody in this building had written a catalogue entry about a box of paper with the tone of a man nailing a lid on, and had left the year off, because the year on a Low Ward reference is the year the series was opened and the series was opened before the mill ever shut, and a man who wanted a box to be administrative would not have wanted anybody doing arithmetic on it.

He got up and went down.

---

The freight lift to sub-level B runs on one card and a rule: two people, no exceptions, no exceptions, no exceptions, in three different places in the policy, one of them in Dael's handwriting in a white correction fluid over a typed line.

Elias went down alone on purpose, because he knew the rule, because it was 19:18 on a Friday and the museum's whole staff was one duty manager on the freight lift and two cleaners and Ivo in the maintenance shop.

The gate interlock at the lift head is not a turnstile. It is a small steel gate and a green lamp and a magnetic plate, and the plate reads a card, and if you are going down without a second registered person on the same authorisation, the lamp is red and the gate is a wall.

He put his card on. Red.

He stood there for a moment, in a service corridor on a sub-level of a building he guarded, holding a card that had just told him no in a way that had nothing to do with his card at all.

He thought about the things he could have done. He thought about them properly, standing at the gate, and he made himself finish the list. He thought about the maintenance stair and whether anybody at night had ever used it with a box of paper, which they had, there was a book about it, it was in the policy he had read, it was not against the rules and it was against everything else. He thought about the fact that two of the cleaners would have said yes if he had asked, and about what he would have had to tell them, and about the fact that a person who has been asked to break a rule with no reason given is a person who has been made an accomplice without a conversation.

He thought about how good it would feel to be standing in bay nine at ten past seven on a Friday night, and about the fact that this was the third time that week a thought had arrived dressed as an emergency.

He went back up and called the shop.

"Ivo. I need you to come and stand at a gate for ninety seconds."

---

"That," said Ivo, twenty minutes later, wiping his hands on a rag, "is the single most pointless thing anybody has ever asked me to do on a Friday night."

"I know."

"I want that on the record."

"Fine. Put it on the record."

They went down. Ivo put his card on alongside Elias's and the lamp went green and the gate opened with the noise of something that had been opened a hundred thousand times, and they went down in the lift together, and level B smelled exactly the way it always smelled, of wet cardboard and cold stone and a faint chemical sweetness off the conservation paper that had been stored down here by people who had never had to suffer for it.

Bay nine was three rows in. Elias had a torch and Ivo had a torch and neither of them had a reason that would survive a question, and they stood in the row and looked at the shelves.

Bay nine held accessions 1961/LW/41 to 1961/LW/52. It held box files, and folders, and two wooden crates, and a shelf of shelf-marks in white card, and the damp had got into the bottom shelf of the second rack, which was why the catalogue said *damp*.

The box was at the end.

Sillick Mill: administrative papers, disposal. Accession 1961/LW/44. A grey archive box, a foot and a half long, and on its end in the Museum's printed shelf-mark label, the same four lines the terminal had given him.

Elias put his hand on the lid. He had it. It was eleven inches away from the sheet a foreman had copied a list out of on the last morning the mill ever ran, and eleven inches away from a piece of paper that had been in a drawer in a woman's hand four miles away for thirty-one years, and that woman had spent nine years of letters trying to reach it.

"Open it," Ivo said.

"No."

"Elias—"

"I'm not opening it in an unauthorised lift on a Friday with you standing here and neither of us able to say why." He took his hand off the lid. "Neither of us is writing this down either. There's no facility to write anything down, that's the point of the room."

"That's the most frightening thing anybody's said to me all year."

They went up. Elias put his card on and the lamp went red behind them and the gate shut, and Ivo went to the shop, and Elias went to the security room and had four minutes alone with a fact.

He had it. It was eleven inches away, and it was legal to get to it, and it was not legal to get to it tonight, and there was no version of tonight that was.

He had a rule and a rule was a thing that could be worked around tomorrow, and he worked out, sitting at the desk, exactly how he would ask for a two-person retrieval of 1961/LW/44 with a reason, and it took him four minutes, and the reason he gave himself was that it would be easier if Ivo already knew.

Then he went to do the second thing, which was the harder one.

---

The payroll terminal is not the Museum's. It is a municipal terminal on a municipal network, bolted to a bracket in the staff office, and the reason it is in the staff office at all is that the guard office does the hours reconciliation against the bureau's monthly statement, and to do the reconciliation you have to be able to look up a name.

Every query on it is logged. He knew that. He had known that for two years, the way you know the fire exits have signs on them. He sat down at ten to eight on a Friday night and typed a name in, having understood the cost of it before he did it, and did it anyway, which he wrote down in his own notebook afterwards as the first item on a list he was not going to call a list.

> CASE, WENDEL. GRADE 2.

The system thought about it for a very long time. Then:

> No current record.
> No archived record (files to 1974).
> 1 suggestion: surname in similar index — CASE, W. (deceased records, restricted, 1998–2008)

Nothing. Not a person. Not a leaver. Nineteen years of a grade-two night porter, and the payroll terminal for a building with five floors and two hundred staff had nothing, and had never had anything, and had gone looking for him in a restricted index going back to 1998 and come back empty-handed.

Elias read that twice. He was aware of the shape of what he was reading, because he had spent Wednesday night constructing it out of a plastic folder of four lines, and it was worse than the shape. It was worse because it was *systematic*. A leaver has a leaver's record. A leaver's record has a date and a reason code and a line in a book in a filing cabinet, and a leaver's record is the one document in the world a payroll department cannot afford to be without, because the leaver's record is what stops a pension being paid twice.

Wendel Case did not have a leaver's record. He had never been recorded as having gone.

Elias thought about the way that had been done. Somebody had gone into a system with a login, and found a line, and removed the line, and had not been asked to write a reason, and the system had not complained, because a line that has never been written has nothing to check itself against. It had happened on a Tuesday eighteen months ago, and nobody had written down when on the Tuesday, and there had been no second copy, and there had been no meeting, and the person who did it had not needed to have ever been in the same room as the man whose name they took out.

Two books. A line taken out of one and not out of the other, because the second one was held by the city and the city had not been told.

He wrote the reconciling sentence into his notebook in the form he would need to be able to use in front of a person who did not want to hear it:

> The bureau took his pension and his union for eighteen months because nobody told the bureau.
> The Museum's line went in and stayed gone, and no one ever wrote down the reason.
> He is proof that two systems can hold opposite facts about one man for eighteen months and neither of them be lying.

And then, underneath it, the part that had been sitting in him since Tuesday night and which he could not make into a sentence that helped:

> I have no document that says he was here. I have a pay stub for a man who might be anywhere.

---

He was still sitting with that when the duty manager's phone rang on the security desk, and then hers on the extension, and then the internal one that rang the security room directly, which it did four times a day and never for a good reason.

She said, "Elias. Director wants you. First corridor. Now, please."

Anja Ward came out of the first-floor office with a folder under her arm and stopped walking eleven feet away from Elias, so that he had to come to her, and she looked at him, and said:

"You've had a long week."

"Ma'am."

"You've had the door, and a note on plinth thirty-four, and a fault, and a Sunday." She said it as a list of facts and offered him nothing to agree or disagree with. "Sit down. Do not sit down, actually, we've got ten minutes before I'm in the van for a dinner thing, so stand."

The corridor had a bench in it and it had a window with the blind half down and, at the end, the door of the director's office with frosted glass and a brass plate.

"I've read your access log for Wednesday," she said.

"Of course you have."

"You don't have a recording of it. There's no camera on the level-two landing and I've stopped asking the Board for one." She put the folder under her arm. "So I'm going to tell you what I know and you're going to tell me if I'm wrong, and then we're going to do something about a thing which is, on paper, very small."

"Ma'am."

"In the small hours of Thursday you were at the Hall of Former Faiths door, which is locked, on a card that had opened nothing and logged nothing useful. At 02:05 you were on the first floor in the label room, which is a conservation workspace, and there is a camera on that lintel. At 02:20 you were on level two in the archive corridor, which is a restricted door and logs. At 02:41 you were at the service entrance." She turned her head. "The Hall's master key went out in a conservator's name at 18:52 and came back at 23:52, before any of it. So at twenty-six minutes past one that hall was shut, and locked, and the key was on its hook, and you were outside it. I have checked twice, because I wanted to be sure I was wrong the first time."

Elias said nothing. There was nothing to say to that, and the one thing in his life he had never once done in this building was lie to a person who had come out of an office to say a thing to him in a corridor.

"That key is a Master key," she said. "There are two in this city. One is mine. And the other one is in a box in the safes in the basement of a building in Northglass belonging to a firm of solicitors who represent people who are aggrieved about this Museum, and it has been in that box since 1994, and the last person who signed it out of their own safe was in 1991." She let that sit. "So when I say that a night key and not a Master key opened a locked gallery at one in the morning, I am not accusing you of theft. I am telling you that I do not like the shape of a week, and that I have a duty to say so out loud, and that the moment I say it out loud it stops being mine and starts being a record. And a record of that shape is a question somebody will ask me on Monday, and I would rather ask you first."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Two more things and then I have to go." She held up a finger. "One. There is an instruction on file, in restricted, dated eighteen years ago, that reorganised the card access to the Quiet Wing corridor, and it is the only restricted document in this building that predates me, and I have read it, and it is entirely mundane, and it is signed by a name that I cannot find in any list of people who have ever worked here. Not the payroll list, not the leaver list, not the old binders, not the Registrar's historic staff file. A name that does not appear. I have assumed for eighteen years that it is a contractor's signatory."

"And the second thing."

"The second thing is a work order." She handed him a single sheet, and it was real, and it was dated Friday afternoon at 16:20, and it authorised an outage on the Quiet Wing corridor reader, door seven, for a window of 23:40 to 00:30, and it was signed at the foot by a director, and the log reference at the top was a systems reference and not a name. The number was 4412-S.

"It's logged off the shop terminal," Anja Ward said. "Which means it is logged under a systems technician's login, which is normal and is not anybody's fault except his."

"He's on it at seven tomorrow morning."

"He's on it at seven tomorrow morning, and so are you, and so is Saturday's school group at eleven, which is forty-one children, two teachers, and an office on the Hill that has been extremely helpful to us and asks a great many questions." She was already walking. "I have authorised a fifty-minute window on a failing door reader because I would rather pay for a swap than explain a card failure to a child. I would like you to consider whether that decision of mine is the sort of decision that puts a person in a corridor, and I would like you to consider it *before* tonight and not afterwards."

The van was late. She got into it.

"Elias."

"Ma'am."

"Are you sleeping?"

"Reasonably, ma'am."

"You've got a widow's mite under each eye that I could sign off in a report, and I've had exactly four nights of proper sleep this quarter, and I am going to say something now and I am not going to refer to it again, because I have already referred to it." She looked at him without any blinking at all. "If you have found something you cannot account for, I would far rather have it on my desk on Monday in a bad paragraph than find it in an insurance file. That is the whole of what I have to say and I have said it."

And she was gone down the corridor in a coat that had cost more than his month, and Elias Venn stood under a half-lowered blind holding a piece of paper that said 23:40 to 00:30, and understood that he had just been given permission by the one person in the building who had the power to take it away, and that he was going to spend the whole night trying to make that permission into a wall.

---

He had been on the level-two landing on Wednesday at 02:20, which was true and was in the log in his own name, on a card event he had put there himself, for nothing, at five past two in the morning, because he had wanted to know. The Master key had been on its hook since 23:52, which was also true, and the night key that opens public galleries and nothing else had opened the Hall of Former Faiths at 01:26 without a word of complaint, because that is the job of a night key, and because the door it opened was not a restricted door, and because nobody in the history of this building has ever needed a restricted door to be anything other than a door.

And that, standing at a terminal at ten to nine on a Friday night with a dead headache and a piece of paper in his pocket, was the thing about the week he could not put anywhere. Every single thing he had done since Tuesday had been possible, and every single thing had left a record he could live with, and the reason was not him. It was the building. Somebody had built this wing so that a man could stand at a locked door for as long as he liked and never once be asked a question about it, and the same somebody had signed the wing's card access over eighteen years ago in a name that did not exist, and Elias understood that he was not the first man this building had arranged to be a problem for, and that the arrangement was eighteen years older than he was.

He had not lied to her. He had made a decision, in a corridor, at half past eight, to answer a woman who had been decent to him on purpose, and the decision had not felt like trust. It had felt like the discovery of a third option in a choice he had believed was two-way.

He did not open the instruction either. It was restricted, it was on the second floor in a locked cage, he had a card that would not open the cage, and he had a name on the front of it that he could not put anywhere.

He also did not ask about it. That was the decision the whole evening had actually been about, and he made it deliberately at ten to eight on a Friday night, standing at a terminal, with a corridor and a woman in it still waiting on the other side of the building, and the reason he made it was not integrity and it was not fear.

The reason was that the moment he asked Anja Ward about a name on an eighteen-year-old instruction, she would have to go and look. And if she went and looked, then within a week somebody would know that a night guard had asked about the Quiet Wing instruction. And he did not yet know what the answer was, and he was extremely clear, standing there, about the fact that he did not want anyone to know that he was asking.

That was the whole of it. It was not a plan. It was a man who had worked out that the fastest way to lose the only thing he had was to move before he knew what he was moving towards, and who had therefore decided, quite calmly, to wait — and who was entirely aware, in a low grey voice underneath, that waiting is what he had been doing since Tuesday and that a wait of a certain length becomes a decision to never ask.

He put the work order in his pocket. He read the terminal. The 1961/LW/44 shelf-mark was still bay nine.

Ivo came out of the shop at four to nine with his toolbag and stopped in the doorway of the security room and said, "So I've got a fifty-minute window on Friday and I've got to strip the frame and flash a new card over from the terminal on the far side of the site, so the corridor's going to be down for a bit. Do you want the ticket number, or—"

"I've got the paper. Anja signed it out."

"Good." Ivo dropped the bag. "That's a bit different, isn't it. Last time I had a go at that door it was my fault and an entry in a book."

"It is a bit different."

"Right." He was looking at his own hands. "Elias, I'm going to ask you something and you're going to say no."

"No."

"I hadn't finished it."

"You were going to ask me whether I'd stand in the corridor while it was down."

"I was going to ask you whether you'd mind if I went down the stairs to the shop and left a door down for about fifty minutes on a Friday night because that's the job, and it's the last of the job, and I have to be in at seven." He pulled his jacket tighter. "I said no in my head, by the way. About four seconds ago. So there's no question, and you're welcome, and don't say anything about it."

Elias said nothing about it.

He had a piece of paper in his pocket that said 23:40 to 00:30, signed by a director, and a technician on the floor who had decided, on his own, without being asked, to say nothing to anyone about leaving a restricted door standing open for fifty minutes on a director's signature.

And the rule he had written for himself on Wednesday — that asking would have made it a favour, and that he wanted a fault he could have found on his own — sat exactly where he had put it, doing exactly what he had built it to do, which was to keep him from asking anybody for anything at all.

He went home up the hill in the rain at ten past nine with a piece of paper in his pocket that a director had signed, and a corridor in his head, and a number in a building he had been in for two years that he still could not reach, and by Saturday night he would have done all three of the things that were going to cost him, and he knew it, and he went to bed anyway, because that is what a man does on a Friday when the rota says Tuesday to Saturday.
