# Chapter 0037 — The Bench by the Door

He got to the laundrette at twenty past eight on Monday the sixteenth of November and told his mother he was going to open the letter, out loud, in the doorway, before he was through it, which is a thing he has never done and did because he had worked out over two days that if he said it in the doorway then it would be a decision with a time on it, and he wanted it to be one.

Helen Venn was behind the counter with a sheet of large paper and a pen and eleven machines going, and she did not look up.

"All right," she said.

"That's it? All right?"

"What would you like me to say, love." She wrote a figure on the sheet. "I've had that envelope on a shelf since the seventh of October and you have been in this room eleven times in five weeks and you have not once put your hand on the shelf, and if you were going to open it you were going to open it about nine months ago, and if you were not going to open it then you were not going to open it at half past eight on a Monday, and either way I have not got anything to say about it until you've had it in your hands and put it down again."

He stood in the doorway for about four seconds and then he went and sat on the bench by the door, which is where he has sat since he was a boy, and the machines ran, and the boiler ticked, and he did not put his hand on the shelf.

The shelf is above the boiler at the back and it is about five feet up and it needs the small stepladder that lives behind the door, and there is one thing on it, and it is a white envelope with his father's name on it in his mother's hand, and the paper has gone soft along the fold from being taken out and put back so many times that the fold holds itself.

Eighteen years. The fold has learned to hold itself.

He looked at it from the bench for about two minutes and then he stopped, because he had done it about forty times since 2006 and he knows exactly what it looks like and there is nothing in it tonight that he has not already had.

"What are you doing?" his mother said.

"Counting machines."

"There's eleven."

"There's eleven," he said, "and there's about nine people in this street with a machine going and there are four houses above this one and I've never once known how many of them there are, and I've never once asked, and I did it just now without deciding to and it's the third time this week I've done it with a room instead of with a thing."

His mother put the pen down.

"Well," she said. "That's new."

---

He got the tram up the hill at half past nine and was on the desk at ten on Monday the sixteenth of November.

The day book had his own entry in it from Sunday, in his own hand, with a time, about a drawer, and it had not been queried. Nobody queried it. That is what a day book is for: a record of an event, initialled, not a judgement, and a man who puts a line in a day book is a man who has done a thing, and a day book does not ask him why.

He did the dead letters. There were three hundred and five of them and it was a Monday.

At about eleven he went and stood in the loading bay with a clipboard and did a job he had not done in three weeks, which is a bay check, and which takes twenty minutes and produces nothing, and which he does because a bay check is a man going into a room and looking at it and writing what is there, and it is the closest thing in this building to what he actually is.

Bay 9, sub-level B, the old frame store.

Cage 2, where it has stood since the first of November 2024, and where it stood for the fifty years before that, and where it was standing in 2019 when a company bought the freehold off a receiver and did not know it was there.

Custody Disc 42, red, on the front rail. Padlock QA/1187. Eleven items, not opened. A grey polythene bag over the front rail against the dust, which the last bay check had found and which had been reported and had been dealt with by a man from facilities with a roll of tape.

It is a piece of furniture. It is the most expensive piece of furniture in this building and there is no column anywhere in any document in this city in which a piece of furniture can say what it is worth.

He wrote the time and the disc number and the padlock number and the fact of the polythene and the fact that he had not touched the cage, and then he copied all five lines into the notebook, because that is what the notebook is for, and because it was the first thing he had put into it in five weeks, and he did not think about that at the time and has thought about it every day since.

> **Bay check, Mon 16 Nov, 11:20. Not opened. Not to be opened before Fri 27 Nov, 16:00, in the addressee's own front room, per the undertaking of the conciliation service. The bay check is a check of a cage and this is a cage and the cage is a cage, and on Friday it stops being a cage for about two hours and I am not going to be in the room.**

And then, in the loading bay, at a quarter past eleven on a Monday, with the clipboard on his knee, Elias Venn did the thing he had been not doing since the sixth of October, which is to take out the notebook and go through the list of what there is in it, and to ask the only question that has ever been attached to it, which is whether any of it is true.

The notebook is a pocket notebook. It has a hundred and fifteen pages and about a hundred and ten of them are written in his own hand in biro, and there are times on almost all of them, and there is one entry in two hands and he has never been able to make it sit in an order.

Here is the whole of what is in it, as of Monday the sixteenth of November, and he wrote it out for the first time, in a loading bay, on the back of a bay-check sheet because there was nothing else to write on:

> **1. A kitchen in a flat in Ferry Bank, 14 Oct, about ninety seconds, on a lamp, no question, no cost, no card, no witness. Mrs Sarn was in the room.**
> **2. The case of the bell, loading bay, 00:52–01:01, Sun 25 Oct. One question, nine minutes, four seconds of a name.**
> **3. A sealed box in a cage, west range service corridor, 26 Oct, about ninety seconds, no question, no bound, no cost, no card, no witness. On my own.**
> **4. A public gallery, 7 Nov, 11:12, one question, two and a half minutes, fifty-one people, a nosebleed, and a child.**
> **5. Four seconds of a name, from 2, which I cannot identify and never will.**
> **6. The position on a peephole, 15 Oct, and the fact that I have been in this corridor more times in five weeks than the systems print can account for, and the fact that I have not asked anybody for an explanation and have not been asked.**

And then, at the bottom, on a fresh line, because he had known for about three weeks that it was going to be there and had never written it:

> **7. The notebook itself. A hundred and fifteen pages. Nobody in this building has read it. Mara Iles has never seen it. She has asked for it and I have not said no, and that is worse, and I have been not-saying-no to a woman I have four times promised there is no more private channel with.**

He read the seven lines and put the pen down on the cage rail and left it there for about a minute, and did not touch anything, and a bay check is twenty minutes and he had done it in nine.

That is the whole of what he has to give Mara Iles. Not a fact. Four readings, a cost, a corridor, and a book.

And the promise he made on the eighteenth of October, on a bench by a laundrette door in Low Ward, in about eleven words, was that the letter came after he had told Mara everything, and the reason he made the promise in that form is that "after I have told Mara everything" is a thing with a shape and can be looked at, and it is not a promise to *do* anything about the letter, and he knows that, and she knew that, and she said, "good, that'll keep."

And it has kept, and it is doing exactly what he built it to do, and it is a rule he wrote for himself in October, in a laundrette, at ten o'clock on a Sunday morning, and it is a good rule, and it is the same rule as before, with a better coat on.

---

Doreen Sarn was at the table with the fire on at ten past one.

There was a plate on the sideboard and under the plate a registered letter face down, and there was a tumbler on the sill with a dead carnation in it that she had not thrown out, and there was a cardigan over the back of a chair and a pinafore over that, and a hardback cash book on the table with a rubber band round it.

She had been expecting him. She said so, which unsettled him, and he could not have said afterwards for how long, and then she explained: "You said Saturday. I've been expecting you since Saturday and I've decided that a man who says Saturday and comes on Monday is a man who is doing something in between, and I'm not going to ask you what."

"You can ask me."

"I can and I won't, and it's the same favour you've done me twice." She poured him tea without being asked, from a pot that had been on. "Now. You've come with a thing on your face. You've had a bad weekend and it's not the mill and it's not the paper, because I heard about the paper on Saturday and I have had two days and I have not thought about it very hard, and I told you that, and I meant it. So it's not the paper. What is it."

"You told me something in October and I've had it for four weeks and I want to check I've got it right."

She put her cup down.

"A coat pocket," she said. "Go on."

"A coat pocket is a book with one reader. And one reader is a liability."

"That's what I said."

"You said it about Cliff Sowerby's letter," Elias said. "You've had it since the Wednesday of the week before last — since the thirtieth of October, in a coat, in a flat on Sillick Lane, and you put it in there, and nobody has ever told Cliff Sowerby that there is a letter for him in his own coat, and you have known for seventeen days."

"Seventeen," said Doreen Sarn. "I had it as eighteen on Friday and I have not told anybody that I had it wrong."

"You said it in the third week of October. To me. At this table."

"I did." She did not look away. "And you've had it four weeks and you've come to check you've got it right, and you've got it right, and now go on, because you've not come to this table to say a thing back to me that I said to you."

"I'm going to tell you a date," Elias said. "And then I'm going to say the thing you've just said back at me, and it's not going to be polite, and you can put me out."

"Frankfurt."

"The sealed envelope. Item five on the 1974 receipt — *envelope of loose papers, sealed, contents not examined.* It's in the cage in bay 9, in this building, under a red disc and a padlock, and it has not been opened since the seventh of November 1974." He made himself keep going at the same rate. "It's yours. It's very probably yours: a sheet folded in three with a carbon copy of a pay sheet on it, and you said so on Thursday and you said you copied it out at your own table in November 1974, and the item says *contents not examined*, which means nobody in fifty years has looked, and which means we do not know that it is yours, and we are going to open it in your front room on Friday the twenty-seventh of November at four o'clock in the afternoon, with the gas fire on, with two people from the conciliation service and a supervisor and a camera, and there will be no Museum employee in the room, and I will not be in it."

Doreen Sarn did not move for about four seconds. Then she said:

"Say the last part again."

"There will be no Museum employee in the room. I will not be in it. I made that argument in October and Mara Iles agreed with it and the conciliation service put it in the undertaking, and it is in writing, and it is the only thing in this business that anybody has got right in fifty years."

"You've not told her you've come here today."

"No."

"No," she agreed. "Well. That's the first thing you've said in two minutes that I believe, and I'll tell you why." She sat back. "Everybody who's ever come in here about that envelope has wanted to be in the room. The man in the good coat wanted to be in the room. The one from the claims office in Northglass wanted to be in the room and had a camera and a reason. A woman from the council in October wanted to be in the room and was told she couldn't and went off like a firework in my front doorway. And you have come at a quarter past one on a Monday and told me a date and told me you are not coming, and you have not asked me for one single thing."

She put her hand flat on the cash book.

"Now. The thing you came to say and you've been circling round for ten minutes. My coat pocket, and one reader being a liability." She waited. "Say it."

"You said a book with one reader is a book that can lose itself. Cliff Sowerby's letter is in his coat pocket. If he had died on the Sunday it would have gone into a house and been thrown out, and nobody would ever have known there was anything in it."

"That's right."

"And the book on the table in front of you is a list of one household, and you have not told one person in nine years where the shelf is."

"I haven't."

"And that is the same thing, and you have known it since you said it, and you have had four weeks."

"I've had four weeks," said Doreen Sarn, "and I have not done a single thing about it, and you're going to tell me that's not a coincidence and you're going to be right."

Then she stood up, which she does not do easily, and got the plate off the sideboard, and put the registered letter face up on the table, and left it there.

"That's yours," she said. "It came on the Wednesday before last and I've had it face down for eighteen days and I want you to understand that I have done that on purpose and not out of spite. There's a thing in it that I have been not knowing for three weeks, and I have earned three weeks, and I'll open it on Friday night after the box has been open and I'll open it at that table, and if I have to sit down halfway through I'll sit down."

She pushed it across the table with two fingers.

"Take it away."

"Mrs Sarn—"

"No. Take it away, and take it to whoever you want to take it to, and don't tell me what it says, because if you tell me what it says then I have known and my three weeks is a different three weeks. It's got my name on the outside and it came to a first floor in Ferry Bank and it was signed for by a niece of mine who's post, and it is the first letter anybody from that building has sent me that was not a solicitor." She pushed it a second time. "And while we are on names: there is a list of eleven of yours that went round this town in October with a hearing date on the top of it, and one of the names on it is a man at number fourteen who has been on my list since the Tuesday, and you'll not tell me about that either, and I am not asking."

"I'm not going to tell you about that."

"Good." She sat down. "Now go and be useful to somebody, because my last two visitors this month have been a man with a caution and a woman from the council, and you've been the only one who's told me a date."

---

He did not tell Mara Iles about the registered letter, and he did not tell her about the date, and he did not tell her on the Tuesday either, and that is the sixth thing in three weeks and there are seven lines in a notebook in his coat and not one of them is in a file in this building.

He was at the building until a quarter to nine. He did the dead letters, all three hundred and five, because it was a Monday and a clerk's is a slow job.

At about four o'clock he went up to level two and stood in the archive corridor for six minutes, twice, because on Wednesday the eleventh of November the same corridor had four things in it and five on the second run, and a figure with a word in front of it is not a figure, and he wanted to know whether the building had got louder, and it had, and he was glad, and he was frightened.

He got to the laundrette at twenty past nine on the sixteenth of November, and the machines were going, and the letter was on the shelf, and he did not get the stepladder.

He did not get the stepladder for about four minutes, and then he sat down on the bench, and then he said the thing out loud to the room, because he had said it in a doorway and had been answered *all right* and had not been answered.

"I'm not opening it tonight."

"I know."

"You know because I said it at eight o'clock and I've been here nine hours and I'm saying it again, which is a man doing a thing twice because the first time didn't take."

"No," said his mother, taking a machine out and folding it on the trolley. "I'm saying I know because you've not touched it since the seventh of October and you have come in here eleven times and this is the twelfth, and if you were going to open it you would have done it in the first ten seconds, and the reason you are not going to open it tonight is not the promise you made me in October. It's the reason you made me the promise in October. They've come back round to the same place and you have not even noticed, love, because you have been very busy getting better at saying it."

Elias Venn sat on a bench in a laundrette in Low Ward and looked at the front of a machine with a woman in it going round and round.

"That's a nasty thing to say to somebody at nine o'clock on a Monday."

"It's a true thing to say to somebody at nine o'clock on a Monday."

"It's true and it's not the whole of it."

"Then it is today," said his mother, "and today is the one you get."

He sat there for about forty minutes and she did the sum and he did not help and she did not ask him to, and at about ten she started the machines down one at a time, which she does at ten, and which she has done every night for about thirty years, and which he has watched about two thousand times.

And at about five to ten, with the last machine still going behind her and the boiler ticking, she said:

"Which is it, then. The promise."

"The promise is real," he said. "I've not told her everything. She has never seen the notebook. There are four things in it and one of them is a woman in a kitchen in Ferry Bank and two of them are a box and a bell and there is a hundred and fifteen pages of it and I have promised her there is no more private channel and I have been sitting on a private channel the size of a coat pocket for five weeks. And if I open this letter tonight, then that promise is the only reason I'm not, and in about a year I will have got good at having reasons."

"And you'll still not have told her."

"I'll still not have told her."

She shut the last machine off and the noise went and the room got very quiet, which it always does, and which he has never once been in the room for at the end of.

"Then you've done the right thing tonight," said Helen Venn, "and I want to say it out loud so that you have to have heard it, and then I want to say the other thing and I want you to hear both of them about four inches apart, because you are thirty-two and you are my only one and I get one at a time."

"Yes."

"The right thing is that you did not do it. That is the growth and I know it is a growth because you have never done it before. In five weeks you have done six things that were hard and this is the first one where the hard thing was to do nothing, and there is more of that in the next ten years of your life than there is of the other kind, and that is not a thing anybody tells you, and I am telling you."

Elias Venn did not say anything.

"And the other thing," said his mother, "is that you are thirty-two and you have a notebook with a hundred and fifteen pages in it and a woman of twenty-eight in a registrar's office who asked you for it once and did not ask you twice, and you have made a promise out of the not-asking. And in about eleven months she is going to be the Director of this building, and she is going to be asked by somebody with a clipboard why a man she promoted had a private notebook for five weeks, and she will not be able to say *he promised me the right order was after he told me everything*, because that is not an answer anybody can use at eleven o'clock in a room with a projector." She put the last of the coins in the drawer. "So do the other thing. Not the letter. The other thing. Take it in and shut the door and give it to her and watch her face while she reads the first page, and then never do it again for the rest of your life."

She turned the light out in the shop.

"And she will read the whole thing," said Helen Venn, "and she will not make a fuss, and she will be the best person in this city to have read it, and you will have wasted five weeks being frightened of a woman holding a pen."
