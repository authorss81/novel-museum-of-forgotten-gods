# Chapter 0034 — Four Hundred and Ninety-Four

The telephone in the loading-bay office rang at twelve minutes past seven on Friday the thirteenth of November and Elias Venn picked it up on the second ring, which he had never done in his life with that telephone, and it was Arkwright.

"It's the dry run," she said. "I ring you on a dry run the morning of every booked collection, and then I ring you when it's done. That's the job."

"How long have you got collections on Fridays?"

"Every Friday for nine years, and twice on a month-end, and the month-end is always a Friday because the merchant runs a five-day week and Monday is a shut." She had a radio going behind her. "Eight o'clock. It is eight o'clock, forty minutes of work, and gone by twenty to nine."

"Hold it."

"I can't hold it, love. It's a booked collection and the booking's on the sheet and the driver's on his way and there's four men coming and about forty minutes of work." A pause. "You know what would hold it."

"I know what would hold it."

"It hasn't come in the night."

"No."

"Then no," said Miss Arkwright, and she was not unkind about it, and that was the entire conversation.

---

He was on the quay at ten to eight and he was not working and he was the only person on the hardstanding who was not working, and there is nothing to do on a quay but stand where you can see the bay number and say nothing to anybody.

Bay 4 had its door open and the door had been open since Wednesday morning.

There was a vehicle in the middle of the hardstanding that was not there on Thursday. It was a long flat-backed thing with a cab and a lifting tail and four men in it in overalls and hi-vis, and they came out of the cab and one of them put a sheet of polythene down on the concrete and one of them started rolling two wheel skips out of it, and the skips were the yellow kind, and there were four of them, and they were on castors.

Four skips for four hundred and eighty-two kilos. That was not right either. Four skips is four skips.

Miss Arkwright came out with her clipboard and a headset round her neck and stopped when she saw him, and did not say anything about him, and said:

"Bit early for a dry run, aren't you."

"You did ring me."

"I did ring you." She turned the clipboard round so he could see it and put her thumb under a line, and the line was the collection line and the line underneath it said **RELEASE TO MERCHANT — GENERAL, AS PER SCHEDULE**, and there was a time in the box at the end of it, and the time was **08:00**, and it had been typed on Monday. "There it is. Signed off on the Monday. You can read it and you can copy it and it will not help you, because the sign-off is correct."

"I know it's correct."

"Everybody who rings me says that. It is correct and it is the whole of your problem and it will be the whole of your problem in a hearing as well."

They did it fast. That was the thing. Elias Venn had been braced for something slow — for a man to open something, for a man to look, for a man to hesitate — and the four of them did not hesitate for one second.

The first skip went in empty. Then the lot-three paper, in bundles, the whole lot, all of it, off the pallet and into the yellow in about ninety seconds, and a man up on the tail throwing it down, and a man at the back not letting anything go past the wheel stop. Lot one, the sacks, went whole, six of them, lifted. Lot four was the trolley, and the oddments, and the bicycle, and the two wooden trays, and a man said to another man, "Want the trolley back?", and the other man said, "Nah," and the trolley went in.

Lot two did not go in. Lot two was the books — two pallets of them, strapped, and the pallets were about four feet high and there is no way of getting a strapped pallet of bound books over a wheel stop into a skip without a tail lift and a decision about a tail lift, and the man with the clipboard had a decision about a tail lift and made it in about four seconds.

"That one's the merchant's other run," he said. "Same firm, different collection. They come back Tuesday for the books. That's a fortnightly."

"Does it go to the same place?"

"Same works." He wrote something in a box. "Doesn't matter, it's all one yard to them. They weigh it all on the same weighbridge and it's all in the same shed."

Elias Venn stood on a hardstanding in Northglass at about a quarter past eight on Friday morning and understood, standing there, with a notebook out, that a book pallet going to a different collection on a different day was the only thing in the whole of that bay that was going to survive the week, and that it was going to survive for reasons to do with a wheel stop and a tail lift and a man's four seconds.

---

He had to write the times down. He wrote them on the back of a dead letter envelope because his notebook was in his coat and it was raining and he is a man who writes things down and cannot stop.

> **08:04** — Lot 3 away. Bundles, all of it, ninety seconds. **08:09** — Lot 1, six sacks, whole. **08:11** — Lot 4, trolley, oddments, bicycle, 2 trays, "Nah."
> **08:14** — Lot 2, books, NOT TAKEN. Tail-lift decision, four seconds. Fortnightly, Tuesday.
> **08:22** — Weighing, bay scale. Four skips, gross. 08:26 — signed.

The man who signed was called Renwick and he was about fifty and he did it on the tail of a vehicle with a clipboard on a wheel and a pen on a string, and he came over the hardstanding afterwards and used the same pen to fill in four boxes on his own sheet on the driver's door, and he was pleasant, and he was working, and he had done four collections that week.

Elias Venn said, "Do you know what's in it?"

"No."

"Do you want to know?"

"No," said Renwick, pleasantly, working. "I sign for the weight. I've got eleven thousand of these a year and I have never opened one and I'm not going to start. If there's a sack in there I don't want, that's a phone call to the office, and the office has got a sheet, and the sheet's got a seal number on it, and that's how it works." He put the pen in his pocket and got back up on the tail. "You'll be the museum? You want a bit of the paperwork?"

"Yes."

"Give us your number, I'll post you a weighbridge docket."

"You weigh it on your own bridge?"

"Every load. The yard's on the approach, you drive up it, they weigh you over and under. It's law for a reason." He got a card out. "That's the docket number off that one, actually, if you want to write it down now, because it takes four days to come out of that office and it comes out with the month on it."

And so, at ten past nine on Friday the thirteenth of November, a man called Renwick wrote a weighbridge docket number on the back of a dead letter envelope in a hand that was not his, and gave it to a man who had asked him whether he wanted to know what was in his vehicle, and got back up on the tail, and did not know and was never going to know, and had a yard-load of somebody's working life waiting to be weighed on an approach four miles away, and was going to weigh it, and write it in a box, and go home.

Elias Venn watched it come down off the tail, go back up, and take the gate at 08:40, and it was forty minutes' work, and there was a man on the concrete the whole time whose job that was.

---

He had until five o'clock on Thursday. It was twenty to nine on Friday and the thing that would have stopped it was a piece of A4 in a bonded store in Northglass with a letter of authority on it and the signature of a person who did not work there, and there was no version of the last fourteen hours in which that piece of paper had existed.

He did the correct things. He wants that on the page because it is the only part of Friday morning he is going to be able to look at in six months, and because the correct things are the whole of what he has got.

He got the consignment sheet off the top of the Monday file — it was a Monday, she had posted it up, which is a thing bonded stores do. He got the container seal number, which he had anyway, from Thursday, in a notebook, in his own hand, from a board on a quay. He got the number off the 1974 carbon: 1961/LW/44.

Then he telephoned the mobile number on the sheet, which was a mobile number, at 08:44, from the desk in the front lobby where anybody could see him doing it.

It rang eleven times and a man answered, and the man was brisk and pleasant and professional and did not know anything.

"Morning. Brill's site office — actually, the site office is closed, the site's been cleared, we're down to a compound and a fence. I'm doing the compound and the fence. Can I help?"

"This is about a consignment from that site into a bonded store at Broomhead Reach, and I need to speak to somebody who can authorise—"

"Yeah, the paperwork went. All of it went. The last of it went on a lorry on the Friday and the office is empty and I took the keys in on the Monday." Wind on the line. "Are you the client?"

"I'm the museum."

"Right." A pause, and the man was not being obstructive, he was doing the thing a man does when he is on a compound in November. "Look, mate, if there's a dispute then it's not me to sort out, I've got a number for the solicitors that did the clearance and I can give you that—"

"Could you hold the collection? It's a consignment in bond and there's an item on it that has—"

"There's nothing I can hold. I'm not the company. I clear sites. It's the one thing I'm good at and the one thing I don't have a pen for." A beep. "That's them ringing me back, I have to go. Ring the solicitors, they're in Lincoln's Lane, they'll know."

He rang the number and it rang out.

At 08:56 he rang the Museum's own switchboard from the front desk of the Museum, which is the single most embarrassing thing available to a man who works in a building, and Priest put him through to the Director's office and a woman's voice said the Director was not in until nine and would she take a message, and Elias Venn said no thank you, and hung up, and stood at a counter in a lobby for about a minute and a half.

Because at 08:56, and he worked it out at about nine o'clock and it is the only clear thinking he did all day, a letter of authority from a Director to a bonded store in Northglass about a consignment of paper to a paper merchant at eight o'clock on a Friday morning is not a thing that anybody obtains in four minutes, and it is not a thing she would have granted at four minutes' notice, and there is no clause in the whole of this business that says otherwise.

He had done the correct things, and they had taken from half past eight until nearly nine, and they had not worked, and he had done every one of them after the van was through the gate, which he was going to go over for the rest of the day, and nobody had done anything wrong, and the material had gone at twenty to eight.

---

He telephoned Mara Iles at twenty past nine from the front desk, and she took it, and he said:

""It's gone. This morning. The collection was at eight and it went at twenty to eight and I was standing on it when it went."

She did not say anything for about four seconds. Elias Venn, who has spent a month in a building with a woman who writes everything down, has never heard four seconds of that silence from her and did not know what it was.

"Give me the accession number," she said.

"Nineteen sixty one, slash L W, slash forty-four."

"And a description."

"A bound wage book for October 1974. The mill's own. Pay office. Two hundred and ninety-one pages in a leather binding with a printed label on the front. It is item four on page one of a bond sheet and it came out of a container in a bay on a quay on Wednesday morning with a pencil against it, and on Friday morning at eight o'clock the pay office's own posted wage sheets for the last eight weeks of 1974 went into a skip on a quay in Northglass on a vehicle belonging to a man called Renwick, who signs about eleven thousand of those a year and has never opened one, and those sheets were the only corroboration in this city of what was paid on the final run."

"Say the last part again."

"He asked me if I wanted to know what was in it and I asked him if he wanted to know and he said no, and that is a professional answer from a man doing his job, and he is not the reason."

"No," she said. "He isn't."

"Give me a minute," said Mara Iles, "and do not say anything else on this telephone, I have to look at something."

He stood at the front desk of a public museum on a Friday morning with a receiver in his hand and looked at a counter, and about fifty people came through the doors of that building between 09:20 and 09:26, and a pound a head, and a poster of a weighbridge at four foot six, and he counted them without meaning to and got fifty-one and put it away.

"Right," she said. "Two things, and I am going to say them in the wrong order because I have not had a cup of tea. First: you were right, and you did it correctly, and there is nothing else available to anybody in this city at a quarter to nine in the morning about a consignment in bond at a store in Northglass, and I have checked, and there is no clause. I have wanted there to be a clause for five weeks and there is not one, and the reason there is not one is that this Museum has never had a consignment anybody wanted to stop."

"And the second thing."

"The second thing is worse than the first and it is not your fault and it is not anybody's fault this morning, and it is the shape of this entire business." A pause. "Elias, I cannot file a claim to that paper because I cannot describe the paper. A claim wants an accession number and a description. I have got your accession number. I have got 'a bound wage book'. And I cannot put 'a bound wage book' in a letter to a paper merchant, because in eleven weeks a person will say *which one, out of how many, and how do you know, and show us the entry in your own collection register.* And the entry is not in our register. The entry is in the schedule of contents, and the schedule of contents is a document that says what this Museum holds and what it is insured against, and it has been empty since the eleventh of September, and it is empty because our records clerk retired in March and the post went to systems, and the only person in this building who has ever been able to describe what is in a room is a man who is barred from two of the rooms it is in."

The line went quiet for a moment, and there was a sound on it of a woman who has been a registrar for five years and is very good at her job putting a receiver down on a desk and picking it up again.

"Ring me at eleven," she said. "Not before. Go and do something useful for ninety minutes."

---

He did the dead letters. There were two hundred and ninety-four of them and it was a Friday, and a clerk's is a slow job, and a clerk's is the only kind of competence this building has ever allowed him without wanting a piece of paper at the end of it.

He did two hundred and sixty of them in ninety minutes, which is the best he has ever done, and at about 10:50 he stopped, because his hand was going, and because he had spent ninety minutes writing a hand that was not his own on the backs of envelopes about a number of sacks.

He telephoned Arkwright at twenty to eleven and she told him the rest of it, and she told it to him exactly as she would have told a haulier, because that is the job.

They got it on the yard weighbridge at about half past ten. Four skips, gross, tared against the vehicle, and the docket went out at 08:26 and the weight went on it at 10:31 and the docket comes out of their office in four days with the month on it.

"Four hundred and ninety-four," she said. "That's up on your bay figure. That'll be damp. It's been sat in a bay in that weather since the Wednesday, and damp paper is heavier than dry paper, and it will come in about the same as it went out, and their clerk will not think twice about it and he is right not to."

"Four hundred and ninety-four."

"Four hundred and ninety-four." She let a second go past. "You know what you've got there, don't you, and I'm not going to say it, because if I say it I'm a bonded goods clerk with a document in front of her and I've been in a bay since six. But I'll tell you what I *can* tell you, because it's a fact and facts are my trade. There is a sack list. Every load out of bond is broken down to sacks and pallets and it's on the sheet and the sheet's got the number and I've sent you a copy because I always send a copy, and the whole load is broken down to eleven lines, which is six sacks of lot one, the bundled paper of lot three, and the lot-four trolley, and the pallet of books is a twelfth line, and those twelve lines are the only things I can tell you about it. Eleven lines and a pallet. And no contents. Because the sheet doesn't have contents, because the sheet has a consignment line, and the consignment line says general, as per schedule, and that is the whole of what was ever written down about four hundred and ninety-four kilos of anybody's anything."

"Right," said Elias Venn.

"And the other thing," said Miss Arkwright. "You'll ring the consignor's solicitor. Somebody will. And you should know before you do that I have been doing this for six years and I have never once been able to tell you whether a thing in a bay in bond is a person's or a company's, and neither can the merchant, and neither can the driver, and I have watched three men in this city try to stop a paper collection in the last two years and I will tell you exactly how many of them managed it."

"How many?"

"None," said Miss Arkwright. "It isn't because of the paperwork. The paperwork's fine. It's because you have to say *that one* to somebody who is holding four hundred and ninety-four kilos of something, at eight o'clock in the morning, and everybody in that chain has about nine hundred loads in their head and the one you've got in yours is not the one they've got in theirs, and the only person in that chain who could have stopped it is a woman in a shed who has never known what was in a bay in her life and is not going to start on a Friday."

---

He had it on the page by twenty past two on the Friday afternoon, on a fresh page, because the page he had been using had four things on it and one of them was a street in Ferry Bank and one of them was four hundred and eighty-two, and he wanted them apart.

> **Fri 13 Nov.**
> **08:00 collection. 08:22 signed. 08:40 off the quay. 10:31 weighed at the yard on the approach. 494 kg gross, four skips, tared. Docket to follow, four days, month on it.**
> **What went: lot one (sacks, six), lot three (bundles), lot four (trolley, oddments, bicycle, two trays, all of it).**
> **What did not: lot two, books, two strapped pallets, merchant's fortnightly collection, Tuesday, same firm, same yard.**
> **Item four of the bay, the bound wage book for October 1974 with the pencil, IS ON THAT PALLET. Nothing I have been able to do in four days has moved it out of the fate of a tail lift and a man's four seconds.**
> **And the book is not what went. What went is the paper round it, and the paper is the mill's own posted wage sheets for the weeks of September and October 1974, and there are about two dozen of them, and they are the pay office's own sheets, and they are the only corroboration in this city of what was paid on the final run, and they went into a skip at eight o'clock this morning on a vehicle belonging to a man called Renwick.**
> **What I have now: a weighbridge docket number, a sack count of eleven, an accession number, and a description a solicitor cannot use.**
> **What I do not have: anything.**
> **Claim period: seven days from the collection. The twentieth of November. Nine newspapers and a hearing on the twenty-sixth.**

Then he stopped for a while, and then he put the pen down, and then he picked it up again, because he had one more thing to say and he could not get it into a box and he was going to try.

> **Nobody has done anything wrong today.**
> **Arkwright read a sheet and weighed four lots and put a new strap on a book. Renwick signed for a weight. The merchant weighed it over and under because the law says so. The man on the compound in the wind is a facilities contractor and he is not the company and he has a pen for a fence.**
> **And the man with the marker at 08:26 wrote a number in a box and the box had nothing above it.**
> **It is a bin behind a loading bay four miles away with a hand truck and a sheet of paper, at a thousand times the scale, and the man with the hand truck is a professional, and the sheet says *general, as per schedule*, and *general* is the word that a hundred and forty people were lost behind at eight o'clock this morning.**

And then, at half past two, with the rain going on the window of the loading-bay office and the building doing the flat dry sound a building makes when it is being kept warm by a plant room rather than by a boiler:

> **To whom is this lost.**
> **Not to Renwick. He signed for a weight and did not know and was never going to and cannot be told anything now, because telling him would be a thing done to a man to relieve a man, and I have watched this Museum do that four times this month and I am not doing it.**
> **Not to Hallowgate Foyston, whose office is a compound and a fence.**
> **Not to Doreen Sarn, who is seventy-eight and who has a cash book in a drawer, and who has been told nothing at all in five weeks, because a woman in a front room in Ferry Bank is not to be having a date handed to her by a man from a museum, and because I have not yet got the sentence right.**
> **To the six, and to a hundred and forty, and to a woman of eighty-one who has never had a letter.**
> **And I cannot write a form. I have looked, in a loading-bay office, on a Friday afternoon, for the form, and there is not one, and that is not a failure of a search. There is no instrument in this business for a person to say *that is mine and it went at eight o'clock this morning and I was standing on it and I could not make anybody stop it*, because the only way to say it is to produce a record, and the record is a sack, and the sack is at a pulp mill on Tuesday.**

He put the notebook down at ten past three and did not pick it up again until Monday, and that was on purpose, and he had made that decision the way he makes all his decisions, which is alone, in a room, with no instrument.

---

He went up the hill at eight. It was not raining. There was a lamp in the cupboard off the old frame store corridor with a key back on a board and a time in a column nobody reads, and a cage in bay 9 with a red disc and a padlock, and a book in a polythene sleeve in bay 4 on a quay in Northglass going to a merchant's collection on Tuesday, and eleven lines of paper somewhere between a yard on an approach and a shed, and a weighbridge docket with a number on it that would come out of an office in four days with the month on it.

He got the tram and sat on the wrong side of it.

He had been in this building for thirty-four days and he had a suspension, a police file, a cautioned statement with the word *reading* in it, a notebook with a hundred and fifteen pages in it, a form in a safe in Irving Quay that he has never signed, two letters that could not be delivered, a name struck off a witness list in his own hand, four seconds of a name he could not identify, a bell on plinth thirty-four under a stay his own form created, and a determination on Thursday morning at eleven o'clock that he is going to walk into as a member of the public, and the thing he has not got is a way of saying any of it out loud in a room in a form that will still be true in eleven weeks.

He was not frightened any more. He had noticed that on Tuesday and had not liked it, and on Friday it had gone from not-liked to something worse, which is that he had a kind of calm about it now, and the calm was the same shape as the quiet that had come into this building on Wednesday morning at half past ten, and he wrote that down too, later, on a Sunday, and did not like it then either.
