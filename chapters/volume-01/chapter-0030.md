# Chapter 0030 — Thirty-Eight Children

They came in at two to eleven on Saturday the seventh of November through a lobby with a poster on the wall.

The poster was A3 and it was at four foot six off the floor and it was face-out, and it was a photograph of the Sillick Mill weighbridge with one line of type under it, and it had been on a hoarding in Lantern Row since the Wednesday of the twenty-first and it had been in the Museum's own lobby since Monday the fourth of November, and the campaign it belonged to was called *Our City in Objects* and it had put up thirty-one sheets in Civic Hill and every one of them was a picture of something and none of them had a caption, and there was no explanation on any of them, and the one on the lobby wall was a cast-iron weighbridge in brick with a hump over it and a small hut beside it and a gate behind it, and it was at the exact height of a nine-year-old's eye.

Elias Venn saw it at nine o'clock on the Saturday morning when he came in to set the room up, and stood in the lobby for about a minute, and then went upstairs.

The Hall of Former Faiths had thirty-one plinths and a rope line and a case at the end of the row on the east side, and the case had a new numbered seal on it and a brass surround and a lead off it to the building earth and a green telltale at the base and a small bronze bell in it about the size of a fist, and the fracture in its shoulder was still visible under a bright machine staple that was never going to heal, and the plinth card said:

> **HAND BELL.** Unpitched at rest, no inscription, no figure, no devotional content. Larger than life-size, used for sounding, and worn hollow inside the lip where it was held. *Cast 1902.*
> **Staple repair, fracture remains visible.**
> **Quiet Accord classification: Annexe D (permanent). To be displayed. Under no circumstances to be removed from public view by a museum acting alone.**

A clamp was on the surround. A brass G-clamp and a length of flat bar padded with silicone, put there by a systems technician at seven o'clock that morning and checked twice.

The anchor card was on the plinth, on the front edge, in a plastic sleeve at a slight angle, and it was a card about four inches by six with a number in the top right, and on the card were five ruled lines, and on the five ruled lines were five things, and the five things were in capitals:

> **ONE QUESTION**
> **NINE MINUTES**
> **COST: 4 SECONDS OF A NAME**
> **WITNESS, IN THE CORRIDOR, WHO CAN SAY THE WORD: MRS BRAMM**
> **WORD: NEXT**

Nobody had written on the card where the object was or what the question was, and nobody had written down what to do if nobody could say the word, and Ivo Pell had put that one out longhand on the back of the card at twenty to nine on the Saturday night, in a loading bay, for an audience of nobody at all, and it was still there, in ballpoint, and it was a paragraph, and it was the only paragraph in the whole of the museum's procedure that had ever been written for the case where the procedure failed.

The cover was on a trestle table against the wall, six feet from the case, and it was not on the case, because a cover on a case is a cage and a cage is the thing in a corridor off the west range, and it had been made out of a pallet collar and a fire blanket in a workshop in about two hours, and it had been carried into this room for the first time at ten to nine that morning and had stood against a wall doing nothing ever since, which was correct.

---

The eleven seated adults were on eleven chairs against the north wall, at a set distance, arranged by Mara Iles at half past eight, and Elias Venn did not know which of them he had asked and which of them had come.

A press officer from the Museum, who had a notepad and a job to do and a genuine difficulty. A woman from the Hill office — Casey, from the donor's office, who had sent an acknowledgement slip in September and had expected to be photographed in the Hall, and who had a folder and a lanyard and the specific disappointment of a person who has arranged a thing and is not going to be in the picture. Two microphones on stands, brought in by a man from a hire company and set up by him without being told where, because a microphone points at the thing you want to hear. A tape recorder on a chair, a mains one, with a red light. A documentary filmmaker with a camera on a tripod and the lens cap off, who had come four times since the twenty-sixth of October and had never once been asked to leave, and who had a producer's question about the release form, and who got a straight answer. A radio producer with a second recorder and a car battery and a manila envelope of tape.

Hattie Bramm was in her own coat, with a folder, and no microphone, and a laptop.

She had a laptop because she is an unpaid-bills organiser and not an archivist, and she runs a Friday queue off a printed grid, and the machine is nine years old and weighs about four pounds and has a sticker on the lid from a computer shop on Kirk Row that has been in her bag since about 2016. She had put it on the chair beside her and had opened it and had her own hand on the trackpad, and she had said, when Mara Iles asked her about the microphone, in front of eleven adults and a filmmaker:

"No."

"You'll be at the back."

"I'll be at the back and I'll be typing and I'll hear it the same, and I'd rather it. There's a woman in this city who used to be on the radio about this and she was on it for eleven years and about four of them were saying things she hadn't heard from anybody." Hattie Bramm had not raised her voice. "And I'm not being difficult. If I stand up there with a microphone, then this morning is a *thing that happened*, and if I sit here with a laptop then this morning is a Friday. And I've been right about that for thirty-one years and I'm not going to be wrong about it on a Saturday in a museum."

She would not refuse the room. That was the other half of it, and she had said that half in about four words, in the doorway at ten to nine, to Elias Venn and to nobody else: *I'll be in it.*

And Perry Loach was on the floor.

He was not on the rota. There was no rota; there had never been a rota for a Saturday tour, and the cleaners' contract returns for the week had the Hall of Former Faiths against eight hours and three days, which is a service and not a person, and there is no column in the whole of the printed grid in which a man can be written. He was there because he had asked Mara Iles, on the telephone, on the Sunday, whether Saturdays were still on, and she had said they were, and he had said, *right*, and had come in at eight o'clock on a Saturday morning to a public gallery with a mop in a closet, and he was on the floor at the far end with the machine off, in his overalls, forty feet from a case.

He was not on the rota and nobody had asked him to be anywhere and he had come, and Elias Venn understood that about four minutes after eleven o'clock that this was the single most important fact in the room and that he was the only person in the building who had not seen it coming.

---

The children came in at two to eleven and there were thirty-eight of them and two teachers and a book of worksheets and a token envelope, and the teachers counted them twice in the lobby, at the paying counter, because that is what you do, and the paying counter is a counter in a lobby where you hand in a pound a head for a guided visit and a teacher hands in forty pounds in a sealed envelope, and the Museum's staff at that counter on that morning were a woman from the front desk who had been told nothing about the object and had asked Mara Iles twice what she should do if a child asked whether the bell was real.

They were on the object's side of the rope. That was deliberate. The rope runs the length of the plinth row and the children were behind it and the adults were not, and the reason was that a school group in a museum stands where you put it, and the adults get the middle and the children get the back, and the object is at the end of the row, and if you put the children on the object side then the only place for their hands is the rope.

Elias Venn stood at eleven o'clock in the end bay of the Hall of Former Faiths with the log in Mara Iles's hand and the card on the plinth and thirty-eight children and two teachers and eleven adults and a case with a bell in it, and he had one question, and he had had it since the Tuesday, and he had not written it down because he was not permitted to write on the plinth card and he was not certain he was going to be allowed to ask it.

He wrote it on the back of his own hand with a biro at ten to eleven, in the corridor, in his own words, and then put the biro in his pocket.

**What is this bell for?**

---

The last thing he did before he started was the thing he had rehearsed four times in an empty room on the Tuesday afternoon and got wrong three times, and he did it out loud, in plain words, at the end of the row, with the case behind him, to thirty-eight children and two teachers and eleven adults, which is fifty-one people, and a camera and a tape recorder and two microphones.

And it came out of him exactly as it had come out of him on the Tuesday afternoon in the empty Hall with Ivo Pell listening and saying nothing, which he had not expected and which he made himself stand still about for half a second before carrying on.

"There is a small bronze bell in that case and it is a bell that was rung at a mill gate to call the shift in, and the last time it was rung anybody heard was the thirtieth of October in 1974, and it is not a religious object and it is not a saint, and there is nothing in it that anybody has to believe in.

"I am not a medium and I am not a priest and I have no idea whether I am doing this the way it is supposed to be done, because I do not think there is a way it is supposed to be done."

"What I am going to do is put my hand on that case and ask it one question, out loud, once. It will answer in its own words. The answer will not be a message for you and it will not be a warning and it will not be for me. I do not know what it will do and neither do you.

"Here is what it costs me. A nosebleed, probably, and a headache for three days. If it goes very wrong I lose a name for four seconds — I did that in the small hours of Sunday the twenty-fifth, at about one in the morning, in a loading bay, and I do not know which name, and that happened once already and I would rather it did not happen in front of you.

"And here is the important one. If it reaches one of you — if it does what it did to me, and to a man with a mop on a Saturday night in October, and puts something into somebody who did not ask to be in this room — then that is on me and not on you, and I will say so in this room at the time.

"There is a woman at the back who is going to be able to stop it. It is her word and not mine. If she says it, it stops, and I am not going to argue with her.

"That is all of it. If you would rather stand further back from the case, do it now, and nobody will think anything of it."

Nobody moved. A boy of about thirteen at the front put his hands in his pockets. A girl of about nine asked her teacher whether she could see the *broken* bit, and the teacher said not yet, and the girl said *there's a staple in it*, and Elias Venn said that she could see the staple from where she was and it was the fixing of a repair, and she said *somebody put a staple in it*, and Mara Iles, at the end of the row with the log, said:

"Yes, somebody did. About a Tuesday morning."

And the room went very quiet.

Not quiet in the way a room goes quiet when a person has said something rude. Quiet the way a room of fifty-one people goes quiet when every one of them has understood the same thing at the same time and nobody else has, which is a thing that happens about four times in a life, and Elias Venn had been in one of them twice.

Somebody had put a staple in it, about a Tuesday morning, and forty people had been hurt, and a man of seventy-four had been in a canal, and a bell had been in a loading bay at one o'clock in the morning, and the whole of the month was a staple.

Elias Venn did not turn round. He knew what the room was doing and he did not know what the room was doing and he was frightened of it, and it was the best thing that happened to him in the whole of that month, and he stood in an end bay of a public gallery at eleven o'clock on a Saturday morning and did not turn round.

---

He put his hand on the case at eleven-twelve.

The case was cold. The seal was on it. The clamp was on the surround. The card was on the plinth in its plastic sleeve at a slight angle and it said five things and the fifth was a word and the word was *next* and the word was not his and the woman who owned it was at the back of the room with a folder and a laptop and no microphone.

The question was *what is this bell for*, and he asked it once, out loud, in a room built for school groups, and the answer came in a man's voice about six inches below where anybody in the room lived, flat, with the rain still in it, the way a man says a thing he has said every working day of his life at six in the morning at a gate.

"**Eleven.**"

And then the room filled faster than the protocol could.

---

The anchor card went at eleven thirteen. It was in a plastic sleeve on the front edge of a plinth and a boy of about nine had put his sleeve on the edge of it to lean on, the way children lean, and the sleeve slid, and the card went off the plinth and hit the terrazzo and skidded about two feet, and the clamp went at the same moment, because the clamp was on the surround and the surround is bolted to the plinth and the plinth is bolted to the floor and the floor is not bolted to anything, and a four-inch card hitting a terrazzo floor in a building of this age is a noise that everybody in a gallery hears, and everybody in that gallery turned round.

The clamp came off the surround and went over and the brass went across the terrazzo on its side and made a noise like a dropped spanner, and Ivo Pell was not there because Ivo Pell was not on the rota either, and the two people who moved were a facilities man and the man in overalls forty feet away with the machine off.

The card and the clamp were on the floor. Both of them. Before Elias Venn's hand was on anything, and while it was on something, and the room was fifty-one people and a camera and a tape recorder and two microphones, and the room had filled faster than a five-line card on a plinth could govern.

The bell did not stop. The bell had never been the thing that stopped. It was the room, and the room was the loudest signal a work-god has ever had, and a work-god is a thing formed out of a count of people spoken aloud at a gate where the counted could hear it, and there were fifty-one people in a room within a hundred feet of each other and thirty-eight of them were children and the object had been put in a loading bay in the dark in front of eleven people and had reached a night cleaner's ears in a man's voice and had taken four seconds of a name out of the only man who had ever been able to ask it anything.

Perry Loach put the mop down against the wall at 11:14, carefully, with both hands, the way you put a thing down when you have had it for eleven years and you do not want to drop it, and he said, without turning round, in a voice nobody in that room had heard from a man in overalls before:

"That's the shop."

Nobody asked him what he meant.

He said it again anyway, because there were thirty-eight children in the room and eleven adults and a filmmaker and a radio producer, and a thing that is said once in a room of fifty-one people is a private incident, and a thing that is said twice is a record.

"The fitters' shop," he said. "Eleven men on that shop on the Friday morning. That's the last time it rang. It wasn't the gate bell and it wasn't the hooter, it was us, and you rang it on the number, and I rang it and there was eleven of us on that shop and the foreman wanted to know if we were all in because we weren't all in, and that's the last time that bell ever made a noise."

And then a boy of about nine, on the floor, on his knees, picking up a plastic sleeve with an anchor card in it, said:

"Twelve."

---

There was no gap in the room, and there was a gap in the count, and the second thing is the one that matters. Nobody who has not heard a tape can be made to understand either of them.

The boy did not look up and he did not stop picking the card up. He was on his knees with a sleeve in his hand and a card in the sleeve, and he said *twelve* in the voice of a child saying a number, flat, the way you say a number in a corridor, and then a girl of about eleven, standing at the rope, said **"Fourteen."**

Then a boy of about thirteen said **"Fifteen."**

Then a girl of about sixteen, who had not spoken all morning and who was at the end of the row, said **"Sixteen."**

And then the adults on the floor began to say them, one after another, in the corridor behind the seated row, in the way people say things in corridors.

A man of about seventy-six, from Sillick Lane, said **"Twenty."**

A woman of about eighty-one, from Sillick Lane, said **"Twenty-one."**

A man of about sixty-eight from Weaver's Row said **"Twenty-two."**

And then nothing for about four seconds, and a voice nobody in the room could locate said **"Twenty-four."**

Elias Venn stood with his hand on the case of a small bronze bell in the Hall of Former Faiths and did not move, and could not have moved, and the nine minutes on the card had two and a half left and he was not going to spend them.

Hattie Bramm was typing. She had been typing since the boy said twelve. Her machine was the only machine in the room that was not a camera or a recorder and it was nine years old and it was on her knees and she was typing with two fingers and she had her coat on and her folder on the chair beside her and her face was wet and she was working, and she was not going to stop, and if the tape had been running for the whole of it then the tape had the gaps in it exactly where they were, because Hattie Bramm typed what was said and she typed the gaps too.

> *Eleven. Twelve. — Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. — — — Twenty. Twenty-one. Twenty-two. — Twenty-four.*

Thirteen. Seventeen, eighteen, nineteen. Twenty-three.

Five numbers that were not said by anybody, in the middle of a count at a gate on a wet morning. The biggest single gap was three, and it was where three people were not on the gate, and a number missing out of a run of numbers is a person who was not there, and a person who was not there and is not in any book is a person nobody has to pay.

The eleven-year-old and the boy of nine were on the floor together with the card, and they had both stopped, and they were not looking at the case.

They were looking at the third row of chairs, where two people were sitting who had been sitting there all morning and who had not said a word, and who had come with a daughter, and who were a man of about seventy-eight and a woman of about eighty-one, and whose names were on no sheet in that room, and who were not in Hattie Bramm's six, and who were not on the Museum's list of eleven, and who had been asked nothing by anybody, because nobody had known they were there, and because in about eleven seconds he had decided that he did not know whether they were the two, and had not said so to anybody, and was not going to.

Neither of the children said it.

The gap sat in the middle of it with its own numbers missing out of it, and it was a child on a floor with a plastic sleeve in his hand who was not looking at the bell and was looking at two people, and that was the whole of it.

---

Two and a half minutes later the object stopped, and it did not stop the way a story stops.

It had been going for a hundred and forty seconds. The first three notes came out of it and were lost in the noise of thirty-eight people and two teachers and one man in overalls, and the fourth and fifth notes were lost in the noise of thirty-eight people, and the interval between the last of it and the light coming up on the case was a hundred seconds, and then the light came up on the case.

It did not hollow. It did not return. It did not explain itself, and nobody in the room, and nobody in the building afterwards, was ever able to say what it had been doing, and Aurelia Roth's file on the object already said what it was doing in six words, and the six words were *no devotional content*, and she had been right.

Elias Venn took his hand off the case, and his nose started at about eleven twenty-six, and it was a nosebleed, and a teacher handed him a tissue from her own bag without being asked, and a man of about sixty-eight from Weaver's Row said *sorry mate* and meant something else entirely.

Mara Iles read back the log. She read out the time the question was asked and the answer and the cost and the word and the times, and she read out the card going off the plinth and the clamp going across the floor, and she read out the two and a half minutes, and the nine minutes promised, and she read out that the reader did not state a figure and that the figure was not on the sheet.

"Reader — did you state a figure?"

"No."

"Any figure at all, in the room, today?"

"No."

The eleven adults and the two teachers and thirty-eight children were still in the room. Nobody had gone out. A girl of about nine asked whether she could see the staple now and was told that she could, and she and two others came and stood four feet from the case and looked at a bright machine staple through plate glass for about a minute and a half, and one of them said *that's a proper repair* and another said *is it old* and Elias Venn said that it was about three weeks old and that there was an older repair under it and that the object had been broken for a hundred and twenty years and had been mended by somebody who did not write down their name, and the girl said *that's the same as the room*, and he wrote it down at the time because he wrote down everything, and it was two days later, on the Monday, reading it back, that he understood what the girl had meant, and that he was not going to be able to put it back.

Then Hattie Bramm said, from the back, in a voice nobody had heard out of her, and there was no microphone and there was no camera on her and she was forty feet away:

"There is a number on this sheet and it is not a count and I am not giving anybody a count."

The film producer was on his feet in about a second and a half. "Mrs Bramm — if I put it in the film, the moment there's a number, it's a headline. I'll pay you. I'll pay you now, whatever it is."

"You'll pay me for a number," Hattie Bramm said. "You will not pay me for a number, love, because the number is not the thing and you know the number is not the thing, and if I hand you a number in the next four minutes then in six weeks there is a headline with it in and every one of the hundred and forty people in my list becomes a figure in a newspaper instead of a person I have to know, and that is the end of me and it is the end of them and you will not even be able to tell when it happened."

Mara Iles did not demand it. Elias Venn, who had wanted to demand it — who had a police number with his name on it and a notebook with ninety-one lines in it and four seconds of a name in his long-term memory and a whole month of his life in a corridor — did not demand it either, and did not ask her, and would not ask her, and that was the fifth time in a month he had chosen not to ask a question he could have asked and it was the only one of the five that cost him nothing at all.

Field Officer Marsh was not there. A municipal conciliation officer was there, because a form had been sent to the Museum in October by a woman who had read the second report card, and Marsh's assistant had a clipboard, and the form was headed **F/28**, and on the form there is a box that says **number of persons present** and a box that says **figure given**, and on the twenty-sixth of October a rule was written into a room off a loading bay that no figure was to be recorded without a name.

Hattie Bramm took her hands off the keyboard.

"**Seventy-one**," she said. "That's the last number on my list. It's a number I wrote myself, on my own sheet, in September, and it's an entry and not a count, and it is the number of a man called Theodore Pyle who is at 19 Ferry Bank and who is seventy-two and who has never once been in a queue in nine years and who I have had three letters from."

She took her hands off the keyboard again.

"You can have seventy-one. It's on a piece of paper in my bag, in my own hand, and if anybody in the world wants to check it they can come to a Friday queue and look at it. And the other box gets the word *unknown* and my initials, and if that is not allowed then leave both boxes empty, and if both boxes are empty then that is what happened in this room and that is a true thing and it can be printed."

The assistant wrote **71** in the *figure given* box and **UNKNOWN — H.B.** in the *number of persons* box, and initialled it, and turned it round so she could read it, and asked her to initial it, and Hattie Bramm initialled it.

And Elias Venn stood thirty feet from a piece of A4 with two numbers' worth of somebody's entire nine years in it and understood that the only number that had gone into a form in the whole of the month was an entry and not a count, and that the first two figures he had ever said out loud, and the two people who had never been entered in any book, had been said on the twenty-sixth of October in a room off a loading bay and had not been said in a public gallery in front of a camera, and that he had made that decision and not Mara Iles and not Doreen Sarn and not Hattie Bramm, and that the decision had been made by a man who had kept a number in a coat pocket for a month.

---

They cleared at twenty past twelve and the children went back to the paying counter and the tapes came out and the microphones came down and the room was empty of the public and full of eleven adults and eleven chairs and a weighbridge poster in a lobby.

And at ten past one, a woman in a plain coat with a case and a satchel and a Registry identity card came along the north wall of the Hall of Former Faiths and stood at the end of the row, and put a form on a clipboard, and did not take her case off her shoulder.

She was not Aurelia Roth. She was about thirty-five and she was from the same rota and she was the assessor who had photographed a hand-cut card on the twelfth of October with a scale rule and had described the erasure as *partial* and had stopped there, because *partial* was what the thing was, and it was what she had written, and she had been right, and she had been right for a very long time before anybody in this building had been wrong about anything.

"Ottilie Grange," she said. "Schedule 12. I have a form here and I have to say what I have to say and then you can all say what you want to say and I shall write all of it down."

"Ma'am," said Mara Iles. "The classification on that object is Annexe D and it is permanent, and it was attached on the nineteenth of October by Ms Roth, and it states that the object is to be displayed and is not to be removed from public view by a museum acting alone. It has not been removed. It is on its plinth. It has been on its plinth since the twenty-seventh of October. There has been a public event in this room and it is now over and the room is being reset and there is a new numbered seal going on at two o'clock this afternoon and Ms Spence is going to countersign it and I am going to re-certify the room in one sentence at three o'clock. I would like it recorded that I have said all of that before you have said anything."

"Noted." Ottilie Grange wrote for about four seconds. "I have a form and I have to complete it and the form is a *disturbance report* under procedure 11, and I have to complete it whether anybody in the room likes it or not, and I am not the person who decided that, and I would like that said too."

She looked at the clipboard.

"Schedule 12 carried object. A disturbance has been reported. The reporting party is the person who made the request, and I am required to name the reporting party, and the reporting party is the person whose request is on file."

"Which is me," said Elias Venn.

"Yes, Mr Venn. QA/CR/1187, lodged at the front desk on the nineteenth of October, reason: *the object is out of condition.* Six words." She did not look up. "Which is a public right, and it is on the notice board, and it has been on the notice board for six years, and it is the route by which a disturbance gets reported. That is not an accident of drafting. It is how the schedule is built. The person who asks for a thing to be looked at is the person who reports it, and there is no route by which a member of the public complains about a thing they did not ask about, and I have had that pointed out to me twice in six years and both times it was by people who had read the schedule more carefully than I had at the time, and both times they were right."

She read out the operative words.

> **ANNEXE E — STAY.**
> *A Schedule 12 carried object which has been reported as having reached, or as being likely to reach, a person who has not consented to it, in a public room, is classified as a disturbance. The object is **stayed** from all handling, use, cleaning, movement, and public access until a material-change inspection has been completed and a determination made.*
> *This is not a closure. The object remains where it is. The room remains open. Nothing has been withdrawn from the public. The lights are on and nobody may touch it.*
> *Dispute route: the requester, in the form of a request.*

"There," said Ottilie Grange. "Three sections and a form. That is the whole of the document and it took me about four minutes and it is correct, and I am not going to argue with any of you about it, and I am going to read it into the record now and then I am going to invite comments and I am going to write the comments down."

She read it into the record.

Anja Ward was in the doorway of the Hall of Former Faiths and had been in the doorway for the whole of it, and she did not come in, and she did not say anything, and she did not sign, and she did not argue, and afterwards she said three sentences in a corridor and they were all of them about procedure and none of them was a defence.

"He is right," she said. "About all of it. The object did reach a person who has not consented. It has done it twice that I know of and once that I do not, and it did it this morning, and the Annexe D assessor has told me in writing twice that this object has no devotional content, and she is right, and none of those four facts touches the other three. That is the design and I have had it my whole career and I have never been able to find the join in it."

"You could appeal it."

"I could appeal it and I would lose and it would take eleven weeks, and in eleven weeks that object is on a plinth in this building with the lights on and nobody may touch it and the school term has started and the Hall certificate is a lie of omission of a size I have signed four hundred times, and at the end of eleven weeks somebody in Northglass will write *no finding*, and the stay will be lifted, and the next Saturday morning it will do it again, and I will have spent eleven weeks being the woman who appealed." She put her hands in her pockets. "I do not know how to stop it. That is not a thing I say often and I have said it once this year, to a man on a stair landing, about a clause."

"You wrote the clause."

"I wrote four of them," said Anja Ward, "on the fifteenth of October, at six in the morning, in a hurry, in a card the size of an envelope, and one of them has just taken a bell out of my gallery for an unknown period, and I wrote it, and he is not wrong, and I have lost."

Elias Venn stood in the corridor outside the Hall of Former Faiths at a quarter past one on Saturday the seventh of November with a notebook in his hand, and he was not permitted to be in that room, and he had been in it in front of fifty-one people, and he was now outside it with the door open and the resealing trolley coming down the north corridor, and a conservator putting a new numbered seal on a case that was not going to be open again for four weeks.

He did not open the notebook at first. He stood in the corridor for about five minutes and worked out how many people he had spoken to that day, and how many of them had said anything, and then put both numbers away, which is not counting and is what he does now.

A girl of about nine had said *somebody put a staple in it* and a woman with a log had said *yes, somebody did, about a Tuesday morning*, and a room had gone quiet. A man in overalls had said *that's the shop* twice. A boy on the floor had said twelve and had not known that he was saying it. A woman of fifty-four had typed a gap and typed the gaps in the gap, and had cried, and had worked, and had refused a number, and had been right. A producer had offered money for a figure and had been refused. An assessor of about thirty-five had read a document into a record because she was required to, and it had taken her four minutes, and she had been right about everything, and he hated her for it for about a day and a half and then for the rest of his life.

Three of the numbers in a tape were a man's memory of a wet morning at a gate.

Not the whole of the count. Not the headcount, and not the pay run, and not the pair. A man of thirty-two in a gallery in November, saying eleven out loud in a room, and then four children, having said it to himself at a plinth at ten o'clock at night on the tenth of October and then stopped dead as though interrupted, and saying it to three children, and the children saying four more, and a woman of eighty-one saying twenty-one, and a man of sixty-eight saying twenty-two, and a voice saying twenty-four.

It was on a tape. And the tape was in a plastic wallet in a hired case in a lock-up in Low Ward, and a copy was going to the conciliation service, and a copy was going to a radio producer's car, and a copy was going to a filmmaker's editor, and a copy was going to Quarry Bank Solicitors in Irving Quay, and a copy was going to a woman of seventy-eight in a flat in Ferry Bank with a hardback cash book and a rubber band round it that she was not going to give to anybody, and a copy was going to a woman of fifty-four in a queue on a Friday with a list of a hundred and forty names in a drawer in a front room that she was not going to give to anybody.

Three of the numbers in that tape were a man's memory of a wet morning at a gate, and they were now a document in three institutions and in nine newspapers, and he could not alter one figure of it, and there was no version of the next ten years in which he could, and that was the entire point of the last four weeks and he had fought it for three of them.

The two who were not on the tape were in the room. Sitting in the third row with a daughter between them, in the front half of a public gallery, at a quarter past one on a Saturday afternoon, having been asked nothing by anybody, and he did not know, and had not known since Monday, and had not told a single person in three weeks that he did not know, because nobody had known they were there, and because a child had not said it, and because two people who are not in any book are only ever in a room if somebody with a book decides to read it out loud.

A woman of seventy-eight was in a flat in Ferry Bank with a hardback cash book, and a letter from the Museum that she had not opened, on a sideboard, face down, and a niece who was post.

A man of seventy-four was at number fourteen in Sillick Lane with a shoulder and a wrist and a temperature of about thirty-four, and a letter addressed to him had been in a coat pocket in Low Ward since Friday the thirtieth of October and was in the coat pocket still.

A form was on a table in a loading-bay office on the ground floor of a building on Civic Hill, one side printed and one side typed, with a blank in the middle of it and a signature line at the foot of it and a box at the bottom that said **MUSEUM OF QUIET SAITS — LOADING BAY OFFICE — Second floor, service lane. Ask for the duty manager. Morrowgate 4 — Temple Street 0118 — 01r**, and a place to begin that was not an accusation.

A docket had lapsed in a corridor on a Thursday night at four minutes past midnight and a cage had become a piece of furniture, and it had gone back into bay 9 on the Friday the first of November, where it had stood for fifty years, and a box in it with a red disc on it and a padlock on it and eleven items in it, one of which was an envelope, and the eleven names were on a schedule that Mara Iles had signed and dated on the twenty-ninth of October, and there was no longer a form in the world that a corporation could put a box on, a docket on and a wall against.

And on a plinth in a hall a hundred and fifty feet away, under a stay, in a room with the lights on that nobody may touch, a small bronze bell with a crack in its shoulder under a bright staple was answering a question nobody had asked it and would go on doing whatever it was doing, for four weeks, or for a term, or for as long as it took, and the Annexe D assessor in Northglass had said in three separate documents that there is nothing devotional about it, and she had been right about that, and Ottilie Grange was right about the other thing, and both of them were right, and the whole of that building's hundred and forty years of keeping this out of rooms had just been handed the first one, and it was a public gallery on a Saturday morning with thirty-eight children in it and a poster of a weighbridge in the lobby, and the children had gone back to the paying counter and counted themselves twice.

Elias Venn put his hand flat on the corridor wall, which was the good, honest, panelled, plastered wall of a public building, and he took the notebook out and he wrote on the first clean page he had had since the tenth of October a time and four lines, and then he went and did the dead letters, because it was Saturday and there were two hundred and seventy-one of them and a clerk's is a slow job, and a clerk's is the only thing he is still, on the seventh of November, that nobody in this city can be got at over.

He had not opened the unlisted room. He had not opened the box. He had not opened locker eighty-one, and he had not opened the top drawer in the label room, and the door halfway down the Quiet Wing corridor with the clouded wired glass was still shut and latched, and it was going to be shut and latched when the whole of the year had gone, and he was not going to open it, and there was not one door in this building that he was going to open because of anything that had happened on the seventh of November.
