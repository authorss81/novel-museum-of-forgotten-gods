# Chapter 0011 — What It Was Kept From

Dael Ordway was on the loading-bay desk at seven on a Sunday morning with his coat still on, which meant he had come in on the way somewhere, and there was a page in front of him held flat with a mug.

"Your name's on it," Dael said. "I want you to read it before you do anything else, and then I want you to tell me whether you think I wrote it well."

Elias put his bag down under the desk where it had gone every shift for two years and came back and stood in front of the page.

> **MORROWGATE MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS**
> **RESTRICTION ON DUTY — SECURITY**
>
> Employee: VENN, E. — Grade 1 (Security)
> Effective: Sunday 11 October, 07:00, until further notice in writing.
> Withheld: night duty; all rota allocation.
> Retained: building keys, current access card, service lane and loading bay desk, security room, ground floor, staff facilities, and attendance at any re-inspection of an object to which the holder has prior recorded access **as escort, ground floor only, and not in the room.**
> Not admitted: Hall of Former Faiths; Gallery of Unnamed Hands; conservation floor; Quiet Wing.
> Review: written review on the fourth Monday of the month by the Director. No appeal to the Board before that review.
> Payroll: unpaid. Leave of absence to be recorded as leave.
>
> *Issued under the night post order, 4(i) (b), for an unclosed authorised work order and a fault report signed in conflict with the systems record.*
>
> D. ORDWAY, Night Supervisor

Elias read it four times, because he had trained himself to read documents the way you read a door, and because he could not think what else to do with his hands.

"It says *scheduled leave* somewhere in the system," Dael said. "It doesn't say that on the paper, on purpose, because if it says that on the paper then it's a suspension and it goes on your file and your file goes to the Board and there's a form. So on the paper it's a restriction, and in the system it's leave, and in a fortnight a month-end runs on a Tuesday in a batch that nobody reads, and it says leave. And the difference between those two words is a couple of hundred a month, and the couple of hundred is the price of not putting the word *suspended* in a system that goes to a Board."

"Dael—"

"I'm not finished, and if I stop now I'll be a man who did this in a corridor." He took his hand off the page. "You are not dismissed. Nothing about you is decided. What's decided is that you do not go out at night on your own and you do not go up. That's all. It's a review, and there's a review on the fourth Monday, and if you turn up to it with something worth saying, you get a decision in a room with a door you can close. And I would rather do this than have a restricted-door outage in a building with forty-one children coming in on a school morning that somebody sees a notice about."

Elias looked at the first paragraph again. *Retained.* He read it three more times, and understood that he had been standing there waiting to be told he was out, and had been handed a list of the specific rooms he was still allowed into, and that the list was kind, and that the kindness and the violence were in the same sentence and had been in the same sentence since seven o'clock that morning.

"And the Hall," he said.

"You're not in the Hall." Dael turned the page round so it faced him. "The Hall, the Unnamed Hands, the conservation floor and the wing. Four rooms. You'll be outside them the whole day and you'll be able to see into all four of them from places you're allowed to stand, and that's the design, and if you want to complain about the design, complain to the Director on Monday and she'll enjoy it as much as you'd enjoy saying it."

"What about level B?"

"Not without a second registered person and my signature on the lift, and I'm not signing that one." He said it without any weight at all. "You know why and so do I, and it is not in my job to write down why, and the reason it is not in my job is that it would make a record."

He had, Elias understood, made it a kindness, and the kindness was that he had said it out loud, in a room, in the morning, rather than letting a man find it out at a gate at seven in the evening.

"Then the desk," Elias said. "This desk. I'm on this desk."

"You're on this desk because the loading-bay desk is where a suspended man can be useful without anybody having to trust him with a key." He finally took his hand off the page and picked his hat up off the desk and looked at it. "Elias, I have to say the rest of it and then I'm going to go and do a systems print, and I'd rather say it in one piece than have it come out over a week."

"Say it."

"Work order 4412-S. Door seven controller, off live at twenty-three forty-one, answering at twenty-eight past midnight, forty-seven minutes, inside a fifty-minute window, signed by the Director, and the order was never closed." He put the hat under his arm. "And that order came off the maintenance shop terminal, under PELL, I. And Ivo was in this building that night and he was not at the frame. That is on the report, that is in the systems, and that is exactly the sort of thing a loss adjuster reads out loud in a room with a lamp on and a camera going."

"It was a *reader fault*. The panel said—"

"I know exactly what the panel said, because I have the panel." Dael came round the desk and put his face close enough that Elias could smell the rain still on his coat. "The panel said *fault*. You said *nobody was in the corridor*. Those are two different sentences and only one of them is true, and the one that is not true is the one I made you write, because I put the whole of the unvouched window in front of you and let you choose, and you chose the one that protected you, and I have signed a great many men off on that and I would do it again tomorrow."

He stopped.

"So that's the thing," he said, more gently. "I didn't get you. I got the systems. The systems are on my side and always will be, and I wrote this restriction on the strength of them, and I would like you to understand that when it comes, whatever it comes to, it won't be me who puts it in front of a panel. I've already done my one document this week and I did it out of duty to a man who told me no on a Sunday morning in a room with a door that shuts."

---

He was out of the building by ten past eight and he stayed out of it for most of the day, which was a thing he had not known he was capable of doing.

He walked to the top of the service lane. The rain had come in on Saturday night and had not stopped. The lane behind Civic Hill was slick and shut and ordinary: the coal chute grille, the bins, the two roller shutters on the loading bay, the puddle at the low corner by the kerb that formed every wet night in the autumn and never quite froze, the notice board inside the glass, the corner camera, and the door he had a key for and was not going to use.

He stood in the rain and looked at a grey steel cupboard through a service entrance and thought, for the first time in two years, about what it would be like to walk past a building every day for a year and never go in it.

At 08:40 the Sunday clerk came down from the main office with a single sheet in a plastic wallet and nailed it to the board by the tray, and then went back up without looking at the lane at all, which was how he learned that a piece of paper could be put up in this building in under a minute and could not be taken down.

It had a heading on it, and under the heading two columns.

> **RE-INSPECTION — PLINTH 34, HALL OF FORMER FAITHS**
> **MONDAY 12 OCTOBER, 09:00**
>
> | ATTENDING | ROLE |
> | --- | --- |
> | A. Ward | Director, chairing |
> | I. Spence | Metals, opening the case |
> | O. Grange | Quiet Accord Registry, assessor |
> | D. Ordway | Night post, minute and post-record |
> | R. Priest | Duty manager, service desk and door |
>
> | ALSO PRESENT | ROLE |
> | --- | --- |
> | P. Loach, cleaner | Floors, west side, from 08:00 |
> | E. Venn, security | **Ground floor, loading-bay desk, escort only. Not admitted to the room.** |

He read his own line four times. He was on the paper. He was the second column, which was the other column.

After a week of being the only person in the building who was paying attention, the correct outcome of his week had been printed, in a plastic wallet, with his name spelled out, in the column of people who are *also* present.

A day-cleaning crew came in behind him at ten past eight, four of them, in overalls, the front one pushing a cart with a bucket on it. They went up the back stair to the lavatories and the offices and did not come near the galleries, because it was Sunday and the galleries were shut and the day clean did not go into a shut room. One of the four was a woman in her forties with grey in her hair under the cap, and she came past the desk and said, "Morning," to the desk and not to him.

She was the first thing for four days that had not been a document.

---

He went down to Low Ward because there was nowhere else to go, and the tram took forty minutes, and his knee was very slightly wrong in a way he did not examine.

The laundrette shut at ten on a Sunday. He sat on the bench by the door, where he had sat since he was a boy, and there were eleven machines running and none of them had anything in them that belonged to him, and his mother was behind the counter doing the one thing she does between four and five on a Sunday, which is a sheet of large paper and a pen and a sum.

"I've been suspended," he said.

Helen Venn did not look up. "Off nights?"

"Off nights and off the floors. It's a paper. I can show you the paper."

"No, love." She carried on with the sum. "I don't want to see the paper. You'll bring it round when you've read it twice and you've stopped wanting me to be angry." She put the pen down. "Tea?"

He said yes, because there was no version of the next twenty minutes that did not include tea, and she made it in the way she made it, too strong, in a mug with the rim gone white, and she brought it over and sat down on the end of the bench in the gap he had left her, and neither of them said anything for about a minute.

Then she said, "Right. Go on then."

So he told her. Not the reading, not the number, not the man with the mop, not the Hall and not the box and not the box's label. He told her the door and the window and the forty-seven minutes and the order that was never closed, and he told her that he had signed a report four words of which were false and that he had known which four.

And Helen Venn sat on a bench in a laundrette and looked at her son, and then said:

"You'd have written it, then, in your own words, if they'd asked you first."

"Yes."

"They did ask you first." She picked the pen back up. "That's what a form is, love. It's a question with a box next to it."

He had not expected that, and he had expected several other things.

---

The letter was on the shelf above the boiler where it had been for eighteen years, in a white envelope with his father's name on it in his mother's hand, and the paper had gone soft along the fold from being taken out and put back so many times that the fold held itself.

He looked at it for a long time.

He had a suspension and a review on the fourth Monday and a notebook in his coat with two numbers and a name in it, and a conservator on the first floor who had made him promise to be told everything, and an unopened letter on a shelf above a boiler in a laundrette in Low Ward. And it was the only room he had been inside all day that he was allowed into, and it was a room with hot water in it and a woman in it who had never once asked him for anything.

He put his hand out and stopped with it about nine inches from the shelf.

He wanted to say that afterwards: that he stood in a warm room in a wet Sunday and did not take a thing that was his own, from his own mother, in a locked cupboard nine inches from his hand. He wanted to say that he had thought about it for a long time and that it had taken him a very simple decision and that the decision was that he was going to spend the rest of the week being a man who could be told the truth, and that he could not do that starting on Sunday with his hand on an envelope.

She watched him do it. She watched the whole nine inches.

"Go on, then," she said, when his hand came back down.

"No."

"No?"

"Not till I've told Mara I lied to her on Saturday, and I've not done that yet, and I'd be doing it in a bad way if I did it in a good minute." He put his hands round the mug. "I'll ask you properly. Soon. And you'll tell me and I'll do something stupid with it, and you'll be cross with me, and then I'll have told her first, and you'll be in the right order."

Helen Venn looked at him for a long moment and then she did something she almost never did, which was to say nothing at all about it, and she went back to her sheet of large paper, and the eleven machines ran, and the rain came off the flat above them onto the back yard in a long grey sheet.

"Under that boiler," she said, after a while, without looking up, "for eighteen years, love, and you've had the run of the place twice a week since you were nine."

"I know."

"You've never asked."

"I know that too."

She wrote a figure down and put the pen in the tray. "Then say it out loud once, so that it's a thing that's been said, and then go and be useful to somebody."

"What?"

"That I want it," Elias said. "I've wanted it for three years. I'm not asking today."

"Right," said Helen Venn. "Good. That'll keep."

---

He got the tram back at twenty to two and walked up the hill and stood outside the museum from half past two until the light went.

It is not much of a thing, a building, in the rain, at half past two on a Sunday in October. Stone and glass, a portico, four columns, a notice case with a donor's name on it, and above the door a bronze plate with the Museum's name on it and, underneath, in small letters, the thing he had never once looked at properly.

> *THE MORROWGATE MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS*
> *For the keeping of what this city has forgotten, and for the remembering of what it has not yet learned to say.*

He had walked past that for two years, four days a week, and he had never read it, and it took him four seconds on a wet Sunday to work out why, and the reason was that it did not say who kept it, or who looked after it, or what it was kept *from*, and a sentence like that is very easy to walk past and extremely hard to stand in front of.

A school group came down the front steps at ten past three, nine or ten of them, with a woman in a brown coat at the front and a man at the back, and they went in through the portico and the door shut behind them, and Elias Venn stood in the rain on the opposite pavement and counted ten children going into a building he was not allowed to walk into, and worked out, without wanting to, that the Hall of Former Faiths would be empty and the Gallery of Unnamed Hands would be shut behind steel shutters, and that a group of ten would go past both of them to a room with a threshold stone and a dead shrine lamp in it.

He had started to count, because it was better than the alternative, and he stopped, and stood there, and was embarrassed by the counting, and did it anyway because that was what he was.

---

He got his pay at a terminal in the security room and it was seven pounds sixty an hour, and the terminal printed a line under the amount that said **LEAVE — UNPAID** in the same typeface as everything else in the world.

He was in the building from eight until ten past two, and then there was a gap, and then he was in it again from eight.

At 14:40 Ivo Pell came into the loading bay with a toolbag, put the bag down under the desk, and stood there.

"Sunday," Ivo said.

"Sunday."

"You've not done that round in your life. Noon to eight, seven days, for *systems*. Even I don't do that."

"No."

"No." Ivo turned the bag round on its face. "So here's the thing, and it's a good thing, and I want you to hear me say it's a good thing before I say the rest of it. For the last month I have been trying to find out how a person with a login gets a name out of every list in this building and not off any of them. And I cannot. I have looked at the way the payroll request works, and at the way a manual payroll addition is authorised, and at the bureau's own month-end, and there is no one in the city who can tell me how a person is removed from three systems and kept alive in a fourth unless they know which four, in which order, with which forms." He took his hands out of his pockets. "And I have found that the woman who *can* tell me is the registrar, and the registrar has been told about plinth thirty-four in a way that means her file is now the front of a queue, and the front of a queue is where a good idea goes to die."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I've run a systems inventory," said Ivo, "for eight weeks, and it is due to be presented, and I am going to present it on the Monday, at seven, in the systems office, to Spence and to the duty manager and to the health and safety officer, and the insurance schedule is one of the documents, and the insurance schedule is the document that says what the building is worth and what it is insured against, and it is due for renewal in April." He picked the bag up again. "And a man who is suspended for a forty-seven-minute reader fault is not a man anybody is going to give an eight-week systems inventory to."

There was a space on the loading-bay desk about eleven inches wide and it was Elias's space, and he looked at it.

"Ivo."

"Don't."

"I'm not going to ask you for anything."

"No," said Ivo. "I know. That's the whole of the problem with you, that is, and I'm not being unfair, and I'll still have done it." He went to the stairs, and stopped on the second one, and said, without turning round, "You didn't ask. I did it anyway. I want that on the record and I'm not coming back to it."

Then he was gone, and the building was quiet, and the roller door on the bay was up on its chain with the rain coming under it, and there was an envelope on the desk that had not been there before, and the label on it said **DEAD-LETTERS — TONIGHT**, and it had a number written on it in biro.

Elias Venn, who was not permitted on the collections floors, and who was not permitted in the Quiet Wing, and who was not permitted to be in the Hall of Former Faiths at all, and who was on a Sunday in his own city with nowhere he was allowed to go, sat down at the loading-bay desk in a building full of other people's letters, and started to sort them into two piles.
