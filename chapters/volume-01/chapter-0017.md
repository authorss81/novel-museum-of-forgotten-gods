# Chapter 0017 — Attendant: Two

The registered letter came in at half past eleven on Saturday morning and it was marked NOT BEFORE 10:00 and HOLD FOR SIGNATURE, and it was served on the Director at twenty past eleven by a young man in a good coat who was not wet even though it had been raining since eight.

Elias did not see the letter. He saw the young man, and the Director, and the way the Director stood at the desk afterwards with a sheet of A4 in her hand doing the thing she did with the cup, which was holding it about an inch off the surface in both hands, and then went up.

At ten to one Ivo rang the loading bay from the conservation floor, where he was on a systems day, and there was a lot of noise on the line behind him.

"They've come up," he said. "Two of them, and a plan chest, and a measuring tape, and the one in the good coat has been into the Hall, the Hall, Elias, and the cage, and he's read the number off the tag and he's written it in a book and he didn't go near it. And there's a *ladder* out."

"Ladder?"

"A folding one, out of a van, in the west range corridor, and the one's doing it is a man called Nunn from a firm called Foyston and he's asking the duty manager where the fire assembly is." Ivo's voice went up. "What is a fire assembly, in a corridor, by a cage?"

"Somebody's writing down that the cage is in a corridor of escape."

"Right. And he's got a floor plan out on a trolley and he's measured the width of the corridor and he's written it down, and he's written down that the door to the lavatories is on a swing that fouls the cage, and it does, and it's a folding cage on castors and it fouls it, and I've watched him notice it and I've watched him write *noted* and I've watched him not do anything about it because it's not his job." A pause. "He's a transfer clerk. He's doing a pre-removal survey. They're taking the box out on Monday."

"They're not taking it out on Monday. The docket says the sixteenth and it's the seventeenth and Dael's name is on it and the day is in the past."

"Elias, the docket in the corridor says *four November*. What's in that letter is different and you should come and look at it, and I can't look at it, and I'm not going to."

---

The letter had one paragraph in the middle of it and the paragraph was correct.

> *In consequence of the continuing delay in the removal of one item, the contractor's programme has been accelerated. Site clearance will commence on Saturday 17 October and is programmed for completion by the close of Sunday 18 October. Notice is hereby given to any person who may be affected. Museum staff are not admitted to the site without the prior written consent of the company, which is refused.*

Elias read it in the loading bay at twenty past one, standing up, with the day book open in front of him because that is where a thing goes now, and he read it four times, and then he worked out what had happened and it took him about nine seconds.

On Wednesday, at half past seven in the morning, in a service corridor, with a cage four feet away and a padlock and a tag, he had put his hand on the mesh and stayed there for a second longer than a suspended man should, and the thing that had made him do it was not the bell. The thing that had made him do it was that a person who put a date in a form had not moved it, and a person who moved it would have had to destroy the form.

And a date in a form had cost a site clearance contractor a fortnight.

He had spent six days learning to read the small print of this building, and the small print had said that a proposed disposition not executed within ten working days lapses, and that a lapsed disposition is a fairness to everybody, and that the way you take a thing out of a cage in a corridor of escape is with a docket and a date and a chain of signatures. And the way a company with a clearing contract answers a cage sitting in its corridor is not with a better cage. It is with a programme.

He had read the condition on the card on Wednesday morning and thought it was the Director being careful.

It was the Director being careful, and it was a card, and the card had a cost, and the cost had arrived on a Saturday morning in a plan chest on a trolley.

---

The tram took forty minutes and he walked the last eleven hundred metres in the rain in the middle of a Saturday afternoon with no shift, no pass, no authority and no card that opened anything on that site, and the only honest word for what he was doing was *trespass*.

The gate was open.

Not wide. Thirty inches, and it had been open since the spring, when a man had cut through the fence once and had clearly thought better of it, and it had been left like that, and on Saturday it was being left like that on purpose, because a man in a good coat had stood in a corridor this morning and written *noted* about a cage and had not put one stone back in a wall.

There were three lorries on the site. Two were up at the far end by the mill office and one was down on the slipway with its tailgate up and a strap hanging off it, and a man in a high-visibility jacket was standing beside the tailgate with a sheet of paper in a board, doing the thing that a man with a consignment note does, which is to read it and then read it again and then look at the water.

Elias got as far as the second section of hoarding.

The slipway is the concrete ramp that goes down from the yard to the old loading stage at the water, and it is the only part of a working mill that is not a building, and it is still there because you cannot cheaply take a concrete ramp away. It had been under six inches of water since the spring because the pumps at the head of it had been switched off when the site was fenced, and nobody had ever switched them on, and nobody ever would, because the water in a slip does not matter when the yard is empty.

And the pumps had come out that morning.

Elias understood that in about a second and a half, standing in the rain, and then he did the only thing he was any good at, which was to stop and look at the exit.

The ramp went down and the water at the bottom of it was up over the top step. There was a timber staging across the head of the slip where the crane used to sit, and two of its bearers had been unbolted, and a third was a split length of scaffold board that had been thrown across for the men to walk on, and the edge of it was six feet over the water, and there was no handrail on that side, and the handrail on the other side had been taken down for the machinery and was in a pile by the office steps.

And on the consignment note, on a clipboard, on the tailgate of a lorry, there was a box with a number in it.

> **ATTENDANT: 2 (TWO)**

And there was one man on that slipway.

---

Cliff Sowerby was seventy-four and he was on the site because Elias Venn had not asked him, and did not know he was coming, and was going to be told about it in a corridor at six o'clock that evening.

He was at number 14, Sillick Lane, and his father had been the fitter's mate in the dye house, and Hattie Bramm had given Elias his number on Friday morning at ten past ten outside a branch library with the rain coming back, with a piece of paper in her hand and a considered number in her coat, and she had said: *he's the one that put the standpipe in. Him and a lad from the gas, and neither of them would take a drink, and I've been trying to get rid of it for thirty-one years and he died in 'ninety-four and his son's still at it.*

Cliff Sowerby had been in a trade himself — a heating engineer, forty years, retired at sixty-five, and he had kept the keys to the row and the row's habits and the row's standing water bills, and he had told Hattie in the spring that there was a steel box in the site office on a shelf behind the meter, and that he had put it there himself when the mill shut, and that it was not the mill's.

It was his father's. The gang sheets — the fitters' record of who had worked what shift, kept in a tin because fitters keep everything, worked out of the gate — had gone into a steel box with a hasp and a padlock and a key, in nineteen seventy-four, in a back room, because the fitters had been told to bring their own papers in, and his father had been one of about nine men who did it, and every one of them had a key, and Cliff Sowerby had the last one, and had had it in a tin of his own since about 1996.

He had not been up the slip in thirty years. He had not been on the site at all. He had watched the site go over from the far pavement on Sillick Lane for thirty years, and he had a woman's wage slip in a biscuit tin in his kitchen which he had promised his mother he would look at one day, and one day had been the morning the hoarding came off.

He came through the gap in the fence at ten past one, and he was wearing a jacket and a flat cap, and he was seventy-four and he had a bad left shoulder and he was carrying a rucksack, and he went up the ramp because the office was on the left at the top and the ramp was the way to the office from the yard, and there was a man in a hi-vis with his back to him, and the man did not turn round, because a man in a hi-vis with a consignment note in his hand is not going to turn round for an old man in a flat cap on a Saturday.

The scaffold board went.

Elias Venn was forty yards away and he saw the whole of it and he could not do a single thing about any part of it. There was a noise like a dropped plank. The old man was on the staging on his side with the rucksack under him and the near end of the staging went in after him with a crack like a shot, and then he was in the water, and the water was four feet deep at the step and eight feet at the outer edge, and it was a canal in October, and it was about half past one in the afternoon.

The man in the high-visibility jacket turned round at the noise and did not shout, and did not go anywhere, and put both hands flat on the air like a man who has been on a site for thirty years and has seen this happen, and said, to nobody, in the voice people use for that, "Right. Right. Oh, that's a bad one."

Elias ran the last forty yards.

He did not go in. He went down the slip on his knees with the rucksack in his hand because it was in his hand, and then he threw it behind him onto the wet concrete, deliberately, hard, at the wall, which is a thing he did not decide to do and could not afterwards account for, and then he went up the office steps three at a time and got the first aid box off the shelf in the office doorway.

Inside the first aid box, on the hook, on a nail, was a coil of line. Eighteen metres of it, a blue polypropylene hawser with a splice eye at one end, a shed thing, kept in a first aid box on a nail because a site with a slip on it has a throw line in it and knows it.

He took it out and ran it back down the ramp and threw it, and it fell short, and he took it in and threw it again, and Cliff Sowerby was at the outer edge with his head and one shoulder out of the water and his good arm up, and got it over his shoulder, and Elias got the other end round the stump of a handrail post on the yard side, which was the only thing on that whole site that was not going anywhere.

And the water was four feet deep and it was a canal in the afternoon of the seventeenth of October and the sky was going and the air was six degrees, and a man of seventy-four was in it up to his chest, and it was not the fall that was going to kill him.

"There is nobody to shout for," Elias said, out loud, on a wet ramp on a Saturday, to nobody, in a building where that sentence had never once been true. "Fault. *Fault.*"

And then a woman came out of the site office cabin and saw the whole thing, and Elias did not remember her ever being there.

---

The gate count on a canal in October is three.

How long he was in it: about four minutes, and Elias could not put it closer than that and could not have put it closer at all, because he was on a wet ramp with a hawser round a post and his hands were going. Four minutes in six-degree water is not survivable in the sense that a man in a museum's office would understand the word. Cliff Sowerby was seventy-four, in a jacket, and he had been in it four minutes, and he was talking.

That was the thing Elias could not get over afterwards. He was on the ramp and the old man was in the canal shouting *I'm all right, I'm all right, hold on, I'm all right* in the voice of a man doing a job, and his good arm was round the line, and his other arm was under him in the water and clearly not working, and the water was taking him.

The woman from the cabin had a phone and got the ambulance and then got the fire brigade, and then came back and stood on the staging with a blanket and did not go in, which was correct. And at some point the woman in the cabin and Elias between them got the old man's other arm, and got it round the line, and got him in against the ramp, and the old man went from shouting to not shouting.

The thing that came up first was the rucksack. It came up out of the water on the line with the old man's arm round it, because he had had it round his neck, and it made a sound like a bucket coming up a well.

It did not matter. Elias wrote that down in the notebook afterwards and underlined it and never showed it to anybody, and it is the only line in that notebook he is ashamed of: *it did not matter, and I noticed that it did not matter, and the reason it did not matter is that I already knew what was in it, and I was looking at the old man.*

---

They got him up. It took twenty minutes and four of the brigade and a man off the lorry, and Cliff Sowerby was a bad colour and had a shoulder that was not where a shoulder is, and they put him in a boat at the head of the slip because it was quicker, and the paramedic said two words that made the whole day go a different shape: *and his head.*

The hospital was in the north of the city and he had a suspected wrist, a dislocated shoulder, an impact to the head from the staging edge, and a temperature of about thirty-four, and he was seventy-four, and he was admitted, and the doctors were careful in the way doctors are careful, which is not the same as being reassuring.

Elias sat on a plastic chair in an anteroom at half past four with a blanket round his shoulders that a nurse had put there without asking, and a man from the site in a hi-vis jacket came in and gave a statement to a policewoman, and then came out and stood in the corridor.

"Name and address for the form."

"Elias Venn. Fourteen Mill Lane."

"That's the museum's address."

"It's a letting agent's address. I'm not at that address. I'm above a laundrette in Low Ward."

The man looked at him.

"You're the one that was on the slip."

"Yes."

"Right." He had a form on a clipboard. "That's a trespass, that is, and I've had to write you on this, and I'll write what I saw and you can add to it if you want, and I'm not going to write anything else because nobody asked me anything else and I'm not going to invent it."

"That's fair."

"Name of the injured."

"Cliff Sowerby. He's at number 14, Sillick Lane, he's seventy-four, and he works — he worked, he worked in heating, and he came up that slip himself, nobody sent him, and he was not warned there was any work on, and I want that in it."

The man wrote it down.

"I want it in that he shouldn't have been on there and I want it in that nobody from that site spoke to him before he went on, and I want it in that the ramp's got no rail on that side and the rail's off on the other and the staging's got a scaffold board on it, and I want it in that the consignment note on the tailgate of that lorry says two men and there was one, and I want that in because he's dead, is he not, he's a man of seventy-four and he's had four minutes in a canal in October, and I want that on a piece of paper in this building tomorrow morning."

The man on the clipboard looked at him for a second.

"It's not my form," he said. "It's a form for an accident."

"Then it's the right form."

---

They let him go at ten past seven with a piece of paper with a crime number on it and a name on it, and he did not get a copy of anything, and he walked to the tram.

And the whole time, for four hours, the thing he could not put down was not the water.

It was the consignment note.

**ATTENDANT: 2 (TWO).**

Not a gate count. Not a pay figure. Not a shortfall. A number in a box on a form that a man reads out loud before a lift, and a man in a high-visibility jacket with the whole of a Saturday crew behind him had read it and gone down a slip on it with one man, because a *consignment note* is not a document anybody checks against the men who turned up. It is a box. It is a box you fill in at the yard and it is a box nobody in the history of the world has ever read back after the event.

And the reason it said two was not a lie and not a fault. It said two because a lifting operation on a slip with a fall into water requires two attendants, and it is the number in the form, and the form was filled in by a young man in a good coat in an office on a Friday, in the ordinary way, and the young man in the good coat was not a liar and had done nothing wrong and would never in his life be asked about a box on a form.

And a man of seventy-four came through a gap in a fence and went up a ramp because it was the way to a door, and the door was not locked and the man at the tailgate of the lorry did not turn round.

Elias Venn sat on a tram in the rain at twenty to eight in the evening with a crime number in his coat and understood that he had spent six days and a great many arguments with himself about the difference between hiding a number and handing one over, and that the number that had put a man in a hospital was in a box on a form about a *lift*.

---

He got back to the museum at half past eight and he went in at the service lane on the shift that was not his and nobody stopped him, and the duty manager was on her stool by the freight lift.

"Saturday," she said. "You're not Saturday."

"Mr Nunn's lot are in the west range corridor and there's a folding ladder by the cage and the Board's phone has been going since one o'clock and there's a woman from a firm called Foyston in the Hall writing down the width of my corridor, and I know that because I've spent six days learning to read this building, and I am telling you as a member of staff and not as a guard, and you can do what you like with it."

"Sit down," Priest said.

He sat down on a sack of frames for about ninety seconds, and then he said:

"Go and ring Mrs Sarn. Ferry Bank. Second floor, first door. She's seventy-eight and she's got a bad chest and it's the only person in this city who can tell you what was on that slip."

"What was on that slip."

"Paper," Elias said. "And a man in an ambulance."

---

By ten past nine on Saturday night the Sillick Lane branch library was shut and Hattie Bramm was in a flat at number 14 with a blanket round her shoulders and a man of seventy-four in a bed in the north of the city, and by half past nine there were four telephone calls inside the museum: from the Board Secretary to the Director about a fall on the Hallowgate site, and from the Director to the police about a name on a statement, and from the Director to Night Services — which is what a suspended guard's rota is administered by — and from a woman in Northglass who had read a police log in about ninety seconds, because a Registry does not wait for a Board.

And Elias Venn was on a loading-bay desk in a building he was not allowed to be on the floors of, with a day book open in front of him, and he wrote down the time, and the time was 22:34, and he had been in the building for an hour and a half and had already had the most consequential four hours of his working life.

Then he put his hand flat on the day book and said out loud, in an empty loading bay, to nobody, because there was nobody there and because he had run out of other things:

"I didn't ask him. I never asked him. I had his number in my pocket on Wednesday and I never once rang him, and I was frightened of what I'd find out, and today a man of seventy-four went into a canal on a wet ramp because I was frightened of what I'd find out."

Nothing in the room answered him.

The cage was four feet away with the bell in it and the red disc on it, and the corridor of escape was still fouled by it, and there was a registered letter upstairs saying the site would be clear by Sunday night, and a box in a cage with a Saturday date on a docket, and a picture of a bone-white stair in a morning report, and a man in a hat, a woman with a print, a district registrar and a conservator, all of them in the building, none of them knowing.

He worked until one in the morning. There was a lot of dead letters, and two hundred and six of them were a clerk's, and a clerk's is a slow job, and he had never in his life been glad of anything so much as he was of a stack of circulars with a clipboard.
