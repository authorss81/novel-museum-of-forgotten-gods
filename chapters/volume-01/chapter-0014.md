# Chapter 0014 — The Woman Who Kept a Copy

The forwarding address on the back of the Museum's envelope was in Low Ward and it was not a street.

> *c/o Mrs J. Rusk, Sillidy Court, Connaught Wing, 4th floor — please pass on.*

The reference to a fourth floor and a named person meant somebody else, and the fourth floor meant that the letter had been forwarded twice, because the fourth floor is where the letters go when the first three have stopped. The reference to a pass on was the single most useful thing on the piece of paper, because it meant that the person who had written *deceased — family* in pencil on the outside of it had not been guessing.

It took him three days to turn the rest of it into a place, and the reason it took three days was that he was doing it in the evening on a loading-bay desk in a building he was not allowed to leave the ground floor of, from memory, with no list of addresses, because the Museum does not keep a list of where anybody lives.

On the Wednesday of the second week he got to 12 Ferry Bank at twenty past one in the afternoon, which is a street of ironmongers and a laundrette and a place that shut at two, above a shop, with a buzzer that did not work and a door you knocked on.

The stairs went up past a flat on the first floor with a cat and a smell of cooking, and stopped at a landing with a window and a landing light that had a bulb in it and a landing carpet with the pattern almost entirely gone, and Doreen Sarn's door was the front one and had a plastic bag on a string loop holding a bundle of envelopes, and the string was knotted, and the knot was the sort of knot a person ties when their hands have got worse.

---

She opened the door before he had finished knocking and she said: "You'll be the one from the Museum."

"I'm not. I'm security."

"You'd come from the office and you wouldn't say it." She was seventy-eight and she was about five foot two and she had a cardigan on over a pinafore and a pair of glasses on a string, and she turned and went back down the passage without waiting. "Come in. Mind the step, it's the step that's the step. I put a paper over it, you can see the paper."

The flat was two rooms. A lino floor with the pattern worn through in a path between the chair and the table and in a second path between the chair and the door. A gas fire with a tea towel over the rail. A radio. A net curtain, half down, and a plastic tumbler on the inside of the windowsill with a teaspoon in it, which told him she had been drinking tea out of a plastic cup for years and that the china was in the press and would stay there.

The table had a cloth on it. On the cloth, squared to the edge of the table, were three things: a blue plastic folder, a hardback cash book with a rubber band round it, and a hard copy of a letter that had been folded in three so many times that the ink had come through the creases.

On the table, at the far end, eleven feet away, there was an oil lamp that had been converted. Navy blue enamel over pressed steel. A cradle handle. A short lead ending in a bayonet cap. A dead filament.

Elias Venn stood in a doorway in Ferry Bank and looked at it, and had the specific experience of a man who has heard a room in the dark for four seconds and has just been let into it.

"Sit down," Doreen Sarn said. "I'll put the kettle on. You'll want a cup, and I'll not have a cup of tea with a man stood up in my front room, I've not that much left."

He sat down.

---

She made the tea and brought it in and sat down in the other chair and did not drink hers, and looked at him, and waited, and did not help him, and it was the most patient silence he had sat in for three days.

"I've come about the mill," he said.

"Aye."

"I'm not from the office."

"No."

"I can't tell you why I know what I know, and you shouldn't ask me, and if you ask me I'll lie to you badly." He put his hands flat on the cloth. "That's the first thing. There's going to be more and it's all going to be like that."

"Then don't bother with the shape of it. Go on."

"My building has a box of your old mill's office papers on a pallet. It's a museum, four miles up the hill. It came in on a van from the works on the last working day in October 1974 with a lamp and eleven other items, out of a solicitor's office in Northglass, on a receipt, and it has never been opened, and there's now a note on it in handwriting that isn't a printed label, and the note is a live staff number at my building, which is a way of saying that somebody in the last few days has gone looking in the same direction I have and got further than me."

Doreen Sarn put her cup down on the table without any noise at all.

"1974," she said.

"The seventh of November 1974. It's a carbon. It's the office copy and it lists eleven items and item ten is a bundle of wage sheets tied with tape, and the van is on it, and a lamp is on it, and a man in a fitter's trade is on it, and it went on a shelf and it has been on a shelf for fifty years." He did not put a hand on anything. "You told me it went in the second post and you told me it came back in the sack and you told me it went in the van. That's item five, or item ten, and it went out of this building on the Friday, and it came in here in the same load, and nobody has ever opened it."

"And what have you come to ask me about it," said Doreen Sarn, "because you have not asked me a question yet and you have been here twenty minutes."

"No, ma'am."

"No." She turned the hardback cash book about on the cloth. "It's furniture, and in about three weeks' time a man with a dog and a skip is going to take it away, and I'll tell you exactly what happens to paper in a building like yours. It doesn't get read. It gets *weighed*. A merchant weighs it and it goes on a lorry and it goes to a place where paper goes, and there is no version of that where a wage sheet in a box of office papers is found by anybody who is looking for it, because a wage sheet looks like a wage sheet and a wage sheet weighs the same as a circular."

"Then somebody's got to open it."

"Somebody's got to open it and read it and copy it and give the copy to a person who will stand up in a room and be cross-examined on it. That's four steps and none of them happen by accident and all four of them take money." She looked at him over the glasses. "Are you the fourth step?"

"No, ma'am."

"Then what are you?"

"I'm the fourth step with no authority, no money, and a list of what I did wrong that I gave to the Board Secretary on Tuesday in an envelope."

Doreen Sarn sat back in her chair and for the first time looked at him properly, and the room went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with him.

"Say the last part again," she said.

And so he told her, and it took twenty minutes, and he did it in the order it had happened and without stopping, and he told her about the four photographs and the drawer and the finisher who had told him where the drawer was and would not put it in writing, and the statement, and the fault report with four words of it false, and the fact that he had a number in a notebook and a name in a notebook and had said no to a question that had been asked to him directly.

And Doreen Sarn listened the way a person listens who has been a clerk for forty years, and did not interrupt once, and at the end of it she said:

"Twelve lines of that is you being hard on yourself and it's the part you think is the important part. The important part is that you heard a bell in a case and you got a figure out of it and you never wrote it down in the book with the other names in it. Have you got it written anywhere a person can reach?"

"No, ma'am."

"Then whatever you were protecting, you weren't protecting it, because a thing in a coat pocket is a thing that dies with the coat. That's not me being clever. I've been a pay clerk since before you could walk."

---

"You've brought me something," she said. "That's why you came and not a telephone."

"I have."

"Then put it down and don't talk while I read it, because I'm seventy-eight and I read slower than you do and I have to get to the end of it in one go or I'll lose the thread, and there are four things on that page and I want them in the right order."

He put the three sheets on the cloth.

The first was the Museum's letter of the ninth of October 1976, the office copy, returned and dead and photocopied by a branch library and copied out in a man's block hand.

The second was a sheet of Museum notepaper on which he had written, standing up, on a loading-bay desk, the nine entries of a printed inventory headed **GALLERY OF UNNAMED HANDS — OBJECT NUMBERS**, which he had read off a clipboard inside a door on Civic Hill in the dark on Monday night, and beneath it the nine names, and beneath that, in brackets: *Eight cased. One plinth, felt top, no case, drawer 6B.*

And the third was a page of his own with a typewritten line on it, which he had been up most of the night typing at the desktop terminal in the security room, which was permitted, and which printed a carbon and did not keep a record of anybody's name, and which was a thing he had thought about for two days and would think about for a great deal longer.

Doreen Sarn read the 1976 letter first, and she did not read it, because she had had it in her hand for forty-eight years, and she put two fingers on it and said, "Aye," and went on to the second.

She read the nine entries. She went down the list with a finger, the way you go down a list you have done a thousand times, and she did not hurry, and at the seventh her finger stopped for slightly longer than at the others and came on, and Elias did not know why and thought about it for four days.

Then she came to the ninth and read it out loud.

"**Lamp, fitter's, electric, navy enamel.**"

"That's a shop lamp." She put the glasses off and cleaned them on the hem of the cardigan, which is a thing you do when you want a moment and do not want it to look like one. "That's the fitters' one, that is. There's four benches in that shop and a lamp on each one and a mate to each one, and the office had a pair of them, and I bought the office's off the forecourt for two shillings on the last morning, and that's the fitters' lamp in front of you and it's been to a museum and back round again and I don't suppose it knows it. Mine's its mate and it's in the press and it's been there fifty years and I'll not have anybody handle it."

"How much did you pay for that one?"

"Two shillings, same as mine." She said it and then looked up. "Aye. I know. Two shillings. It's a fortune. I've thought about the two shillings for fifty years and it's a fortune and I'd do it again and that's the whole trouble with me."

She put her glasses back on and went to the third sheet.

And it was a long time, and Doreen Sarn did not move, and the gas fire made a small noise.

"Where did you get that," she said.

"There is a receipt in a drawer in a gallery at the Museum. It's a carbon, from 1974, and it lists eleven items collected at the works by the estate's agent, and item nine is the lamp, and item ten is a bundle of wage sheets tied with tape and marked *pay office*, and they came into this building in the same van on the same afternoon."

"In the same van." She said it to the cloth.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Who brought them?"

"The receipt says collected by P. Loach, fitter, at an address in Sillick Lane." Elias held on to his own voice. "The same eleven words are on the Museum's own posting sheets for that address, for grade two porters, for a decade, in this building's own record."

Doreen Sarn put her hand over her mouth and left it there for about four seconds, and then she took it away, and said:

"Perry."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Perry Loach was in the fitters' shop with me on the last morning, and he carried a lamp down to the gate on his shoulder because I'd no way of carrying it, and he got a handcart off the bench and wheeled it out to the road himself, and I never gave him a penny for it and he never asked me for one." Her voice did not change at all. "And he was a good lad, and he got the last gate count, and he came in on that, and he was one of the ones that never got their—" and then she stopped.

And then she said, in a completely different voice:

"Item five."

"Ma'am?"

"You said item five." She had both hands on the tablecloth now. "What does item five say?"

"Envelope of loose papers, sealed, contents not examined."

Doreen Sarn sat in her chair by a gas fire and looked at a piece of Museum notepaper on which a suspended night guard had written out eleven lines of a carbon, and her mouth went, and she put her hand over it.

"Then it's in the box," she said. "Then it's the post."

"What's the post?"

"The mill's post. When a mill closes, the post comes back to the mill, because the mill is the sender of record and there's nowhere else for it to go, and the office clears it into a sack and the sack goes with the rest of the office paper, because a sack of post is office paper." She took her hand away from her mouth. "I put an envelope in the second post on the twenty-second of October in my own writing with a proper stamp on it, and it went out of the building, and it came back to the building about ten days later, and I took it out of the sack and I did not look at it, because I knew what was in it and I'd just sent it, and I put it back in the office's out-tray and it went in the van."

Elias Venn put his hand on the tablecloth and left it there.

"So there's a wage sheet in a museum on a plinth's worth of shelf away from a box, in a city four miles away, in a drawer, in an envelope, with a date on the top corner of the twenty-second of October 1974 in your own writing."

"Aye."

"And nobody in this building has ever opened that envelope, because the card on the box says *administrative papers, disposal* and a box of disposal goes on a shelf."

"That'll be it," said Doreen Sarn.

She had the hardback cash book in her hand without any sign of having picked it up.

"Thirty-one years I've had that cash book," she said. "Thirty-one years I've had a copy of the sheet I made for myself, with the same columns and the same figures in it and my own hand on both, and I have never once been able to find out whether the sheet the mill sent to the bank is the same as the sheet I sent to myself, and neither of us was a liar, and it has eaten my life in exactly the way you're describing to me and I have been doing it alone since about four o'clock this afternoon."

"You've come about the lamp," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"You've come about what the lamp saw."

Elias Venn sat in a chair in a flat in Ferry Bank and did not say a word, and that silence went on for a good deal longer than was comfortable, and Doreen Sarn looked at him across a table with a cloth on it, and he understood that he had just made a mistake, and that the mistake was that he had said *the lamp saw*.

"Then you can tell me," she said, "or you can not, and I am seventy-eight and I have a bad chest on a cold afternoon and I would rather you did not tell me than tell me badly. But you will tell me eventually, and when you do I will not be impressed, because there is nothing in a lamp and there never has been, and I have had a mate of that lamp in the press in this flat since the week it came out of the shop."

---

He did not tell her then, and did not tell her on the Thursday, and told her on the Sunday in a laundrette in a way that cost him the only thing he had ever wanted to keep, and that was the whole of what happened between those two days and it belongs to the Sunday and not to this afternoon.

What he did on the Wednesday afternoon was hand her a third thing, which was a fact and not a mystery, and which he had been carrying since Thursday night of the first week standing up in a corridor with a torch in his teeth.

"Mill Lane. Do you know it?"

"Aye."

"There's a block of flats on the mill side called Mill Lane, and my building's address is 14 Mill Lane, and my building has a letting office, and from 1998 my building has been the postal address for every man it employed on the hourly grades." He put a finger on the cloth, an inch from the hardback cash book, and left it there. "There's a man called Wendel Case. He was a night porter in my building for nineteen years. He was on the books of the city payroll bureau at a different address from mine, and the bureau's remittance records carry his pension and his union subscription every month, and he has a locker in my basement with a tea tin in it. And he has not been on my building's books for eighteen months. And I'm not here to ask you about him, and I'll come back to him."

"Then why are you telling me?"

"Because in about a fortnight," Elias said, "somebody in the wrong room is going to work out that the same eleven words on that posting sheet are the same eleven words on a returned envelope, and they are going to say he was living at 14 Mill Lane. And he wasn't. A letting agent is a post office in a building. A man can be written to at 14 Mill Lane for eleven years and live over the laundrette in Shale Row, and both of those things can be true, and the reason they can both be true is that nobody in my building has ever had to be in the same room as him to know where he slept."

He tapped the table once, gently, with a knuckle.

"And your flat," he said. "This one. That's a letting too, isn't it."

Doreen Sarn put her hand out flat on the cloth.

"Seventeen years I was in this flat," she said, "and it was a letting. I was Mrs Rusk to the postman and Mrs Rusk to the gas and Mrs Rusk to the shop, and when they put the letters through the door with two names on them I was Mrs Rusk to the letters as well." She pushed the glasses up. "Aye. It's a letting. Everything in this district is a letting and everybody's been moved twice since the war and nobody's got a deed."

"Then I want to say something to you that I would not say to a stranger, and I am saying it to you because you are the only person I have met this week who is owed something and has a copy of it."

"Go on."

"You've spent nine years writing to a firm that isn't there any more, and you have had four replies, and three of them were from an office with a telephone number and one of them was from a firm that has been at the same address since before you started writing, and you have a hardback cash book with a rubber band round it and there is a thing in it that they have never seen." He looked at it. "It's the only copy in the world of something that everybody in every building I have been in this week has been treating as though it doesn't exist. And I am not here to take it off you."

Doreen Sarn's hand was flat on the cloth, an inch from the cash book, and it had been there the whole time, and it did not move at all.

"Then what are you here for," she said.

"I'm here to tell you that the box is real, and that it is not going to be opened by me, and that in three weeks it is going to be weighed. And to ask you one question, and I am going to ask it badly, and I'd rather ask it badly than not ask it."

"Go on, then."

"Was the sheet put in the mill's own post?"

Doreen Sarn looked at him for a long time.

And then she laughed, which she had not done once in the whole afternoon, and it was not a happy sound and it lasted about a second and a half.

"No," she said. "It went out in the *second* post, and it went in an ordinary envelope, and the address on it was in my own writing, and the stamp was a proper stamp and not a chit, and the date on the top corner was ten days before the last shift. And I put it in the second post because I wanted it to be *dated*, because a thing with a date on it is a thing that happened on the day it says, and a thing that went out in a van with everything else is a thing that happened whenever anybody said it did."

"What was the date on the top corner?"

"Twenty-second of October, nineteen seventy-four." She said it to the cloth. "And the mill shut on the thirtieth, and it was the last week of trading, and a mill in its last week of trading does not post anything, and I posted it anyway, out of a habit I had had for twenty-six years, into a *second* post that had one bag in it because everything else had already gone to the van."

"The van."

"The estate's agent's van, with the stuff, on the Friday."

"Who was the agent?"

"I don't know. It was a firm." She took her hand off the cloth and folded both in her lap. "It was a firm in the North End with a man in it who had a bad chest and a good hand, and I never asked his name because I never had to ask anybody's name at that mill, that's the sort of firm it was."

"Then that's the second envelope," Elias said, "and the wage sheets themselves, and both of them went out of this building in the same van on the same Friday, and neither of them has ever been read."

---

They rang at ten past four.

Doreen Sarn had made a telephone call at about a quarter to three, on a telephone on a sideboard with a coiled cord, while he was in the kitchen, and she had told him about it afterwards in one sentence: *I telephoned a firm I've been writing to for nine years and told them a man from the Museum was coming, because I'd rather you were introduced than found out, and because it's my flat and my name on your letter.*

The knock came at half past four and it was two of them: a man of about fifty in a coat that had cost money, and a young woman with a case and a lanyard. He gave his name as Mr Rhys and he gave the firm as Quill & Frayle and he gave the address as fourteen Lincoln's Lane, and he did all three in that order, and the address was on the museum receipt Elias had read in the dark on Monday night.

"I'm Doreen Sarn," she said. "I've been writing to you nine years."

"Ma'am." He had her file. She had not had to tell him anything; he had a plastic wallet and it was thick, and he was the fourth or fifth person in a week to open a plastic wallet in front of Elias Venn, and the reason was always the same, which was that paper is heavier than people expect and harder to get rid of. "Doreen Sarn, and there's a hundred and forty-one items. I'd like to say I'm sorry, and I'd like to say that we'd have answered all of them."

"You have answered all of them."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Then don't."

He took the refusal the way a man takes a refusal from a client, which is by not showing anything at all, and he looked at Elias, and did the arithmetic, and did it fast, and said:

"You're not from the Museum."

"No."

"Because if you were, there'd be two of you."

"Mr Rhys," Doreen Sarn said. "That's Elias, and he's not from the office, and I've had him here since twenty past one, and he's come about a lamp, and before you say anything about the lamp I want to say that I'll not sign anything this afternoon and I don't intend to be advised by anybody in a coat about my own money, and I have said that on the telephone this afternoon already, so if I've said it twice it's because it's true."

"No, ma'am."

"Mary," said the young woman, "there's a file reference on the call sheet."

"I know there's a file reference on the call sheet, and I've had nine years to get used to that, and you can put the call sheet in your file and be quite welcome to it." Doreen Sarn stood up, which took a hand on the table. "There's a lamp on that table, Mr Rhys. Navy blue, cradle handle, an electric one off an oil frame. It isn't mine and it never was. It's the office's, and it came out of the fitters' shop with the rest of the lot, and there's a mate to it in a museum four miles up that hill, and I would like to know what a solicitor thinks a pair of shop lamps from a mill that closed fifty years ago is worth."

Mr Rhys looked at it for about two seconds, and then he said, in a completely different voice:

"That's a good question, ma'am, and I want to give you a straight answer rather than a comforting one, and I'd like to write it down and give you a copy on the standard terms."

"Go on."

"In 1976 we acknowledged correspondence about that disposal, and there's a file, and the file number is the same one that was on the letter in your folder, which is 413, and that means the letter you have is a matter we dealt with and we have kept the file for forty-eight years." He put the wallet down on the table and did not open it, because there was no reason to open it. "If those lamps were on the pay office bench at the material time then they were plant, and plant passes to the mortgagee and then to the purchaser, and my client's interest in this building's contents is in maintaining a complete chain, and we will be compiling a schedule of the works' records for the purposes of a claim that is not ours and that we have been asked to assist."

"And you want them."

"We want a valuation, ma'am, and we want a photograph, and we'd like to be told in writing when you got the mate and from whom. I don't want to argue about a lamp. There's nothing in a lamp." He said it without any weight at all, and he was right, and Elias put his hand flat on his knee. "What my client wants is not lamps. My client wants the site clear on the fourth of November, and the site cannot be cleared while there is a claim out of the historic record attached to it, and every item anybody produces, from any side, is a reason for another fortnight."

"Which brings me to my lady's next question," Doreen Sarn said, "which is whether a list exists. Whether there's a list of what was in that van, and what left the mill on that Friday, and where it all went."

"There is a list."

"Then you'll give it to me."

"The list exists," said Mr Rhys, "and I'm not permitted to give it to you, and I want to be exact about why, because the why is the whole of my profession. It's a clearance schedule. It's signed by a receiver in 1976, and the office that signed it no longer exists, and my client's title comes through that office, and a document that my client's title comes through is a document that cannot be handed to a party with a claim running against it without an order of the tribunal or a deed, and if I gave it to you in a front room in Ferry Bank on a Wednesday afternoon then in two years' time a judge would hear the words *Quill and Frayle handed a clearance schedule to a claimant in a front room* and my client would be ordered to pay the costs of about four hundred other people's claims." He said it gently. "I have forty-one other clients, ma'am, and eleven of them are in exactly your position, and I have never lied to one of them, and I am not going to start with you because you are the one who telephoned me first."

Doreen Sarn sat back down.

"Then I am one of forty-one."

"You are one of forty-one, and it is my genuine opinion, sitting in this chair, that it is worse for you that I can say that so easily than it is for you that I can't help you." He took out a card. "The office is open nine to five and I am in on Saturdays. If anybody ever comes to me with a document from that works — any document, anything, a scrap of a wage book with a page torn out of it — I will take it, I will date it, I will tell you the day it arrived, and it will be in a document schedule that a tribunal cannot ignore. That is not a favour. That is what the schedule of the claim is for. It is the only lever anybody in your position has and nobody ever uses it, because it is a lever that works at two years and not at two weeks."

---

He left at ten past five with a card and a photograph of a lamp and a *Mailed* receipt for a letter, and Doreen Sarn saw him to the door and made him hold it while she got her coat, because of the step.

"Sonnit," she said, on the landing. "The claim's not just a claim. I did the accounts as well, and I'll tell you that now before you get it out of me, because you will, and I'd rather it was me."

"Ma'am."

"On the last morning, the wage sheets and the pay run didn't agree. That's a thing that happens every week in every mill in this city, and usually it's the wage sheet that's wrong, because a man's missed a day." She had her coat on and her hands in the sleeves. "And on that morning it was the pay run. And I was the only clerk in that office who could make the two agree, because I'd been doing it for three years, and every other week I made them agree by writing a figure off the time book, and there were never more than two, and I knew both men, and I'd take it out of their tea money if I had to."

"What did you do on the last morning?"

"I did what I always did." She said it without any defiance in it at all, which was the thing that made it unbearable. "I wrote the pay run's figure onto the wage sheet, and I made the two books agree, and one of them was wrong, and I knew which one, and I did it anyway, because a mill has to close on a Friday, and there is no version of a mill not closing on a Friday."

---

He walked down to the corner of Ferry Bank and Shale Row and stood in the wind and wrote the whole of it in his notebook, in the block hand, standing up, and the two dates were on the page: the twenty-second of October, 1974, and the fourth of November.

And then he got the tram back up the hill with the two shillings and the seventy-eight-year-old woman in a cardigan and the man in the good coat in his head, and came into the loading bay at ten past six, and stood at the desk, and looked at the bag on the corner of it.

The Monday bag.

It was a canvas post bag with a canvas flap and a lead seal, and it had been standing on the corner of the loading-bay desk since he had started doing this job, and it was where the museum's registered and recorded post went on a Monday morning, and it had been going out of that door every Monday for the whole of his working life.

His envelope was in it. Four photographs, in a block-letter hand, for the Board Secretary, registered, with a receipt.

And the same bag, on the same Monday, every Monday, for eleven years, had gone out with the post of a letting agent, addressed to a man at 14 Mill Lane, and it had come back, and it had gone into the bin behind the loading bay, and the bin went out on a Monday as well, because the paper merchant came on Mondays, and on Mondays the blue bales stood by the roller door in the rain in a row, and every one of them was a Monday bag's worth of a man's post, and none of them had ever been opened, and none of them was ever going to be, and the only reason he knew it was because he had spent three days learning to read the small print of this building and the small print said *post is received, not opened, not kept*, and every museum in the country had that line, and he had signed next to it for two years.
