# Chapter 0015 — A Lift and Not a Removal

The Board papers came down onto the systems terminal at four minutes past nine on Thursday morning with a Board paper number on them, and Elias read them standing up at the loading-bay desk because a man on a restriction has nothing else to do with a Thursday.

> **MORROWGATE MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS — BOARD**
> SAFETY AND COLLECTIONS SUB-COMMITTEE
> Tuesday 21 October, 18:00, Committee Room. Papers circulated Wednesday.
> **Item 3** — Reader fault, restricted access, door 7, week 1: maintenance position and actions.
> **Item 4** — Incident report and insurance position, week 1.
> **Item 5** — Audit and Assurance: schedule of contents **unavailable since 11 September**; recovery and control.
> **Item 6** — Any other business.

Item 5 was the one that mattered and it was one line long, and it was on the agenda of a sub-committee of a public trust in a building where a suspended man had spent two years reading the reconciliation column every month.

He wrote it in the notebook in his own words, which was a habit he had and which he was aware of, and which he was doing anyway, because the alternative was that the sentence lived in his head until Tuesday.

Then the morning post came in at half past ten and the tray came down, and the tray was the grey steel one and the book was the one with his name in it in three columns, and the book had a printed line across the head that he had read every shift for two years and had not read once.

> **NO PHOTOGRAPHY OF COLLECTIONS MATERIAL, LABELS, OR RECORDS.**
> **THE BOOK IS THE RECORD. THE TRAY IS NOT.**

---

Anja Ward came down at eleven and she was carrying a sheet of card the size of a small envelope and she put it on the loading-bay desk and squared it to the wood before she said anything at all, which was so entirely her that Elias had a moment of something that was not quite fondness and was not quite fear.

"Director's standing conditions," she said. "Movement and access of Schedule 12 carried objects. Four of them. They came into effect at ten o'clock this morning and they apply to the bell and to the box and to the lamp if anybody ever lets me know there's a lamp."

> **1.** No Schedule 12 carried object to be moved, caged, or removed from the building except under a written docket, countersigned by the Director, stating the destination and the date, and to remain open on the desk of the post holding the docket until the object is on its pallet and the pallet is sealed.
>
> **2.** No Schedule 12 carried object to be removed from the building except on a docket countersigned **by a person who is not an officer or employee of this Museum.** My signature is not sufficient.
>
> **3.** No public access to a Schedule 12 carried object between the posting of a proposed disposition and the execution of that disposition. A proposed disposition not executed within ten working days lapses, and the object returns to its last certified condition and its last certified place.
>
> **4.** The Hall of Former Faiths shall not be opened to a school group, or to any party not holding a collections pass, while any Schedule 12 carried object in it is caged, moved, or otherwise out of its last certified condition.

Elias read it three times.

"Number four is going to be a problem," he said. "The bell is in a cage in a service corridor now. It is out of its last certified condition. You cannot open that Hall to a school group until somebody puts it back and says so in writing."

"I am aware of it. It's a condition I wrote on Thursday morning at six o'clock in order to force the question of where the bell goes, because I did not have time to force it properly and this is a document that forces it." She took her hand off the card. "And I want you to understand that I have issued four conditions in nine days that are all aimed at this building, and that not one of them is aimed at you, and that this is not generosity. It is that you are the cheapest thing in this building to leave alone."

"Ma'am—"

"You have a restriction that has been re-described in the payroll system as leave, which means I cannot dismiss you and cannot pay you, and which also means that in a fortnight somebody has to write a sentence about you on this piece of card and put it in a drawer, and I have looked at what I would have to write." She put her hands in her coat pockets. "Elias, I have been doing this for nineteen years and I have never had a week like it, and the sentence is going to be *the employee was unable to act within the terms of his restriction and the information available to him was, in my judgement, material to a matter before the Board*, and that sentence is true, and that sentence is going to be read in a room by a man who has a liability position, and it will make you somebody's problem permanently."

"That sounds like you telling me not to hope."

"That is me telling you that I have already spent something on you and I would like it to cost me one sentence." She looked up. "And you have about four minutes before I have to go and do the thing I cannot tell you about, and the thing I cannot tell you about is that I have been instructed, as a director, by two solicitors, to do the thing I cannot tell you about, and that I intend to do it, and that when it is done I will not be able to say publicly that I did it, because the privilege is mine and not yours, and you did not make it mine, and I have to live in that."

She went up.

---

The Registry notice came on the same post and it was four lines long, and it was countersigned in a box at the bottom, and it was a document that had taken two bodies and three signatures to say almost nothing.

> **QA/N/34** — Morrowgate Museum of Quiet Saints, 1961/LW/44.
> Site works at sub-level B: **completed.** Third ward-bell, South Sluice: **out of service. Will not return to service this quarter.** District lock positions: unchanged.
> Museum action required: none.
> *Morrowgate District Works, River Ward* / *countersigned: Registry, records and bell office*

Elias read it at the desk and then he read it a second time with his finger on the third line, and then he sat down on a sack of frames, which he had not sat down on for a week.

*District lock positions: unchanged.*

He had spent nine days working out that a bell in a sluice station four hundred metres away had something to do with a bell in a hall four floors above him, and the answer was not a wire. The answer was that a thing had stopped being held down, and that the holding down had a maintenance organisation behind it, and that the maintenance organisation had just written on a piece of letterhead that it had stopped, that it was not coming back this quarter, and that everything else was where it had always been.

They had taken it out of the building's control and told the building it had nothing to do, and they had done it in a form that said the *bell* was fine.

The bell was fine. The bell was the only object in that building that had never been damaged. It had a staple in it. It was not the problem.

And there was a piece of pressed brass on a bracket in a corridor on the second floor, hard-piped to the ward-bell repeater line, that had answered every sound the sluice station had made for two weeks, half a tone flat, and that at eleven o'clock that morning had stopped, silently, without a fault and without anybody coming, because a repeater has nothing to repeat.

The corridor outside the unlisted room was going to be quieter from that morning than it had been in eighteen years, and no door in this building was ever going to be opened because of it, and he was going to think about it at some point in the next four years for no reason he would ever be able to put in a document.

---

The device tray went at about half past one in the afternoon, on the same post, under the same heading, and it was the single most sensible thing that happened to Elias Venn all week.

> **NOTICE — DEVICE POLICY, REVISED, 15 OCTOBER**
> Personal telephones are no longer signed into a numbered tray.
> Devices are issued and returned at the front desk and signed for in the **day book**, one line per device, name, time out, time back, and the department.
> The tray has been removed to the paper store.
> The printed notice on the front desk now reads: **NO PHOTOGRAPHY OF COLLECTIONS MATERIAL, LABELS, OR RECORDS. THE DAY BOOK IS THE RECORD OF ISSUE AND RETURN.**
> *W. Ward, Director. Reviewed with the Board Secretary and Health and Safety.*

Two people came down at half past one with a sack truck and took the grey steel cupboard — two hundred and six slots, three columns, unlocked, with a sign wired to the front of it that said PHONES — off the table at the service entrance and down the service lane, and Elias watched it go and felt, for a moment, something he had not expected to feel about a piece of furniture.

Then he read the notice again and the feeling went.

It was right. It was right in the way that everything in this building was right, which was to say it was right about the thing that was easy to fix. A hundred and eighty unused slots and a book was a system that demonstrated nothing and cost a review. Removing it was an improvement. It made the Museum look as though it had heard a complaint and done something about it inside forty-eight hours, which it had, and it had done it about the thing that had been photographed and not about the thing that had been opened.

And the effect on the four photographs on a telephone in his coat was precise and he could work it out in about four seconds, standing there with the empty table in front of him.

Before: a telephone could be signed out of slot twelve and signed back in, and the book said so, and the book was a record.

After: a telephone carried in a coat pocket in a gallery was in a coat pocket in a gallery, and the book said nothing, and there was no slot to be missing, and the tray that had been the evidence was in the paper store being used for something else.

He had taken four photographs on a telephone that the Museum could once have proved he had in his hand, and the reason it could no longer prove it was that the Museum had improved itself, eleven days after he had broken a rule, and had done so in a document with a date and a Director's initials on it.

He wrote that down too, in the notebook, and then he crossed it out, because a man who writes down the fact that an institution improved itself at the moment it stopped being able to see him is a man who has started keeping a second set of books again, and he had given the second set away on Tuesday in a sealed envelope and it was in the Monday bag on the desk beside him.

---

There was a letter in the tray.

It was on the bottom, under a circular and a bank statement, and it was in a plain envelope, and on the front of it, in a hand that had been taught at some point and had gone rusty, it said:

> **MR W. CASE**
> **14 MILL LANE**
> **LOW WARD**
> **MORROWGATE**

Elias Venn stood at a loading-bay desk in a building that had had this man's name rubbed off a card with a rubber in a drawer in 1987 and had kept his pension going for eighteen months in a system it did not own, and read his own name on an envelope.

There was no *not delivered* on it and no pencil. There was a forwarding sticker on the front, the kind a post office puts on when a redirection has expired, and a date on the sticker, and the date was six weeks ago.

Inside, because it was post for this building and the post room of this building opened everything that came for it, and because two members of the Museum's own staff had to be present for a document, and because a letter addressed to *14 Mill Lane, Low Ward* is addressed to this building and therefore is a record of this building:

---

> **MR W. CASE**
> 14 Mill Lane, Low Ward
> **and if he isn't there then to whoever is**
>
> **Re: my father's account and the money going out of it**
>
> Sir,
>
> I am writing this for my father. He cannot write, and I am not going to tell you he could, and if you want to know why he cannot write you can look at the pay slips, because for nineteen years they went out in his name and not one of them was ever any good to read.
>
> You have had his money since the new year. Not all of it. Some weeks a bit, some weeks most of it, and there is no week now that it is all of it, and I have stopped asking the girls at the office what is going on because they say the same thing and I can tell when a person is saying the same thing.
>
> I am not writing to be angry. He is fifty-eight and I am not going to be able to do anything about it and I do not think you will either, and that is not an insult, that is just the shape of it.
>
> He has had the tin. That is the tin with the tea in it and he has had it from the start. There is a bit of paper in the tin with figures on it that you said you would send him and never did, and he has kept it in the tin with the photographs of his sister, and I am not saying this to catch you out. I am saying it because you told him the money was in the tin and he has been putting his hands in a tin for a year and a half and there is a bit of paper in it with figures on it and you did not tell him that.
>
> You can stop his money for a week and we can live on a week. You cannot stop it for a year.
>
> We know about the money.
>
> Yours,
> **I. Case**
> *(writing for my father, who cannot write, and who is at number 14, Sillick Lane, and has been at number 14 since the year the mill shut, and who did the last count at the gate on the Friday morning, and who says he has never had anything to do with the paper in the tin and will not have it on his record)*
>
> *(the money goes to my mother's name and my mother's dead since 1994 and I do not know how to change it and I have not been able to make myself ring them about it)*

---

He read it three times.

The first time he read it as a document. On the second reading he was aware that he was gripping the edge of the desk with both hands.

Because the letter is perfect, and it is perfect in the way that only a letter written by somebody who has been let down a small amount, repeatedly, by an institution, is perfect. It is not a claim. It does not threaten anything. It says *we know about the money* and it is the only sentence in it that is frightening, and it is frightening because there is no way on earth to answer it.

And it is signed by a person with a name on it. I. Case, writing for his father. And the post went to 14 Mill Lane because that is the address on every posting sheet in the personnel rings for grade two porters for a decade, and it came back with a forwarding sticker on it, and it came back to a loading-bay desk in a building where the man's name has been rubbed off a card with a rubber.

And there is a bit of paper in the tin.

There is a bit of paper in the tin, and the tin is in locker eighty-one, in a run of forty-one lockers numbered sixty-one to a hundred and one, in the basement under the stair, on a printed card Elias had read over every night for two years without reading it, and the locker has a brass name plate on it riveted to the door that says **CASE, W.**, and the door is unlatched, and it has been unlatched for eighteen months, and nothing about that locker has ever required a decision.

He stood at the desk for a long time with the letter in his hand.

---

He went down at half past four.

The basement under the stair is cold in a way the rest of the building is not, and it smells of wet cardboard and cold stone and, from twenty feet away, of the tea that had been in that tin for a long time. Two rows of steel lockers, and the number card by the door, and a gap in the card where a number should have been that he had been reading over for two years the way you read a missing tile.

Eighty-one.

The plate was Museum-issue, riveted, the same as every other one, and the brass had gone dull round the edge of the letters where a thumb had lifted it off and put it back a great many times, the way you handle a plate you read every day for nineteen years.

The door was not locked. It had never been locked. Nothing about that locker had ever required a decision.

He put his hand on it and did not open it.

---

He stood in the basement of a building he was not permitted to be on, on a Thursday afternoon in October, with his hand on a door, and worked the whole of it out properly, standing up, the way he did everything, because that was the only way he knew to keep a thing from getting away from him.

The thing was a person, and the person had a daughter, and the daughter had written a letter that said *we know about the money*, and the letter was in the post room of a museum on the strength of a forwarding sticker that had expired six weeks ago, and if the Museum did something with that letter, then by Friday the letter would be read in four rooms in this building, and by the following Friday it would have a file reference, and by the end of the month the file would have gone to the Board, and the Board would have a sub-committee item, and the sub-committee would want a policy on unaddressed correspondence, and a policy is the cheapest and most permanent thing an institution can make.

And he knew exactly what the policy would say, because he could read it now, standing in a basement with his hand on a door, and it would say that correspondence addressed to Museum letting addresses and returned undeliverable is held for twelve months and then destroyed, and that no member of staff may act on a returned item without the written authority of the Director, and that the existence of an item of correspondence is not of itself evidence that the addressee is or was a member of staff.

Every word of it correct. Every word of it written by somebody who was trying to be fair. And a daughter and a father in number 14 would be told, in the courteous language of a museum, that the Museum could not confirm that Mr W. Case had ever been a member of its staff, and the correspondence would be destroyed on schedule, and the tin would stay in locker eighty-one, and nobody would ever know that there had been a letter.

That was the worst of it. Not that somebody would be cruel. That a document would be *correct*.

And the other thing, which was the thing he actually had to look at, and which he looked at for a long time.

It would be the second time. It was the same shape as the second time.

The first time was a name in a coat pocket, and a box in a corridor, and a number nobody could reach, and it had kept for eleven days because he could not bear to hand it to a person who would have to use it. And on Tuesday he had handed four photographs to a duty manager in a sealed envelope, because a thing in a coat is a thing that dies with the coat, and it had worked, and it had cost him nothing that anybody could count.

This was the same, only bigger, and it had a tin in it.

He was not going to open locker eighty-one. Not because it was locked — it was not locked, it had never been locked — and not because opening it would be a rule, because nobody had ever made a rule about a basement locker, which was the exact reason it would have been the worst thing he had ever done. He was not going to open it because the moment he opened it, the tin belonged to him, and a thing that belongs to him is a thing he can lose, and a thing he can lose is a thing that goes on a Friday for a table in Northglass, and the second time a person in overalls said a number and got no answer, it would be because a man in a basement had wanted to be the only person who knew.

So Elias Venn stood in the basement at twenty to five on Thursday with his hand on a door, and took his hand off it, and went upstairs, and told Mara Iles.

---

He found her on the first floor, in the conservation corridor, with the inventory sheets from the Gallery of Unnamed Hands in a stack in front of her and a cup of tea going cold beside her, and he had never been so glad to see anybody in his life, and she was not glad to see him.

"I have been here since one o'clock," she said. "I have read nine accessions. I have read a Museum inventory sheet that says nine objects in a room and I have been down that corridor twice on my own time and there are six steel units against that wall."

"Say nothing to anybody for about ten minutes," Elias said. "Please. Then say whatever you like and say it well."

She looked at him for a second and a half.

"Go on."

He told her all of it, in order, and he told it the way he had told Doreen Sarn, without stopping, and he did not soften any of it and he did not organise it in his own favour.

The eight photographs and where they had gone. The finisher's refusal, and the five sleeves, and the fact that the drawer was now square and the signal was off it. The statement he had written and the fact that it said he had broken a printed rule twice. The two cards with her name in the margin. The Gallery's nine objects and forty-one drawers. The lamp, and the sheet, and the two shillings, and the envelope in the van that had come back with the mill's post. The 1974 receipt and a man in a fitter's trade off Sillick Lane. A seventy-eight-year-old woman in Ferry Bank who had made the two books agree and knew she had.

And then the letter.

He gave it to her in the same envelope and he watched her read it, and he watched her get to *you told me the money was in the tin*, and he watched her put her hand over her mouth in exactly the place Doreen Sarn had put hers, and he understood that there was no longer any part of this that only he could do.

Mara Iles read it twice and then squared it on the desk and put her hand flat on it.

"Right," she said. "That's not a Museum matter."

"That's what I thought."

"That is a *post room* matter, and it goes up, and it goes up today, and I am not taking it to him." She looked at the ceiling for a second. "I am taking it to the Board Secretary and I am copying it to the Audit and Assurance provider who is doing the review of the schedule of contents that is item five on Tuesday's agenda, because that is a review of records and this is a record, and it is the only route in this building that does not go through a manager, and it cannot be stopped, and nobody will be able to say that a member of staff raised it privately, because I am going to write the covering note myself and put my name at the top of it."

"Mara—"

"You told me on Saturday night that a man with a mop said a number, and I wrote *a number* in the result field because that is what I had, and then I asked you whether there was anything in your notebook that was not on the page, and you said no, and I looked at you for one second longer than I needed to and then I went out of the room and did not ask again." She had not raised her voice once. "And there has been something in that notebook every night since, and I have decided, on the balance of it, that a person who is as frightened as you are is not lying out of malice and is lying out of the thing where they think they are the only safe place for a fact to be. So I have left it. And I would like you to understand that I have left it, and that leaving it was not the same as not knowing."

Elias did not say anything.

"And now," Mara Iles said, "I am going to do something else. I am going to write, on the top of the covering note, that there is a lease-holder's locker in the staff basement that may contain personal effects and correspondence belonging to a person named in this letter, that it has been in the building for the period covered by the review, and that the Museum cannot open it without a written instruction from the Board, because it is not a Museum record and it is a man's property, and I am going to ask the Board for that instruction in writing, and I expect that they will refuse, and I am going to keep the refusal in the file with everything else."

"That's the box," Elias said. "That's the box in the form. That's Perry Loach."

"That is the box in the form," she said, "and it is a *name* in the box in the form, and the difference between a name in a box in a form and a name in a coat pocket is that I have written down every word of the reason and initialled it and dated it, and it is in a file that four people have seen." She put the letter back in the envelope. "Elias, I do not want your facts. I have never once wanted your facts. I want you to stop being the only place they are."

---

He worked until eight.

The caged alcove had the red disc on it by half past four, which was a small red anodised tag, the kind that came in a box of fifty from an office that supplies nothing else, with a number on it and a line for a custodian's initials, and Anja Ward's initials were on it in ballpoint.

> **CUSTODY DISC 41** — object 1961/LW/44 — in temporary custody, west range, ground — *W. WARD* — 16:20

The cage itself was the Museum's own. It had been in the loading bay since before Elias worked here, for use by anyone with a trolley and a bad back, and it had four castors, two of which did not turn, and the mesh front had a padlock on it and the padlock had a tag and the tag said **QA/1187**, and the tag was the assessor's.

And on the wall above it there was a plastic wallet with a sheet in it, and the sheet was not the Museum's.

> **AWAITING TRANSFER — H. & L. Foyston, Northglass — destination: quay-side secure store, awaiting re-issue to purchaser**
> **Site clearance: Thursday 4 November.**
> **No accession permitted. ATE items quarantined in situ pending removal. All contents deemed chattels and fixtures of the works until proved otherwise. Bailiffs appointed over contents, from 4 Nov.**
> **— Quill & Frayle, 14 Lincoln's Lane, in the matter of the Sillick Mill site, p. 34.**

Northglass, where the Registry assessor worked, four minutes away, and the Museum's own director's initials on the tag of the cage it was being put into, and the Museum's own inventory number on the outside of the cage, and a firm of solicitors who represent the freehold holder of a piece of fenced ground with nothing on it, and the box is a *chattel of the works* until somebody proves otherwise.

Ate items. The three letters on his own envelope, from a caretaker in a T-shirt, a man who keeps a light on all night, and a woman called Siobhan Keogh, who had asked what an accession number was on a reply to a lost cat.

Elias Venn stood in a loading-bay corridor for about four minutes looking at a printed sheet of A4 in a plastic wallet and did not move, and then he went and got the day book, and turned back to the fifteenth of October, and put his finger on the line.

> **17:02 — VENN, E. — 1 item of post, personal, returned undeliverable, c/o Museum letting, 14 Mill Lane, Low Ward. Retained in post room, awaiting.**

And underneath it, in the same column, on the same page, four lines further down, because it had been his shift on the seventh of October and it had not been his fault then either:

> **00:24 — VENN, E. — telephone, personal, signed out 19:30 Fri, returned 08:20 Sat. No destination given.**

Two lines in the same book. One of them said *a number in a coat*. The other said *a person's post*. And the top of the page said, in capitals, in print, in the same typeface as everything else in the world:

> **THE BOOK IS THE RECORD. THE TRAY IS NOT.**

And it was Friday tomorrow.

The blue bales of Case's post had been going out of that door every Friday for eleven years, and the pulp went out on the night boat, and a man on the quayside with a clipboard and a list of bales took them without reading them, because the list said *office waste, mixed* and there were a great many of them and they were not interesting, and that is the entire mechanism, and it worked in 1993 as well as it worked in 2011, and the only reason in the world that anyone knew it had worked was that a man had been made to stand at a desk for two days and read a book he had been signing for two years.

He stood there for a long time.

And then he took the day book, and turned to a clean page at the back, and wrote the date and the time and his own name, and wrote down the truth, in full, in the flattest handwriting he had:

> 20:10, 15 Oct. E. Venn. The post of 14 Mill Lane is being destroyed on Friday. I know this because it is my job, on this desk, this week, and because I have signed this book every working day for two years without reading the head of it. There is a man's post in it, going out in the morning, and it is going out because nobody in this building has ever read the head of this book.
> I am not able to stop it. I have no authority, no post, and no way of lodging an objection that would not be answered by a man who has a liability position.
> I am therefore writing it here where it is dated, and I am going to read it to one person tonight, and if I do that and nothing happens, then this book is the record of a thing that was known in this building on the fifteenth of October and was destroyed on the sixteenth.

And he signed it, and he put the pen down, and he said it out loud to an empty loading bay, because he had four days of practice at saying things out loud to empty rooms and it was the only technique he had left:

"Fault."

And nothing happened, because nothing was listening, and that was correct, and it was also, he understood, standing there, the entire sum total of what the word had ever been for.
