# Chapter 0024 — A Lawful Removal of the Wrong Thing

The docket was executed at six o'clock on Saturday morning in a loading bay in the rain, and it was executed correctly, by three people who had all of them read it, and the thing that went out of the building was not the thing on the paper.

Elias Venn came in at ten past six. He was on a 07:00 to 15:00 and 18:00 to 21:00 rhythm, which had been a rhythm rather than a job for a fortnight, and he came up the service lane in the rain at six on a Saturday morning because a docket is not a thing you find out about on a Monday.

Nunn was there at five. He had a van outside with the tailgate up and a cage on a sack truck and a clipboard and a good coat under a high-visibility one, and there was a second man with him called Petrie who had a torch and a strip of load-bearing tape and the air of a man who had done four hundred of these.

The docket was one page on a printed form, dated the Saturday, timed 06:00, and there were three signature blocks on it and all three of them were to be signed in the presence of the person named in the destination.

Elias read it standing at the desk while Nunn filled in the top block.

> **GOODS OUT — REMOVAL FROM SITE**
> **ITEM:** one (1) crate, sealed, Schedule 12 carried material, **1961/LW/44** (Low Ward series).
> **FROM:** cage 2, west range service corridor, ground floor. Red custody disc 41. Padlock tag QA/1187.
> **TO:** Quay 3, Broomhead Reach, Northglass — quay-side secure store, bay as allocated, in bond. Awaiting re-issue to purchaser.
> **PLACED BY TRANSFER:** H. & L. Foyston, Northglass. **Authorising officer:** D. Ordway (D.O.).
> **COUNTERSIGNATURE REQUIRED:** a person who is not an officer or employee of this Museum (Standing Instruction 14, clause 1).
> **PRESENT AT REMOVAL:** name, grade, time, signature.

The third block was empty and it was going to stay empty, and everybody in that bay had known that since Tuesday, and Nunn had come at five in the morning anyway because a transfer clerk who does not turn up is a transfer clerk who has handed in a job he did not do, and the sheet on his own copy says *attended, 05:50, no collection*, and that is a line in a yard office and it is the only protection there is.

It had been on the docket since Tuesday the twentieth and the date on it had been the sixteenth and it had never been signed, and on Thursday the twenty-second it had been re-issued with a Saturday on it, and the re-issue had a new authorising officer's initials on it, and the initials were the same initials.

That is what the whole of Movement Three is, and it took a man to write it down and nobody to believe it.

---

At ten to six, the padlock came off the cage.

Nunn had a key for it and the key was a Registry key and Nunn had it because a transfer clerk is allowed to have the keys to the things he is removing, and Elias Venn had asked the Registry in the first week of October how a thing in a cage gets to a van and had been given an answer about chain of custody in about four minutes, and had understood, standing at a desk on a Wednesday morning with a head full of doors and clocks, that the answer had been *we do not need a man to open a cage, we need a man to be able to say he did not.*

The cage door came open on its hinge and inside it was a wooden box, and the box was about four feet long and eighteen inches deep and it had a Museum accession label on the end of it.

Elias Venn read the label from four feet away.

> **1961/LW/44**
> *Sillick Mill: administrative papers, disposal.*
> *Level B, bay 9. (Relocated — see note.)*

And underneath, in ballpoint, in a hand that was not a label hand:

> *PF 118*

And in the cage, next to the box, there was a vitrine.

A museum case, about three feet by one foot, oak, with a plate-glass top, and it had been standing in the loading bay since the twelfth of October, and it had a card wired inside the lid, and the card said **1961/LW/51** and the words *fitter's lamp, electric, navy enamel*, and the lamp was in it, and the lid was locked and had a lead seal on it with a number on the seal.

Elias Venn stood in a loading bay at ten to six in the morning and looked at two objects six feet apart, and worked out what was going to happen, and had about nine seconds in which to say one sentence, and said it.

"Mr Nunn. That's the wrong one."

Nunn looked up. He had a clipboard and a strap wrench and a good coat and he was extremely good at his job.

"Say again?"

"The docket says 1961/LW/44." Elias pointed at the label on the end of the wooden box. "That's 1961/LW/44. That's a wooden box and that's the crate, and the crate is your crate, and the crate is on the truck. That's a case." He pointed at the vitrine. "That's 1961/LW/51, and it's got a Registry seal on it, and it's a Schedule 12 item on a plinth, and it's not on your docket at all."

Nunn read the docket again. He read it all the way through, the way a man reads a document he has read before and has just been told is wrong.

"No," he said.

"That's what I—"

"No, mate, it's not the wrong one, because there's nothing to be wrong. The docket says *one crate, sealed*. That" — he put his thumb on the wooden box — "is a crate. That is exactly what a crate is, and it is on my sheet, and it is going on my truck. The other one is a case, and a case is not a crate, and a case is not on my sheet, and a case is not something I am taking, because I have not been asked to take it and if it is on my truck at ten past six it is on my truck because somebody put it on it."

"That is a lamp and it is a Schedule 12 carried object and it is classified and it is sealed and it has a Registry tag on it, and if it goes in that van it is an unauthorised removal of a—"

"It's a case," said Nunn. "It's got a lead seal on it, so I couldn't open it if I wanted to and I don't want to, and the seal's got a number and the number's in the schedule of collections and there's a man in Northglass who can tell you whether it's supposed to be on my truck or not." He wrote something on the sheet and underlined it. "But it is not on my docket, so it is not on my truck, and if I have to put one of them off it is going to be the one that is on the sheet. And the one that is on the sheet is the box, because the box is what the sheet is for."

He looked at the vitrine for a second.

"And that's your lamp, is it," he said. "Off the Unnamed Hands. It's been sat in that bay since before you lot came in, hasn't it, because it was the case that was in the bay in the spring."

"Yes."

"Right." Nunn took his hat off and scratched his head, and Elias Venn watched a professional in the middle of a job do arithmetic about his own liability, which is a thing he had never seen anybody do and had never thought to expect. "Then that's straightforwardly the wrong one and it's straightforwardly not mine and I'm going to do the only thing I've got, which is put the lamp on the sheet as *offloaded, in error, sealed, no opening* and ring the office and let them tell me where it goes back to. And if they want it back today they can collect it, and if they don't, it goes back with the next van, and we are not back on that site until the Monday week, so that is a week of it sat on a shelf in a bonded cage with a seal on it and nobody noticing, and in the meantime it's on my manifest as something that came off a plinth and went back on a lorry by accident, and that's a line in a yard book and nobody has to lose anything."

"That's fine."

"That's not fine, mate, that's the *only* thing that's available." He was already writing. "I can't put a Schedule 12 object on a lorry on my own authority and I couldn't have done it with your Director in the room. What I can do is record that a sealed item came off a plinth in error and went back to the plinth from which it came, and a man in Northglass will say *fine*, and if he says anything else then he can come and say it to the seal."

They put the wooden box on the truck.

It took four minutes, and it was a four-foot wooden box and it went on a sack truck and up the tailgate ramp on two wedges, and it did not go into a bonded cage and it was not caged in the van, it was simply a crate on a lorry under a strap with a docket against it, and there was nothing about it that was dramatic and nothing about it that was illegal, and the eleven items of the 1974 receipt — a weavers' time book, a dye-house time book, a gate book with leaves one to ninety-six present, posted wage sheets, an envelope of loose papers sealed and not examined, a tea chest, a monthly wages book, something withdrawn and soiled, a fitter's lamp that was in Northglass and not here, a bundle of wage sheets tied with tape and marked *pay office*, and the works-office clock, stopped — went out of a building on Civic Hill on a Saturday morning in October on a docket that three people could sign and nobody could.

And the lamp went in the van in a sealed case with a line on a transfer clerk's manifest saying it had been offloaded in error.

Elias Venn stood in a loading bay and did not open anything, and had not opened anything for thirteen days, and had told a room with a card cutter in it on Sunday that he was not going to.

The two that went and the one that stayed were on a manifest inside an envelope and the manifest went to the yard in Northglass and a man telephoned the office at seven in the morning and the office asked for the lamp back and the office said it would be collected with the next van, and the office knew as well as anybody that the van was not going back on that site until the Monday week, so that a sealed object with a Registry tag on it was going to sit on a shelf in a bonded cage for a week with nobody noticing, and the office said the word *unfortunately* in a way that meant the opposite.

Then somebody in that office did a thing that was not anybody's fault and could not be anybody's fault and would not have occurred to anybody who had not been sitting in a loading bay for thirteen days.

They put the lamp on a *return*.

Not in a van. On a return manifest, addressed back to the Museum, at the bottom of a list, in a line of its own, with a reference, and a *state on despatch*, and the state they entered was the state the seal said: **sealed, not opened, condition as received**. And the destination they entered was not the Gallery of Unnamed Hands. It was **Quay 3, Broomhead Reach — bay as allocated, in bond**, because that was the destination that had been typed into the system on the docket they were working from, and the docket was for a box, and a man in an office working from a docket does not change a destination halfway down a list at seven in the morning on a Saturday.

Elias Venn found that out at ten past ten on Saturday morning from a man on a telephone in Northglass, and he corrected it in writing at twenty past ten, on Museum notepaper, initialled, dated, timed, and put it through the registered internal post so that it had a movement number on it, and the movement number was the only piece of apparatus in the entire three weeks that he had ever been able to get to do anything at all.

> **MEMORANDUM — MOVEMENT NOTE.**
> The item described at transfer manifest line 4 as *1961/LW/51, fitter's lamp, sealed, offloaded in error* is not consigned to Quay 3. Its correct destination is **Gallery of Unnamed Hands, plinth 9, level 1, this building**, and it should be returned to this building on the first available collection, and the return should be recorded against the original plinth card.
> The lamp is not the item on the docket. The item on the docket is in the vehicle. Please advise the bonded store that line 4 is not to be released to the quayside and is to be held at the store for collection by this Museum.
> *E. Venn, security, grade 1. 24 Oct, 10:20. This note is a request and not an instruction and I have no authority to issue an instruction.*

And the reply came back at eleven forty from a man at the bonded store, on a telephone, in a yard office, and Elias Venn wrote down all four lines of it, because it was the last time anybody in the entire business said anything on the telephone that was written down exactly as it was said.

> **Broomhead Reach, secure store. 11:40, 24 Oct.**
> 1. Line 4 received into bond 09:15, sealed, seal intact.
> 2. Line 1 (the crate) is on the quay at **BAY 4** and will break bulk on the first working day of next week.
> 3. Line 4 is a paper item in bond, small, and it will go on the same shelf as line 1 unless somebody tells us different.
> 4. **Put away.**

And that was the whole of it. Four lines, a good clerk, an ordinary morning, a bay number, and four words at the end, and *put away* does not mean a store room. *Put away* means it stops being a movement and becomes stock. There is a bonded area in that store and a bonded cage in it, and a bonded cage is a cage for things that are not to be handled, and a cage is a *cage*, and a cage is the thing in the west range corridor that was moved into on the nineteenth of October, and nobody in Northglass knew that and nobody in Low Ward knew it and the word had been used four times in four weeks by four different people about four different objects and every single one of them had been correct.

"They're lifting the pump housing off the slip."

"Which day is the shed going?"

"They've got a boy on it." She was not shouting. She never shouted. "There's a lad in a hi-vis and he's about fourteen and he's standing on the staging boards with a man doing the signals and the crane is slewing round, and the man doing the signals has got his back to the lad, and I've come down the lane on the bus and there's a woman with a clipboard and no hard hat on, and I have been on this slip nine times in thirty-one years and I am telling you the water is at the fourth mark this morning, which is four inches and it was four inches at ten o'clock and it is a falling tide."

"Hattie—"

"I'm not telephoning you to be dramatic, love. I'm telephoning you because there is nobody else in this city I can ring." A pause. "Ring the foreman. The hire firm's number's on the side of the crane. You read it off the side of a crane. You did that yesterday and it worked."

He telephoned the foreman from a call box at the top of Sillick Lane at ten to twelve, and the foreman listened to the whole of it, and did not laugh once, and said *my mate's seen him*, and put the phone down on the staging, and Elias Venn stood in a call box in the rain and listened to a man on a site be extremely careful with a boy and never once mention that a stranger had telephoned.

The pump housing came off the slip at twenty past one without a sling swinging. It went on a low-loader on four timbers and it went up the ramp behind the van and it was the last big thing that came out of that canal, and the boy on the staging boards was sixteen inches further up the ramp than he had been an hour before, and somebody's grandad had taught somebody.

Then the crane slewed to the yard to pick the second lift, which was a stopwatch on a string in a tea chest, and the slinger put the hook down and got the empty hook up, and the driver slewed back to the ramp for the empty, which is what a crane does, and a man walking a gantry, coming back to his own vehicle, came round the stack.

The boy backed up four feet to get out of the arc of the hook.

There was a stack of scaffold tubes and battens at the head of the slip where the gang was working, and the tubes were in a bin and the battens were on top of the bin in a bundle, and the topmost batten was a three-metre length of 38 x 38 timber with a sawn end that was about eight inches proud of the stack and had been sawn off at an angle in 1994 and never capped.

The boy put his left wrist on it at about the level of his hip, on his way backwards, at a speed of about one mile an hour, and the batten went, and his forearm went with it.

It was not a crane accident. The crane was not involved. The boy was four feet from a reversing machine and he had been moved twelve feet and he was still four feet from it, and a man was forty feet away with his back turned, and a woman with a clipboard was on the hardstanding with no hat on, and the man who had read the *Chronicle* photograph in a good coat had bought half a day of a crane and an *attendant* figure of two, and the hire firm had a woman in an office who says what the sheet says out loud on arrival, and the sheet said two, and there were two in the cab, and there were twenty on the site, and one of them was a boy of fourteen on a week's work experience from a school in Northgate who was standing in a designated exclusion zone for the whole of the morning and had put his hand on a piece of timber at the moment he turned round to look at a crane.

His wrist came off the batten and came back on and stayed there.

Hattie Bramm was four feet away and did not see the crane and did see the boy.

"I told him," she said, to nobody, on a pavement, at twenty-five past one on Saturday afternoon. "I was four foot away. I said *move, love, move* at the top of my voice, I said it twice, and he did not hear me the first time because of the crane and he did not hear me the second time because he was on the radio, and there was a woman with a clipboard four foot from the boy who did not move, and I could not make him move."

"Who was nearest?"

"Nobody. That's the thing." She was not crying and she was not angry and she was doing the thing she did, which was to give a room the facts in the order they happened. "There was a man with a slinger on, eleven foot away, on the far side of the bin. There was the fitter doing the impeller, who was under the pump housing, where he was supposed to be, and who could not have come out from under it in under a minute. And there was me, on a pavement, without a hard hat, on the wrong side of a fence, being a woman who was right about four inches of water in October, and he was a boy and he was not going to move because a stranger with no hat was telling him to."

The boy went to hospital in an ambulance and had a **fracture of the radial styloid** and came out of it in a cast from the wrist to below the elbow and was back at the college on the Monday, because he was on work experience and there was a college tutor who came and collected him and who had a form, and the form was good, and the form said *no first aid treatment was administered on site beyond initial dressing by the first aider, who is a named person on the site induction record*, and the named person was the woman with the clipboard, and she had not been near the boy because the ambulance had come in nine minutes and the fitter had got there first.

Nobody was to blame. That is the sentence. Not the tone of it — the sentence, flat, with no adjective in it.

Nobody was to blame, and there was a man in a good coat who had written a figure in a box, and a woman from a hire firm who had said it out loud on arrival, and a foreman who had moved a boy twelve feet in the right direction on the strength of a telephone call from a call box, and a man of fifty-four on a pavement who had been on that slip nine times in thirty-one years and had got a lot of things right, and a boy of fourteen from Northgate who had been in a designated exclusion zone for five hours and who put his wrist on a piece of timber because he turned round.

And a wrist is a thing a person needs. Not this week. Not this year. A wrist is the thing a fitter's mate does his work with, and a fitter's mate's boy who has broken a wrist at fourteen on a site he was not paid to be on is a thing that is in a file at the college for thirty years, and every employer in the city will ask about it, and he will have to answer it, and the answer will be *I was standing in an exclusion zone*, which is true, and which is the sentence a person says for the rest of their life.

The tutor said one thing to Elias Venn on the telephone on the Monday, when he rang to ask, which was that the boy's grandfather had been a fitter at the Sillick, and that the boy had asked for that site by name in September, and that the boy had asked to go back out with the crew on the Monday, and that he was not going to, and could the Museum please not ring his mother.

It was not a blanking. It was four seconds in which he could not produce a name, and the cost had been named aloud in a room with eleven people in it at ten to ten the previous evening, on a card, with a number on the card, and the number had been on the card in his own hand, and he had chosen it, and the choosing was the part he would not be able to look at afterwards.

Four seconds. Nothing behind his eyes, nothing in front of them, a room with a loading bay in it and a pay office not in it, and a place where a name was that had been a place where a name was four seconds ago.

Then it came back, and it came back wrong.

For about eight minutes afterwards Elias Venn could not have told you, if you had asked him, the surname of the only person in Morrowgate who had ever said out loud in a room that there was no public room in this city and that somebody was going to have to build one.

He wrote it down in the notebook at 01:05, in the flat block hand, with the time against it, because a fact with no time on it is a fact with no end on it. It was four seconds. It was the correct name. He had it back at 00:57 and he put it on the page eight minutes later, and the four seconds had been the worst four seconds of his working life and the other four hundred and eighty had been four hundred and eighty ordinary seconds in which a man of thirty-two did not know the surname of a woman of fifty-four, which is what seven hundred seconds of a night is for.

Ivo Pell said the word at 01:01 from the corridor, over nothing, in the voice you use for a kettle, and said it once, and it stopped.

Nobody touched the case. The cover went on. The room was left as it was.

---

At twenty past one on Sunday morning Elias Venn sat on a sack of frames in a loading bay and wrote it all down, standing up, in the block hand, and the entry took him a long time and it is the only notebook page in the whole of that book that is crossed out in four places, and none of the crossings-out are the sentence about the four seconds.

> *Sun 25 Oct, 01:20. Reading, case on the table, loading bay office, room of eleven. Q: whose hands; did the sheet make them do it or did they make it do the sheet. Nine minutes promised, nine given. Word said by I.P. at 01:01.*
>
> *Not the pay clerk. Not the man. A third person at a counter with a hand flat on it and the back of a head on. A gate-book box on a shelf with a pay-office stamp on the lid. A figure in a corner of a leaf: twenty-four. It is a corner of a leaf. I do not know what it counts and I am not going to be the man who decides, and if somebody later wants me to say what twenty-four means I will say I do not know and I will say it in writing.*
>
> *A stopped clock, six minutes to six. A folded blanket over a chair. The blanket is how you leave a room you are going to lock.*
>
> *COST PAID: four seconds. I have it back. I want it recorded that I chose that price and that I chose it because it was a small price and because I did not know it was the right one, and that I would choose it again, and that a man who chooses a price before he knows what it costs has not chosen a price.*
>
> *It did not ask me for anything. I want that in as well. Nothing in eight minutes asked me for a name or a number or a promise, and the room supplied the whole of it, and the room was eleven people and one of them had a mop.*
>
> *And it showed me somebody I have not met. ~~Third person, unknown, at a counter, no face.~~ There is a person at that counter who is not the clerk and not the man and is not me, and on Saturday night at twenty to one this morning she was four feet away with a mop, and I have not asked her anything, and I am not going to, and the reading showed me her and did not give me her name, and the reason it did not give me her name is that the name was the price.*

And then he put the notebook away and went and did the dead letters, because it was Sunday and there were two hundred and fifty-one of them and a clerk's is a slow job, and there are two hundred and fifty-one of them, and nobody in this building can be got at over a man counting post that came back.
