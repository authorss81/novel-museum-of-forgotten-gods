# Chapter 0002 — The Bell in the Hall

Dael Ordway was in the security room with the building log open when Elias came on, which meant he had been there since eight, which meant he had done the walk and found it wanting.

"Two-fifty-two on Tuesday night," Dael said, without looking up. "Tuesday. In the Hall."

"That's the time."

"You've got a repeat. Fifteen minutes."

Elias put his bag down. On the wall behind Dael the notice board still carried the River Works sheet from Monday, pinned at its angle, with *bell 3, still ringing* in biro underneath it. Elias had thought about taking that down eleven times since Tuesday and had not done it, and now he was glad of every one of the eleven.

"There's no fault."

"I didn't say there was a fault." Dael turned the book round and pushed it across the desk, and his finger came down on the entry without touching the ink. "You wrote it as a building noise. Good. That's exactly right. That's what I would have written." He let that sit for a second, which was a thing Dael did instead of raising his voice. "So let's be in the same place about it. It's the west range. It's the oldest range on the site and the ductwork on that side is original in places, and stone moves when the heating comes on at ten and again at six. The fans up on conservation are screaming their arses off and they've been doing it since the summer. If a sound happens again tonight, note it and move on. Note it, and move on."

"Who's the note for?"

"The book." Dael turned it back. "That's what the book is for."

He was not a hard man. Elias had worked under three of Dael's predecessors and they were all hard men, and it had taken two years to work out that Dael was something else: a man with a building on his name and a liability figure he could not carry by himself. In the spring the boiler had gone out on a Sunday and thirty-one thousand people had been through the galleries on a wet Tuesday for the spring schools half-term, and Dael had spent a fortnight answering questions about it. He was not covering anything. He was managing, and what he managed was the gap between a thing that could be proved and a thing that could only be survived.

"If I open a maintenance ticket on the west range," he said, "what happens to me?"

"You get an engineer. You get a report. You get a paragraph in the quarterly that somebody on the Board reads out loud in a meeting where they are deciding whether the galleries open on Sundays in the winter." He took his hat off and put it under his arm. "Elias, this building is insured at a rate I have to defend every year. An undocumented danger is not a danger I can hold. It's a danger I can be dismissed for."

"That's not what I said."

"Then what did you say?" Dael waited, and he was perfectly prepared to wait, and Elias found that he had nothing in his mouth worth having.

"Nothing. Understood."

"There's a thing you can do that would be useful." Dael put his hat back on. "Take the service lane cameras tonight. Both of them, the full sweep, and write the times down properly. I want the times written down properly. That is a document I can hand anybody." He was already at the door. "And leave that sluice notice where it is, would you. If there's a noise in this building in the next ten days, I would like there to be a piece of paper in this building that says there is a bell in this city making a wrong note, because the Board has been on about the ward-bell budget since March and I have been waiting nine months for somebody to make it a priority instead of a curiosity."

He went. The door shut with the particular care of a man who does not slam doors in a building he is insured for.

---

Elias did the service lane first, because it was the duller half of the instruction and he wanted it over with. Twenty-two minutes a side, the bins, the grille over the coal chute, the two shutters on the loading bay, the swell of the road, the puddle that formed by the kerb at the low corner and never quite froze. The cameras were good, the footage was half-brightness, and there was nothing on it, and he wrote the times down properly because Dael had asked and because a proper time is a thing you cannot argue with.

Then he went up and started his own record, which was not a record. It was a pocket notebook, cheap, wire-bound, bought from a stall on Lantern Row for the price of a paperweight, and it lived in the inside pocket of his coat and had not been on a form in the two years he had owned it.

On the desk under the keyboard, weighted with a lump of lead, was the Saturday booking sheet for the Hall of Former Faiths. Eleven o'clock. Forty-one children, two teachers, and an acknowledgement slip with a printed name on it and a telephone number, because a Hill office had got into the habit of sending someone to be photographed in the room. It had been on that desk since the previous Thursday. It was the hardest commitment in the building for a night guard, because forty-one children cannot be quietly moved to another floor at eleven in the morning, and because the acknowledgement slip meant that a person with a clipboard was expected to stand in that hall and say something.

He wrote:

> Tue 02:52 — FoF 34. Note out of the metal, no strike. Seal good, sensor good, plinth good, door good. No entry to room.
> Wed — started watching.

Then he thought about how that looked, and crossed out *started watching*, and wrote *WED 20:00 station set on FoF 34, seal and plinth re-checked, hall empty, amb. 15°*, which is what you write when you are starting a log. It is not the same as a log, because a log has an institution behind it and a signature, and a notebook has only a man in it, and Elias understood perfectly well that a man in a notebook is a man who has decided to be the last place a fact lives. He wrote the time anyway.

He was not being clever. He was being a guard. In his experience, the things that got people hurt were the things nobody had written down anywhere, and the things that got *him* in trouble were the things he had written down somewhere a supervisor could find. So he did the only version of the job available to him, which was to make two copies and keep one of them under his own lock and better than his own handwriting.

---

He took the Hall at 20:40 and let himself in with the key that would not let him in with a card, which is a security measure, in that it stops people who have lost a card from walking in.

The hall at night with people in it is a different building from the hall at night empty. With school groups it is warm and loud and the light is up in the coving. Empty, it is fifteen degrees and enormous, and the cases make a long low line down the middle, and if you stand still long enough you stop hearing the building.

He stood at the head of the row with the monitor up on the little wheeled stand, and he watched plinth thirty-four.

He was not being dramatic about it. He had done this before with faulty door closers and a jammed turnstile, and you stand and watch the thing and the thing does nothing, and the nothing is data. The monitor gave him the case in a flat grey rectangle. Time ran. At 20:53 the case told him nothing. At 20:54 it told him nothing.

At 20:55 the note came out of the hall.

It came out of the hall the way a sound comes out of a room, which is to say everywhere at once, off the coving and the terrazzo and the glass, and it was the same note. The same one. Not a note in a key, not a pitch, something you would have called a note in a corridor: a single sound with no obvious source, arriving at the same volume everywhere in the room, and out of it, at the end, a faint hum in his own jaw that was not quite tinnitus and was not quite a sound in the room either.

*The object is answering a question. Touch, speak, or leave.*

It came from nowhere in particular. It was not a voice, and it was not a thought he had, and it belonged to no wall, no sign, and no speaker in a room that had nothing switched on but the cove lighting. It was there for about a second and a half and then it was not, and Elias Venn took it for a phrase he had read off an old sign somewhere in this building, or a phrase somebody had said to him once, or the beginning of a tinnitus, and he put it down the way you put down a phrase you half heard on a tram, and he did not write it in either book, and that was the first thing he took from that building and kept.

He did not think about it. It was not a thought he had. It was a sentence arriving from nowhere, and it did not belong to the room he was standing in, and it was gone.

He looked at the case.

The case was a grey rectangle. The seal was unbroken. The bell was dark. The sensor at the base was green, and the green did not blink, and no sensor in the world blinks, and he watched it not blink for a full ten seconds with the particular attention of a man who has started to see things.

And then, in the middle of watching, he noticed the other thing, which he wrote down afterwards and which he did not understand for a long time:

The hall got quieter.

Not silent. There was the building, there was the fan a floor up, there was a tram somewhere far off doing a shunt. But the objects down the row — the threshold stone, the tally board, the dead shrine lamp with its wick grey as a mouse tail — had a sort of background to them, a hum of their own, a thing you could only notice by stopping, the way a room with a fridge in it hums and does not stop. That went out at 20:55 and came back at 20:56, all at once, the whole length of the row, like a curtain dropping.

He stood in the middle of an empty hall and looked at thirty-four for another nine minutes, and nothing came again, and at 21:04 he shut the monitor down, wrote the times in both places, and went up to the first floor to find out whether the thing was even in the collection.

---

The accession record for an object like that would be on the first floor, in the registrar's office, and the registrar's office at nineteen hundred on a Wednesday was a lit doorway with a woman in it who had not come down to say good evening.

"You'll be the security."

"Mara Iles?" He had seen the name on a hundred door signs. "Elias Venn. Night guard. I'm trying to find the accession file for plinth thirty-four in the Hall."

"Go on."

"A small bronze bell. Cast 1902. Label says recovered from a textile works in Low Ward, acquired 1902—"

"Cast 1902 and *acquired* 1902."

"Acq. 4471 over B."

Mara Iles put both hands flat on her desk for a moment, the way people do when they are being very fair. She was younger than he had expected and looked as though she had been awake since six.

"There is no accession file 4471 over B," she said. "There is no accession file 4471 at all. There is no file for 4471 over anything."

"That can't be right. It's in a case, it's got a sensor, it's insured. Insurance lists a value for it, that value has to come from—"

"From a schedule of collections, which is not the same as an acquisition file." She said it without any weight at all, which was worse than if she had been sharp. "There are objects in this building that came in with a donor's parcel and never got a file, and objects that came in from a purchase and got a file that has never been located, and one category that we do not discuss upstairs, which is objects that were given to us by a member of staff with an instruction not to raise a file. I am not going to guess which category thirty-four is in. I have guessed before and I was wrong once and it took two people a year to unsay."

Elias stood in the doorway with his hands loose at his sides and felt the specific bad feeling of a man who has come with a right answer and been given a true one instead.

"Right," he said. "Who would know?"

"The accession index is on the terminal downstairs. If it isn't in the index, then the honest answer is that it isn't in the index, and you should write down the honest answer, which is the card has a number and the index has no record of the number." She paused. "Why are you asking about this one?"

"I've heard it."

"Heard it what?"

"Sounding. Twice." He heard how that sounded, in a corridor, to a woman whose job was paper. "It's probably the ductwork. Dael thinks it's the ductwork."

"Dael thinks the west range is original in places because the contractor told him so in 2011 and he has written it in four quarterly reports." She was already turning back to her screen. "It is not a criticism. It is just the sort of sentence that stops being true by being repeated. Get your other end of it fixed and then tell me what it does."

He said goodnight and she said goodnight without looking up, and he went down the corridor past the lightbox in the conservation stairwell, which said

> CONSERVATION FLOOR — PAPER, METALS, TEXTILES, GLASS
> NO UNAUTHORISED ENTRY DURING TREATMENT HOURS

and felt, on the whole, extremely ordinary, which is what a person is supposed to feel when the job is going the way it should.

At his desk in the security room, before he keyed off the terminal, he typed one more line into the building log, because it was the kind of thing a man put in a log.

> 20:55 — FoF 34. Repeat of Tue 02:52, same single note, no visible movement, seal intact, hall empty and unentered throughout. Ambient 15°C. Possible west-range ductwork expansion. Noted, not investigated, per instruction.

He read *not investigated, per instruction* and understood that he had just written a sentence with his own name on it that would be very hard to unpick later, and that he had written it because Dael had told him to, and that he was going to be glad of it for about eleven hours.

In his notebook, in the same pen, he wrote the same five facts and then, under them, on the next line, smaller:

> It went quiet everywhere else when it did it.

And he put the notebook away, and he did the second sweep of the service lane, and the bell did not sound again that night.
