# Chapter 0006 — Sillick Lane

He had Friday daytime off, which meant he had Friday daytime in which nobody would phone him, and he used it going the wrong way across the city.

The tram took forty minutes down to the Low Ward loop and the stop was at the top of Sillick Lane, and Elias got off and stood for a moment on the pavement in the rain looking at the wrong side of the street.

He had got the street out of the back of the binder, the posting sheets, not the personnel files. Grade-two porters, Museum payroll, 2001 through 2012: correspondence address, every single one, the same twelve words, *c/o Museum letting, Sillick Lane, Low Ward*. The Museum had rented the mill's porters' row for a decade and put its own address in front of it, and the row had gone to tenancies after that, and the tenancies had gone three times over.

He had not expected it to help. He had expected a row of doors. Instead there was a street with the tram on one side and a low wall on the other, and a laundrette, and a shuttered shop, and about forty flats above them with washing out, and a queue of people at the library door.

And at the bottom of the street, where the row gave out and the land went down toward the river bend above South Sluice, the mill was not a mill. It was eleven hundred metres of corrugated hoarding in a paler green than anything else in Low Ward, with a gate at each end and a gap in the middle where a man had cut through the fence once and had clearly thought better of it. Two-thirds of the way along, at about head height, somebody with a spray can had put the ownership of the place where anybody could see it, in the flat official capitals of a company that had no reason to want to be thought about: **HALLOWGATE HOLDINGS**.

Elias stood in the rain and read the name twice, and understood that the paper in this building and the land at the bottom of this street were in the same pair of hands, and that the pair of hands had a gate at each end and a fence in between and thirty-one years of not letting anybody in.

---

The Sillick Lane branch library opened at nine on a Friday and by nine twenty the back room was full. Not a demonstration. A queue, with tickets, and a folding table with a jug of water on it, and a woman at the table writing down names and addresses in a hard hand while a younger woman with a laptop read the letters out loud to whoever had come to the front.

Elias had never seen an unpaid-bills queue in his life. He had read about hardship in a laundrette for eighteen years, sitting on a bench while a woman took a boy's chest in and out of a machine.

He stood at the back for twenty minutes and watched the room work. That was the room, the queue, the hard hand, the letters read out loud, the jug of water. There were eleven people waiting. Two had come with a warrant threat they were going to be late on. One had a bag of paperwork she had brought in a wheelie bag and had been told to take it away. Nobody was shouting. The woman with the laptop had the flat affect of somebody who had read a hundred of these and still had not got used to them.

At 09:40 the hard hand looked up and said, "Are you here for something or are you looking for a toilet."

"I'm looking for somebody who knows about Sillick Mill."

"Yes, everybody does. Sit down. I'm Hattie."

---

She was fifty-four and she was not going to lose any part of the morning to him. That was established in the first thirty seconds, in the way she moved a chair out from the wall for him and then went back to the list without waiting to see whether he used it.

"You work nights."

"How did you—"

"Nobody who comes in here at ten in the morning works days." She wrote something, said "That's the fourth time this week" to the woman at the front, and looked back at him. "Right. You want the mill. Everybody wants the mill. What do you want with the mill, and don't tell me you're writing something, everybody's writing something, the ones who are writing something don't come to me."

"I'm looking for a man called Wendel Case."

Hattie Bramm's hand did not stop, but it slowed, which was more information than she gave anybody else all morning.

"Wendel Case," she said. "No."

"He was a porter at the Museum. In Sillick Lane, by the address on the old posting sheets—"

"That was all one address." She put her pen down, which was a bigger event. "Every night man this building ever hired off the mill was on that row, and I've a list of the row and it's no good to me, because the row's been sold on three times and the names on the door now are not the names on the row. Half of them are private lets and half of them are the housing people and there's about a dozen I've never seen in my life and I know them the way you know the weather."

"You know him."

"I don't know *him*. I know a Wendel." She pulled a folder towards her out of the pile and did not open it. "Wendel Case, he was at number fourteen, he was a fitter's mate at the dye house, and he did this every autumn — the stopcock on the standpipe, the one at the bottom of the row, he used to do it for a pint and never take it, and there were six of us on that row and none of us thought that was remarkable, he just did it. And the standpipe's been dead three winters. Somebody put a different one in and charged us for it."

Elias put his hands flat on his knees so that they would stay still.

"So he's alive."

"I've not seen him in three years, which is three years, which is nothing in this city." She took her pen up again. "Number fourteen's let. I know because there's a woman in it with a boy with a bad chest who's on my list twice this month." She said this without any connection between the two facts, and then she looked at him for the first time properly. "You said *the Museum*."

Elias had been careful about that. He had got through four minutes of it without giving her a single thing he could be reported on.

"He was a night porter," Elias said carefully, "at a building on the hill."

"At the Museum." Hattie Bramm wrote a short line on the list and underlined something. "Morrowgate. Quiet Saints." She said it the way you say a word you have written on the top of nine years of letters. "And there you are, in my room, with no badge, asking me about a man off my street, in my queue, at ten in the morning, and you've told me your first name and nothing else and I've told you the mill."

She turned the folder round and put it on the table between them, and Elias looked at it, and the first page was a list of names in a hard hand, ruled into columns, and beside each name a figure.

"Sixty-one," said Hattie Bramm.

She took a breath and sat back. "Sixty-one. That's what's on the list. Sixty-one people shorted on the final pay run out of that mill. Not sixty-one people who worked there — God, no, there were hundreds. Sixty-one people who were underpaid on the last run and never got the rest."

"Was there a count?" Elias said. "Of how many actually worked there? I've seen a mill headcount. There's usually—"

"You weren't saying a number, love." She turned the folder again so it faced her. "Sixty-one, and it's a *pay* number, not a head count, and I've had it for nine years, and it's a pay number because the mill never had anything else. No count of us. No list of who was in which room. Nothing. Just what we were each paid and what we were each owed, on the last day, by a foreman with a book, and then a cheque list on the Friday."

"It was the closing day. The last day. That's when—"

"That's when they paid us, yes." She was not looking at him. "That's when they did the final run. And I was the gate clerk on the closing shift and I wrote every figure that was called, because that's the job, the gate clerk writes, and a foreman calls the numbers out in the morning in the rain and you write them down. Thirty-one years ago. And I've had this piece of paper in a drawer ever since and there is not one document on this earth that agrees with it."

Elias had come here to ask a night porter's name, and he had walked into a woman's kitchen table with a folder, and the folder was older than his father's disappearance and had nothing whatever to do with it, and he found that he could not get a question out.

"Did they destroy it?" he said. "The list?"

"No."

"Then where—"

"It wasn't destroyed, that's what I said." Hattie Bramm tapped the folder. "That's not mine. Mine is names and addresses and what the last pay run took off each of them. What the mill had — the shortfall sheet, the actual sheet, the paper the foreman copied from — that went somewhere. And I know where, because I chased it for nine years."

"Where."

"They sold it. That's the story, and the story is true, and it's true the way these stories are true, which is to say it's true of everything except the part that matters." She looked up. "The liquidator took the mill papers in the year after. Office papers, accounts, personnel, the lot. And nine years of me writing to a firm about the mill — a firm that isn't there any more, a firm that has been at four addresses — and the fourth address wrote back. In year three. Told me the papers had been placed."

"Placed where."

She turned her chair round and pointed out of the window with her chin, north, up the hill, over four miles of city and a river.

"Placed," she said, "in a museum that doesn't need them."

---

The rain came harder for a while. Elias sat in a plastic chair in a back room of a municipal library and worked out that a building he walked four nights a week had a box of paper in it, on a shelf, in a catalogue, that a woman four miles away had been writing letters about for nine years, and that nobody had ever told the two facts about each other.

"That museum," he said. "The Quiet Saints."

"It's the one on the hill with the closed galleries and the school groups." Hattie turned back to her list. "They wrote to me. Year three. On their letterhead. Somebody in an office with a job title. Said the Sillick Mill administrative papers had been received as a donation from the liquidator, retained for completeness of the Low Ward series, and that as we had no record of the mill's industrial relations we were unable to assist."

She put the last words in a small careful voice, the voice people keep for the sentences they have said out loud a great many times.

"And you wrote back."

"I wrote back for six years." She capped her pen. "And you'll understand that nobody at that museum is going to be the villain about it, because they've no idea, and if you go in there and tell them, you'll get a face like yours is getting now."

---

"I am on my way to a building on a hill—"

"You are sitting in a back room in Low Ward with no badge and I have given you the name of a woman in a photograph on my own front room wall and a figure that has taken me nine years, and I have not asked you one single thing about who you are." Hattie Bramm folded her hands. "I don't mind being lied to badly. I can work with a person who lies badly. What I can't work with is a person who wastes the morning of a queue. So which is it, are you writing something or are you asking me?"

Elias had had a whole answer ready at the bottom of the tram, and it had gone, and what was left in the room was a man in a wet coat who could not think of a single thing to say that would be true and useful.

"No," he said. "No. I'm asking."

"Good." She was brisk again. "Then ask me properly and be quick, because it's nearly eleven and the queue gets worse at eleven, not better."

And then the room went back to its work, because the room always went back to its work. There were four women and two men in it, and a young man with a head injury who was pretending he was there about a bicycle. There was a woman who had a bailiff's letter in her coat and had brought it to be understood rather than handled. There was a Jamaican woman who had come eight weeks for the third time, and Hattie knew her name and her case number and what she had been told at the hearings, and Elias watched a woman with a hard hand and a laptop run a room of eleven people the way he had spent two years running a set of corridors, and understood that it was the same job.

None of it was his job. That was the part he could not get past. He ran corridors. He locked doors, and he noted the times, and he handed a building back to the morning in the condition he had found it. There was no column anywhere in the four books he had been trained in for this room, and no supervisor whose signature would make the room's problem smaller, and no version of his job that went through a person in a chair.

At two minutes past eleven his phone went and he took it out under the table because the room was quiet and it was Ivo.

"No. Yeah. Listen, is the module there?"

"—in the van. It came yesterday. It's still in the box."

"Are you fitting it tonight?"

"Corridor's dead for an hour while I bench it. I've got to strip the frame and put the new card in and then flash the config over from the terminal, and the terminal's on the far side of the site, so, an hour. Maybe more." A yawn that lasted about four seconds. "Which'll be the last of my Friday, and I've got to be in at seven tomorrow anyway, so thanks very much."

"When?"

"When it's when. I haven't put a time in. It's an hour. I don't do Friday nights for free, mate, I've got a sister with a chest and no lungs to speak of and I am not losing this one too."

He put the phone away and found that Hattie Bramm had read the whole conversation off his face.

"Trouble at work," she said.

"Some trouble at work."

"And you're going to sit in my queue and tell me about the ward-bell fault and the lock, aren't you."

"There is a bell at a sluice station that's cracked, and—"

"Don't." She said it without unkindness. "Love. Don't bring it to me. I don't care about your sluice."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked at him. "Because you came down here on a tram to ask a question about a man on my street, and the reason you came down here is that somebody told you he used to be a night porter, and the reason you care about a night porter is that something is wrong in the building you work in, and I've got nine years of something wrong in a building and the only thing anybody ever sends me is a letter on nice paper."

Elias sat there in the plastic chair and felt a thing he had not been expecting to feel that morning: his own private wound, the eighteen-year letter, the shape on the shelf above the boiler, and a fifty-four-year-old woman's nine years of unanswered post, arriving at the same moment in the same chair and one of them being worth about a hundred times more than the other.

"Yes," he said. "I do know."

"Then stop me before I say the rest of it, because I've got eleven people here and one of them is going to cry if I go down that road twice more."

---

He left at 12:20 with three things and no photographs, which was a first in his week.

He had the shortfall list: sixty-one, and the fact that the mill's own sheet had gone into a museum on the hill.

He had number fourteen, Sillick Lane, and the fact that a fitter's mate had mended a standpipe every autumn for a pint he would not take, and that the standpipe was dead now, and that nobody at the top of that street had any idea that was a fact worth keeping.

And he had a woman with nine years of letters in a folder on a table, who was going to write to him, because he had not given her a name and she was going to find one, and who was going to be right when she did.

He got the tram back at 12:34 and was on the hill by one, and slept for two hours, and was in the Museum at ten to seven with a bell on plinth thirty-four, a card in a drawer a storey above him with a name under the ink, and a Museum catalogue number he could look up on the terminal before seven, which was the sort of thing that took nine seconds and which he had spent all day on a tram being told could not be had.
