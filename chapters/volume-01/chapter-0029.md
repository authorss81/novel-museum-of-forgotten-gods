# Chapter 0029 — Told First, in a Room, in Advance

The disposition died at four minutes past midnight on Friday the thirtieth of October, which was the first four minutes of the day after the tenth working day had run out at five o'clock the evening before, and Elias Venn found out about it on Friday morning at twenty past nine because nobody rang him, which was correct, and because the corridor was different.

The cage was still there. It was a piece of furniture.

That was the whole of the news. There was no minute and no letter and no form. A proposal that had been posted at about ten o'clock on the fifteenth under a plastic wallet on a wall had run out of days on a Thursday night in a building where nobody was working, and the object in it had returned to its last certified condition and its last certified place, and the last certified place of a sealed box with a red disc on it was a cage on four castors in a service corridor off the west range lavatories, and there it was, with Custody Disc 42 on it and the padlock and a new handrail on the wall at the height of a person's elbow.

Elias Venn put one line in the day book in pencil, against the Friday, with the time, and then he went and found the third castor, which had seized in the last fortnight, and put a piece of card under it, because it was the kind of thing he could still do.

> *Fri 30 Oct, 09:26. The proposal lapsed last night at 4 min past midnight. Cage is furniture. Red disc 42, padlock QA/1187, box not opened, not moved. Facilities have been asked to take the cage out of the corridor and put it back in the old frame store in bay 9, where it stood from 1974, and they will do it on Friday the first, because they do not move a cage on a Friday afternoon unless it is urgent and this is not urgent, it is the opposite of urgent.*

Nobody in the building said anything about it. There was no minute because a lapse is not an event in an institution; a lapse is a document that was not acted upon, and the only record of it is a clause and a wall calendar and the fact that nobody wrote anything.

---

The letters had gone out on Wednesday the twenty-eighth of October, at about four o'clock in the afternoon, in a plain envelope, by ordinary post, in a hand that was not a printed hand because a printed hand on a letter to a widow of a textile mill is a different object from a letter.

They were not the Museum's letters. That was settled on the Wednesday morning at a meeting in a conservation office that had four women and two men in it and no minutes, and the settlement had four words in it: **in her own person.**

> Eleven households in Low Ward and the north of the city, named on a list of eleven prepared by an unpaid-wage organiser and checked by a municipal conciliation field officer against the electoral roll at two addresses, sent by the Museum, in the name of a Registrar, in her own person and not on the Director's letterhead, in a plain envelope, with a reply card, and not one word of the contents in the Museum's own hand except the address.
>
> **M. ILES, 29 October, 11:20, and I want it minuted in whatever this is: I am not doing this for the Director and I am not doing it for the Museum, and I would not be allowed to do it for either. I am doing it because there is a form in a safe in Irving Quay with a blank in it and a hearing on the twenty-sixth of November with a fee of a hundred and forty pounds and a schedule of contents in a building on Civic Hill that names a gate book, and because every one of those three things is a piece of paper, and eleven people are going to find out about all of it from a newspaper on the thirty-first of October unless somebody puts a piece of paper in a postbox first, and the piece of paper has to reach them before the newspaper does or it is not a kindness, it is a press release.**

The letter said four things and it said them in the plainest English anybody in that building had put on paper in thirty-one years, and Elias Venn had read it in Mara Iles's hand on the Thursday and had been told he was not to have an opinion about it and had not had one.

> 1. *On Thursday 26 November at 6:00 p.m. there is a meeting about the pay at the Sillick Mill on its last day. It is not a court. You may come. You may say nothing. You will be paid a hundred and forty pounds for the afternoon whether you come or not.*
> 2. *The Museum holds papers from the mill's office, collected on 30 October 1974. They have never been opened.*
> 3. *There is a meeting on Friday 30 October at 11:00 a.m. at the Sillick Lane branch library, for anyone who has been sent one of these letters. It is not a meeting about the Museum.*
> 4. *If you do not want to be contacted about this, tell us and we will not contact you again, and that is the end of it and it costs nothing.*

Nothing in that letter said a number.

---

The front room of the Sillick Lane branch library at eleven o'clock on Friday the thirtieth of October held six people and four staff and a tape recorder and a folding table, and the back room held eleven people and a jug of water and a queue, and the two rooms shared a wall, and Hattie Bramm was in both of them for about two hours.

The Library had been told it was a "community meeting about historic employment records" and had been told it was not a political meeting and had been told, on the Thursday, by Field Officer Marsh, in a telephone call, that if anybody asked her whether she had been told that it was a meeting about a textile mill that closed in 1974 she was to say yes, because a lie to a librarian is the fastest way to lose a room in Low Ward.

The eleven names were on a sheet of paper in Elias Venn's hand and he had typed it in the post room on the Thursday at a terminal, which printed a carbon and kept no record of anybody's name, and it said:

> **PRESENT (6)**
> PILLING, V. — 81 — 6 Sillick Lane
> HOYLE, A. — 77 — 22 Mill Row
> KEMP, D. — 74 — 9 Kirk Row
> ARDERY, J. — 68 — 31 Weaver's Row
> PRYCE, E. — 79 — 2 Broad Street
> OKONKWO, S. — 73 — 14 Chapel Yard
> **NOT IN TOUCH (5)**
> CASSIDY, M. — 83 — 40 Sillick Lane
> LOMAX, E. — 79 — 3 Ferry Bank
> LOWRIE, G. — 72 — 88 Sillick Lane
> SARN, D. — 78 — 12 Ferry Bank, second floor, first door
> SOWERBY, C. — 74 — 14 Sillick Lane

Six people in a front room in a municipal library in Low Ward on a Friday morning at the end of October, and five households that had produced nobody, and the Museum had instructed itself not to find out.

Not to find out was the whole of it, and it had been put in writing on the Thursday by the Registrar, and it was the single hardest sentence she had written in a month, and Elias Venn had read it and had understood it and had wanted to argue with it and had not, because the alternative was that a registrar in a building on Civic Hill telephoned five houses in Low Ward to ask whether a woman of eighty-three was alive, and that there is not one version of that telephone call which is not an invasion, and that the Museum had spent an entire month discovering that it was the kind of institution that did things like that to people and then called it administration.

So the Museum did not know. It did not know whether May Cassidy was dead in a room in a house on Sillick Lane or in a home on the north side, and it did not know whether Ernest Lomax had been in a hospital since August, and it did not know whether Gwen Lowrie had moved out of the city in 1991 and never told anybody, and it had five names and five addresses and nineteen days of not finding out, and it had a written reason.

---

Vera Pilling was eighty-one and she came on a bus and she was the oldest person in the room and she was not on anybody's list of speakers, and she stood up at about twenty past eleven without being asked, and the reason she was allowed to was that Hattie Bramm had said one sentence at the top of the meeting and refused to say anything else for an hour and a half.

"I'm not speaking," Hattie Bramm said. "And I'll tell you why, so that nobody has to wonder about it later. I have spoken in a room about a number four times in nine years and every single one of those four times it was because somebody who owed me money didn't want to be in the room, and the day I get up in a municipal library and start talking, this becomes a demonstration and not a meeting, and I have got a queue in the next room with a woman in it from Broad Street who has not been here since the spring and I am not going to use her to make a point."

She put a folder on the table.

"I'll do the paperwork. The first voice in this room has to be a person who stood on that gate on the Friday morning and is still alive and can say *we*, and there is exactly one requirement and that is it, and I am not going to be it because I have not stood on a gate since 1974, I stood on one with a book, and a book is not a gate."

Mara Iles said: "For the record, I am here in my own person. I am not here for the Director and I am not here for the Board and I have not brought a position. I am the registrar of this Museum and I will take the minutes, and I will not give an opinion, and if anybody asks me what the Museum thinks I shall read back what the Museum has written and let them decide whether it thinks anything."

The six people at the table were told three things by Elias Venn and one thing by Hattie Bramm, and Elias Venn was not in charge of anything and had been told that in writing on the Thursday and had accepted it.

The three things: that the Museum holds eleven named items from the works office, collected on the thirtieth of October in 1974, and that they have never been opened, and that a schedule of contents naming them had been drawn up on the twenty-ninth of October and signed by the Registrar; that one of the eleven is an envelope, sealed, contents not examined; and that the bell in the Hall of Former Faiths is a small bronze bell from the works and that it was rung at the gate, and that it is the only object in the building that any of them will ever be able to look at.

The one thing Hattie Bramm said, at about ten to twelve, and said once, and did not repeat, was the shape of it, and she said it in the plainest words anybody had used in that room:

"There are about a hundred and forty of us. That's my figure, from nine years of letters and a folder, and it isn't a mill's and it isn't a bank's and it isn't in any book in this city. Of them, nine are dead and I have got the certificates. Two are in a home. One is in a hospital. And I can put six to a street and a date and that's what I can account for and no more. And I am not giving you the hundred and forty names, and I've told the registrar why, and the registrar's written it down, so there's a piece of paper somewhere that says I refused and I was entitled to."

Nobody in that room did the sum. Elias Venn watched all six of them not do it, and it was the single hardest thing he had ever watched, and it was easier than he had expected, because Hattie Bramm had told them in advance that it would be attempted and had asked them not to.

And then Vera Pilling stood up.

She did not stand up quickly. She got her hands on the edge of the table and she got herself up and she stood at the side of it and she had a cardigan on over a dress and a headscarf and she had come on a bus from the north of the city, and she was eighty-one.

She said four things and they took about ninety seconds.

"I'm eighty-one. I was on the last shift. I was on the looms, eleven years, and I came in on that morning in the rain at about half past six with my mother's umbrella because I'd no coat of my own that'd take a wetting, and that's the only thing I've got about that morning and it's an umbrella." She put one hand flat on the table. "And I'm not here to be sad. I've been sad thirty-one years, it's no good, everybody's been sad about it, and there's a woman in the next room who's been running a queue on a Friday for nine years and that is the single most useful thing anybody has done for the people on that gate and it wasn't done in a gallery."

She looked round the table.

"And the thing I want said, and then I'll sit down, is this. There's two hundred and forty-odd households got a letter off that woman in the last month, aren't you. Two hundred and forty. In a fortnight. On a form. With a stamp." She turned to Mara Iles. "And in thirty-one years I never had one. Not one. And I want it on the record, from a woman of eighty-one, that that's what this has been for me. Not the bell. Not the museum. The letter. And I'd have had it in a fortnight if the bell hadn't been in a case, and I'd never have known that, and I'd have gone on being fifty-one in nineteen ninety-four, thinking nobody in that city had ever heard of the Sillick Mill."

She sat down.

And then, from the back of the room, Hattie Bramm said, "That's the first sentence of this, and it's the one I want in the paper, and the rest of you can have the rest of it."

---

The two flags were read out at about twenty past twelve because Elias Venn had been asked to by Field Officer Marsh, who had said *if you have anything that isn't a wage in this business, say it now, because I am putting a form in front of six people and a form with a hole in it is better than a form with a lie in it.*

The first flag he was certain of, and had been certain of since the twenty-fourth of October at about ten past six in the morning: the docket said **1961/LW/44** and the load was **1961/LW/51**, and the thing that left the building on a lorry on the Saturday morning was a fitter's lamp from a plinth in a public gallery, and the thing that stayed in the cage was a box of eleven named objects including a gate book and a bundle of wage sheets, and the lamp had gone to a bonded store on a quay in Northglass on a destination that was on a docket for a box and not for a lamp.

The second flag he was only guessing at: that somebody in an office in Northglass had entered a destination into a transfer system at seven in the morning on a Saturday because it was the destination on the docket they were working from, and had put away, on a shelf, in a bonded cage, a Schedule 12 object with a Registry seal on it, and that if it had stayed there for ninety days it would have gone into a bonded warehouse and the duty would have been paid, and that the only reason it was coming back was a memorandum on Museum notepaper with a movement number on it, initialled by a suspended night guard, which is not an instruction and has no authority whatever.

"Could you prove that?" said Hattie Bramm.

"No."

"Then it's a flag and not a fact," she said. "And I'd write it in the paper as a flag. And I'd write the lamp's number and the box's number both, because if somebody in six years reads a piece of paper that says a museum put a bell in a box and a lamp in a cage they will not believe a word of any of it, and they'll be right not to, and the only thing that will make them believe it is two numbers on the same line."

She wrote them on the back of the minutes herself, in a hard hand, and gave the page back, and Elias Venn put it in the file with the other eleven pages, and Field Officer Marsh initialled the top of the file and dated it the thirtieth of October and wrote on the front: **Record of statements made at a meeting. Not a hearing. Not evidence. A record that statements were made, by named persons, in a room, on a date, in front of a municipal officer.** And underneath that, in a smaller hand, because she had a list of things she wrote underneath things: *Held by the conciliation service and by the Registrar. Copy to the convenor of 26 Nov. Not to be sent to any employer or any insurer. Not to be given to any newspaper.*

---

They came out of the library at ten past one and the press office sent its notice at four o'clock and it was four lines long and it did not mention the meeting.

> **MORROWGATE MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS — STATEMENT 4**
> *The Museum holds records relating to the Sillick Mill, Low Ward, collected in 1974. The records are the subject of a claim which is being heard in Northglass. The Museum has written to affected individuals. The Museum will not make further comment on the claim.*

Redgate & Kell rang the security room at half past four and asked the fourth question, which was *who wrote the letters*, and the press office said the Museum would not say, and then said it anyway, on the Thursday, in a way that let a byline be built.

The *Chronicle* had it on the Friday evening, on the early city page, and it was in the street before eight. Not from the Museum. From a man who was in the front room of the Sillick Lane branch library at twenty to twelve that morning and who telephoned the reporter from a call box outside a bank at two o'clock and who telephoned him again at five and who was **not one of the six**, and who had been in the room because he had a letter off the same post, and who had a sister in Ferry Bank.

The first sentence in the *Chronicle* was: *A museum registrar told eleven households in Low Ward on Friday that a bell in its Hall of Former Faiths may be the last working voice of the Sillick Mill, and that it may be heard in public on 7 November.*

That sentence was not written by anybody in the Morrowgate Museum of Quiet Saints. It was written by a man of about sixty-eight in a municipal library in Low Ward who had a stake in a wage and a grievance about a bell, and it was not accurate, and three of its four claims had not been established, and it was the first sentence, and the Museum could not touch it, and could not touch it *because it had promised a room that it would not*, and had done so in writing, on the Wednesday, at a meeting with four women and two men in it and no minutes — which is a Wednesday, and not the Wednesday the letters went out on, because that is the sort of sentence a Museum writes.

Elias Venn had bought one at seven forty-five and read it standing at a loading-bay desk at ten past eight on Friday evening, and did two things. He wrote the time and the four claims and which of them were not established, in the block hand, in the notebook. And then he read the whole article twice more, and looked for the name of the Registrar, and there was not one, and looked for the number of the households, and it said *dozens*, and he wrote under the line, in pencil: *It was eleven. He could have had eleven. He had a sheet of paper in his coat.*

And then he went and did the dead letters, because it was Friday and it was the last day he was going to be in this building on a Friday for a while, and there were two hundred and sixty-four of them, and a clerk's is a slow job, and a man who has said a number in a room has got about nine hours of work in front of him and a very good reason to do it.

---

The two undelivered letters were the last thing in the day and he found them at about nine o'clock at night because the post had been counted and the two reply cards had come back and there was nothing else in the envelope.

**12 Ferry Bank, second floor, first door.** A registered letter, signed for by a niece who is post, on the Wednesday. The niece had put it on the table and gone to work and had not read it, because it was in an envelope with a window and a printed line about who it was from, and the niece had seen a museum's name on it and had put it face down on a sideboard, and a woman of seventy-eight with a bad chest had come home on the Friday, seen it face down, and had left it there for two days because there are letters you do not open while you are still working, and because she had a fire lit and a radio on and a hardback cash book on the table, and because she was seventy-eight, and because the letter said *there is a meeting* and she had had a meeting in 1974 that had cost her the rest of her life.

**14 Sillick Lane.** Not registered, ordinary post, put through a box in a door. It was addressed to **CLIFF SOWERBY**, and Cliff Sowerby had been in a hospital in the north of the city with a shoulder and a wrist and a temperature of about thirty-four until the Monday, and was at the number himself since the Monday night, and had not told anybody that he was out, and 14 Sillick Lane was a flat with a woman of fifty-four in it, and the woman of fifty-four had taken the letter out of the box at about eleven o'clock on Friday morning, in her own hall, and had read the name on the front of it, and had put it in her inside coat pocket, and had come down the road to the library at half past ten, and had put a hundred and forty names in a drawer in 1974 and had said *I am not giving it to anybody*, and had carried a letter in her coat all day and had not opened it and had not mentioned it.

Elias Venn stood at a loading-bay desk at nine o'clock on Friday the thirtieth of October with two reply cards and two undelivered letters in his hand, and did the arithmetic he had done every night for a month, standing up, the way he did everything, because it was the only way he knew to keep a thing from getting away from him.

Doreen Sarn, seventy-eight, in a second-floor flat in Ferry Bank with a hardback cash book that is the only copy of a wage shortfall sheet in the world, and who has been to a registry office in Northglass and read a four-line letter from a chief clerk, and who said *on the basis of a record of that kind I will not treat a sealed item as destroyed*, and who is on a list of eleven and has not been told that she is on it.

Cliff Sowerby, seventy-four, who went through a gap in a fence on the seventeenth of October because a date in a document was ten days out, and who had the pump housing lifted off a flooded slip on the twenty-fourth by a crane his nephew arranged, and who came out of a hospital on the Monday before last and gave evidence for forty minutes on the Wednesday in a hearing in Northglass and told them he would not be a trespasser in a room with a tape in it, and who is on a list of eleven, at his own front door, in his own hall, and who has not been told.

Two people out of about a hundred and forty who are the most owed in this entire business, and neither of them knows it, and both of them could have been spoken to in about eleven minutes on a Friday morning by anybody in this city who had a telephone, and the reason nobody spoke to them is that the woman who built the letter wrote eleven lines that said *if you do not want to be contacted about this, tell us and we will not contact you again*, and a building on Civic Hill has spent a month learning that there is not one of its procedures that does not eventually cost somebody the thing it was supposed to protect.

He wrote the time against it. It was 21:14 on Friday the thirtieth of October, and the page was the last of that month and it was the fourth page in a notebook that had ninety-one lines in it, and the ninety-first was a sentence and he had spent a month making sure of it.

> *Fri 30 Oct, 21:14. Two letters not delivered. SARN, 12 Ferry Bank — registered, signed for by a niece, face down on a sideboard since Wednesday. SOWERBY, 14 Sillick Lane — through a box in the door, taken in by the one person in this city who could have opened it, and carried in a coat all day, and not opened.*
> *Both of them are owed the most in this business. Neither of them knows it. There is no telephone call that fixes that and everybody who wants it fixed wants to make it.*
> *The eleventh line of the letter says: if you do not want to be contacted about this, tell us and we will not contact you again. And sixteen days ago, in a front room in Ferry Bank, a woman of seventy-eight said a book gets read in a room with the people it is about in it, and after that it belongs to more than one person, and nobody can be got at over it.*
> *I have had that sentence in a notebook since the fourteenth of October and I have not once been able to make it work in a letter.*
> *A letter is a knock on a door with no knocker.*
