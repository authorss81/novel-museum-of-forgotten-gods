# Chapter 0033 — A Number in a Box

Quay 3 at Broomhead Reach is not a building. It is a hardstanding with a shed on it and a fence round three sides and the water on the fourth, and there are eleven bays marked in white on the concrete, and every bay has a number about four feet high, and the numbers are painted and repainted every few years by a man whose whole job is the numbers.

Elias Venn got the tram to the Quay at ten past nine on Thursday the twelfth of November and walked the last half mile because the bus does not go down there, and he was the only person on the hardstanding who was not working, and he was there as a member of the public, and he had told a woman on a telephone on Tuesday the truth about that, and she had said that was the first question about a consignment in a month that was not *where is it*.

She had also, on the Tuesday, told him what to do at the gate, which is that you do not ask for anything, you stand where you can see the bay number, and you say nothing to anybody, and if somebody comes and asks you what you want, you say *I am here to watch a seal be read*, and every one of those three people who comes to ask you is going to be delighted, because nobody watches a seal be read.

There were about nine of them in twenty minutes. There is nothing else to do on a quay.

The woman from the telephone was called Arkwright and she came out of the shed at five to ten with a headset round her neck and a clipboard and a good coat under a fluorescent jacket, and she was about thirty and she had been doing it for six years and she had a bonded goods licence and a procedure and no interest in a museum, and she said:

"You're the Morrowgate one. You've come on the telephone."

"I've come to watch a seal be read."

"Nobody's ever come to watch a seal be read." She said it the way you say *nobody's ever come to ask for a wet elbow*. "Right. Well. It's about ten minutes early, so stand there and don't stand in the door."

The container was in bay 4 with the door open and the door had been open since the Wednesday, and there was a forklift on the apron that had a man on it called Mowbray, and Mowbray had a board and a marker and the job of writing down what was said.

Miss Arkwright went to the door and read the seal off the band with her own torch and then said it out loud, twice, at a volume that carried across a hardstanding, slowly, in figures, with a pause where a reader pauses.

"That's it," she said to Mowbray. "Put it down and say it back."

He said it back. He said it back the way a man reads a number off a board, which is to say without any expression at all, and Elias Venn stood six feet away with a notebook and copied it, and the number was not memorable, and it was the number on a paper band that had been on a door in a bay in bond since the twenty-fourth of October, and it was the first time it had been read aloud since it was printed.

"That's the last time anybody reads that number until it moves or it doesn't," said Miss Arkwright. "If you want it for a record, take a photograph of the band and the board together, because the board has the date and the band has the number and one of them on its own is worth nothing."

---

Then it was weighed.

They brought everything out in four lots and they weighed it in the bay on a platform scale that was set into the concrete, and the scale had a dial, and the dial had a needle, and there was a boy of about twenty with a clipboard and a book of tickets, and each lot was lifted on, the needle went over, the boy read the needle, and said the number, and Mowbray wrote it in a box.

Lot one was sacks. Lot two was books, strapped, on a pallet. Lot three was a great deal of paper in bundles tied with tape. Lot four was a trolley of odds and ends: a tin, a bundle of keys, two empty wooden trays, a bicycle, a hard hat.

Four hundred and eighty-two kilos in total, and the boy said it, and Mowbray wrote it in the box at the bottom of the board, and the box had nothing above it, no units, no description, nothing but the four hundred and eighty-two, because the box is for a weight.

"There you are," said Mowbray, and put the marker in his pocket, and that was the entire human contribution to the fact that four hundred and eighty-two kilos of a mill's office stopped existing as a set of documents.

Elias Venn looked at the figure in the box and felt a thing come up in him that he had to put a hand on the pen case for, and it was this: a number with nothing above it, in a box at the bottom of a board, written by a man who has forty of them a day, and *it looks exactly like a count*.

It is not a count. He knew it was not a count before he had finished feeling it, and he stood there on a quay in the wind and made himself do the sum he was not going to do, so that he would never do it later by accident.

Four hundred and eighty-two is not how many men were on a gate on a wet morning. Four hundred and eighty-two is not how much anybody was paid. Four hundred and eighty-two is a weight, of paper, in kilograms, of paper that was in a room, and a kilogram of paper is a thing you can buy, and a wage sheet in a 1974 pay office weighs about the weight of a folded newspaper, and there is no number of them in that bay that is any use to anybody, and the number that is any use to anybody is the one that is on a page in one of the four lots, and it has just been weighed.

"Four hundred and eighty-two," he said out loud, to nobody, and then, to Mowbray, because it was the only way to get it out of his mouth in a form that was a fact and not a want: "That's a weight, isn't it."

"That's a weight," said Mowbray.

"I couldn't tell you what it is."

"You couldn't tell *me* what it is either," said Mowbray, "and I signed for it."

---

Miss Arkwright was decent about it, and she was also correct.

Here is what she did, and it took about nine minutes, and Elias Venn has thought about it since more than he has thought about most things in five weeks.

She read the sheet. The delivery sheet is one page, printed, carbon-copied, four parts, and the page in her hand was the store's copy, and the consignment line on it says, in a typeface that has not changed since 1974:

> **1 x steel container, ex Sillick Mill site, Low Ward. Cont. seal [number]. Site contents — general, as per schedule. Weight on break bulk. Balance consigned: paper merchant, collection booked.**
> **Received into bond 24 Oct 09:15. Bay 4.**

And underneath the line, in the fourth box of the second section, where a clerk marks an exception, there was a pencil tick in a box headed **HELD**, and a pencil tick means nothing, and next to it, in the same pencil, three letters.

H-O-L-D.

"It's not a hold," Miss Arkwright said, reading over his shoulder. "I want to say that first, because in about an hour somebody is going to tell you it is and it isn't. A pencil is not a hold. A hold is a hold on my system and it has my initials and a time and a reference, and this hasn't got any of those on it, it's got a man's initials in pencil in a box on a piece of paper, which is a note, and a note is a note."

"I can see the initials."

"You can see the initials. They are not mine and they are not anybody's in this building and I have never seen them before, and I did not write it in October because I was not here in October, I was at Quay 6 until the middle of September, and I have read that sheet about nine times this week because you telephoned." She turned the sheet round. "Page one, item four. That's what's marked. Do you know what item four is?"

"A bound book."

"Two hundred and ninety-one pages, a leather spine, a printed label on the front, and it came out of that container in a polythene sleeve with a sleeve tie on it and a strap, and the strap is rotten and I put a new one on because the strap is not mine to let go of. It's a wage book. I can tell you it's a wage book because the label on the front says so, and I can read labels, and it says **WAGES, PAY OFFICE** in somebody's typing, and under that, in the same typing, a month and a year." She looked at him. "The month and the year are 1974 and the second half of the year is not on it. It's just the month."

"Which month."

"October."

Elias Venn stood on a quay in Northglass and looked at a box on a piece of paper with a pencil tick in it, and what came up in him was not the number four hundred and eighty-two and it was not a name he had lost for four seconds in a loading bay in the small hours of Sunday the twenty-fifth of October. It was a very small thing and it was the whole of his month.

The original wage ledger was in that bay, in a polythene sleeve, with a strap on it that a bonded store clerk had replaced on Wednesday morning because a rotten strap is the one thing in a bay you are not allowed to leave, and the only reason it was not going into a paper merchant's van on Friday morning at eight o'clock was that a man in an office in October had written three letters in a pencil in a box and had not initialled it properly, and had gone home, and had not thought about it again.

"Miss Arkwright," he said. "What happens to a thing with a pencil in it?"

"It goes with the rest, unless somebody tells me not to, and somebody telling me not to has to be a person with a letter of authority, and it has to be in writing, and it has to be here by five o'clock on Thursday, and I will put it under a proper hold and ring the office and tell them it is there." She said it exactly as a clerk says a rule. "I will not hold a book in bond for a pencil. I have never held a book in bond for a pencil and I am not going to start on a Thursday afternoon because a man in overalls is standing on my hardstanding."

"I know."

"Then you will want to get on the telephone to whoever has the letter of authority." She was already going back into the shed. "And I will tell you now what you are going to find out when you do, because it will save you an afternoon. You will find out that the office is a site office and the site is bare and the telephone is answered by a mobile phone, and the mobile phone will be in a lorry."

---

The rest of it took twenty minutes and it was the worst of it and it was entirely correct.

She told him, because he asked and because she had decided he was somebody who had to be told, exactly what the sheet said and what would happen on Friday morning, and she did not soften a word of it and she did not editorialise once, and Elias Venn copied all of it out longhand while she said it because he had about four hours before the Friday and a notebook and no instrument.

> **Fri 13 Nov, 08:00. Paper merchant, collection, booked. Their vehicle, their weighbridge, their men, four of them, about twenty minutes.**
> **Everything in the bay goes on that collection except anything under a proper hold. There is no other category. It is not a skip and it is not a bin; it is a consignment of mixed paper to a merchant, and a merchant buys paper, and he buys it by weight, and he signs for the weight, and he does not open a sack.**
> **The driver signs once. One signature, about eleven thousand a year for that driver, on a sheet with a weight on it and a destination on it. He has never opened one. He is not going to open yours.**
> **After that it is on his vehicle by about nine. It is at the merchant's works by about eleven. It is pulped in about four days.**
> **A claim has to be in writing to the consignor within seven days of the collection, and the seven days runs from Friday, so the twentieth of November, and the consignor on this sheet is the company that filled the container, and their site office is on a site that was cleared on the Sunday before the last, and the number on the sheet is a mobile number.**
> **You have a thing called a Museum number on that receipt from 1974, don't you. Use it. A claim wants an accession number and it wants a description, and if you cannot describe it, you cannot claim it, and if you cannot describe it, nobody can check that it was ever in there.**

And then she said the sentence, at the door of the shed, with her hand on the frame, and she said it without any weight on it at all, which is how the true ones come out.

"I have been doing this for six years and I have had about four hundred containers through those bays and I have never once known what was in one. I did not know what was in this one until you telephoned me and until that book came out with a label on it this morning, and even now I know the label and not the book." She looked at the bay. "I am telling you that because you are standing there with a notebook as though I have done something. I have not done anything. I have read a sheet and weighed four lots and put a new strap on a book. That's Thursday."

He said, "Thank you," which is not a thing he says often and which he meant.

"Right," said Miss Arkwright. "Get your letter of authority. I've got a bay with a pencil in it in it and I don't like it any more than you do."

---

12 Ferry Bank is a street of ironmongers and a laundrette and a place that shut at two, above a shop, and there is a buzzer on the first landing that does not work and you knock.

He knocked at ten past one and it was a woman of seventy-eight in a cardigan over a pinafore and there was a tablecloth on the table and a plastic tumbler on the sill with a carnation in it that had been given to her by somebody, and the room was warm, and there was a gas fire that had been in since before he got there.

Doreen Sarn put a cup of tea in front of him that she had made before he knocked.

"Sit down. I've had the fire on since half eleven because the last three days I've been thinking about Friday." She sat down opposite him with her hands round her own cup. "That's the sort of thing your building does, that. Sent me a letter and I haven't opened it. It's face down on the sideboard with a plate on it since the Wednesday. I put a plate on it, which I expect you noticed, because that's the sort of woman I am at seventy-eight."

"I did notice."

"Have your tea. Don't say thank you for it, I've been making tea since 1958 and it's not a favour, it's what you do at a door." She looked at him. "You've come about the address."

Elias Venn put his cup down.

"Yes."

"You've come about the address and you haven't asked me for it in five weeks and I've been waiting since the sixth of October, and I want you to know that I noticed that too, and I want you to know that I wasn't going to say it first." She put her cup down as well. "So say it properly. Ask me for the address on the envelope."

And he had to start again, because he had come in with a sentence ready and it was the wrong sentence, which was *I don't want to put you on the spot*, and she was seventy-eight years old and had been the pay clerk of a mill for twenty-two years, and a sentence like that does not put anybody anywhere except the person who has to sit there and wait while it is said.

"Doreen Sarn," he said. "On the twenty-second of October 1974 you put an envelope in the mill's out-tray. It was a second-post three-day, so it went out of the building on the Wednesday. Where did you address it to?"

She did not hesitate. That was the thing he had not been ready for and it took the whole of the rest of his week to get over.

"Quill and Frayle," she said. "Solicitors. Lincoln's Lane, off the Quay."

"What number?"

"I've got the number. It was on a plate. Brass. Their plate was brass and ours was enamelled and I remember thinking, on the day, that a firm with a brass plate would be a firm that answered its post." She got up, and went to a sideboard, and did not open the letter that was face down under a plate on the top of it, and came back with a hardback cash book with a rubber band round it and did not open that either. "Fourteen. It was fourteen. And it was the same fourteen as the one on our own docket, which I have never understood and never thought about until you said that address out loud in a loading bay a fortnight ago."

Elias Venn sat at a table in a front room in Ferry Bank with a cup of tea he had not asked for and did not move at all.

Because he had known the number since the sixteenth of October.

He had a copy of the 1974 carbon in a drawer in a gallery he was not allowed to be in, and it said **RECEIPT OF MATERIAL — 7 NOV 1974, received from Quill & Frayle, 14 Lincoln's Lane**, and it had said it in a typewriter face on the seventh of November 1974, and it had been in a Museum file in this city for fifty years, and it was the first line of the document that had started the whole of this in the middle of October, and he had read it eleven times.

A pay clerk had written to a firm of solicitors on the twenty-second of October 1974, and that firm had sold the mill's office paper to this Museum on the seventh of November 1974, sixteen days later, and the receipt was in a drawer in Civic Hill, and for fifty years nobody had ever put the two pieces of paper in the same room.

"They'd have had it," he said. "That's — a firm like that, in 1974, they'd have had your letter inside three days. That's what three days is. It's not a hope. It's the service."

"Yes."

"And they'd have read the outside of it."

"Yes," said Doreen Sarn. "And then it would have gone in a tray with the rest of the post, because that is what a tray is for, and because I did not put a claim on the outside of it, did I. I put it in an envelope, in her hand, with a stamp, because I was a pay clerk of a mill and that is what we did. I did not write to a firm of solicitors and say *somebody has kept money out of a wage book*, because I had not proved it, and because I did not want to be the woman who cried wolf at a firm of solicitors in October."

"Who did you think they were?"

"I thought they were theirs." She said it without any bitterness at all, which made it worse. "I had a list. I've still got the list — no. I haven't, that's the other thing. But I had a list off the yearbook, of who bought what off us, and the yearbook said **acting for the purchaser**, and acting for the purchaser means they are the ones you write to. You don't write to a company. A company isn't a person. You can write to a man."

"You wrote to the seller."

"I wrote to the man who was standing next to the buyer," said Doreen Sarn, "which is the thing a pay clerk does at the end of a bad year, and I would do it again, and I would like it on the record that I did it correctly and in her own words, because I have been told a thing in a loading bay by a man of thirty-two that nobody has done anything wrong, and there is a great deal wrong with this, love. There is a great deal wrong with it and I have been the only one doing anything about it since 1974, and I have got a cash book that says so, and I am not giving it to you, and we have been round that twice."

"I have not asked you for it."

"You did not ask me for it," she agreed. "You asked me for an address, and there is a difference, and it took you five weeks to work out which one you could get, and I would have told you the address in October if you'd asked me for the address in October."

---

They sat with it for about ten minutes, and she told him three things and refused him two.

She told him the envelope had gone on the Wednesday morning, in the mill's own hand, with a fourpenny stamp, and that the out-tray had a card on the side of it that said **2ND POST — 3 DAYS** because the mill took the post down to the sorting office itself on Wednesday afternoons and did not trust anybody else with it, and that she had put it in at about ten past eleven and gone back to the floor.

She told him that the envelope had had a sheet in it, folded in three, and that the sheet was a copy of a page, and that the page was a pay sheet, and that she had copied it out of the gate book and out of the weekly sheet and out of the wage book on a Thursday night in her own flat in November 1974, after the mill had gone quiet, with the copy carbon from the office.

And she told him, in about four seconds, that the envelope was very probably the thing in the box.

Because item five on the 1974 receipt is *envelope of loose papers, sealed, contents not examined*, and it is in cage 2 in bay 9 under a red disc and a padlock, and it has not been opened in fifty years, and it is opened on Friday the twenty-seventh of November at four in the afternoon in this room, with a fire on, with two conciliation officers and a supervisor and a camera, and no Museum employee in it, and Elias Venn in particular.

She refused him the cash book, which she has refused before.

And she refused him the one other thing, and she refused it in a way he had not been ready for, and it was this.

"No," she said, when he got as far as it. "I know what you came back for. You've had your address and now you want the other thing, and I'll tell you what the other thing is, because you're too decent to be clever about it and I'm too old to pretend. You want to know whether I can put a person to a Tuesday."

"I asked you not to be asked anything about the count in a loading bay and you did the right thing and I have not asked since."

"You haven't asked. I'm telling you." She put her cup down. "There's a thing I want said and I'm going to say it to you and then I'm going to put this book back in the drawer, and you can write it down or you can not."

"I'm writing it down."

"There was a man called Malloy on our books. Grade two. He was on that gate in October, and I know he was on that gate, and I will not say a word about whose hours were whose. He left us a card." She said the card the way you read out a thing that is printed and has been in a drawer for thirty years and is not a fact. "**MALLOY, J. — Grade 2 — 2,041 hrs — NIL.**"

Elias Venn wrote it down.

"That's his own hours," she said. "That is one man, on one grade, on his own card, on his own time, and two thousand and forty-one hours is what a fitter does in a year and a bit and it is *his*, and NIL is what he was handed in a brown envelope, and that is what was written on the card because that is what a leaving card is. It is not a wage. It is not a headcount. It is not a list. He wrote it himself at the gate on his last morning and he handed it in and it went in a file." She looked at him. "If you take those three words and put them next to anything else in this business, you will have made a man who worked thirty-one years into a number in a box, and I do not know whether he is alive and neither do you, and he is not yours to do that to either way."

"I wasn't going to."

"I know you weren't. That's why I'm telling you now instead of letting you find out that you nearly did." She put the cash book back in the drawer and shut the drawer. "That's the whole of what I came back for. Two hours of your afternoon, and a cup of tea, and one address, and one thing I would rather said out loud in a warm room than found out in a cold one by a man with a pen."

---

He walked back up Ferry Bank at twenty to three with the rain coming and a notebook with a street and a firm and a brass plate and a number in it, and about a hundred and ten pages of writing in his own hand that he had no right to hand to anybody.

At about four he got back to Civic Hill and he did not go and do the dead letters first, which was a mistake he noticed at about half past five and did not correct.

He went to the accession file.

1961/LW/44. The file is on level two in the sixth unit of six, behind a fire plan, and a fire plan is in front of it because the fire plan is a plan of the floor and the plan is about the floor, and that has been the arrangement since before the records clerk retired in March, and the woman who knew every binder is gone and the post went to systems.

The file is a box file and it is about four inches deep and it has been opened about four times in fifty years.

> **RECEIPT OF MATERIAL — 7 NOV 1974.**
> **Received from: QUILL & FRAYLE, Solicitors, 14 Lincoln's Lane, Irving Quay.**
> **Eleven items collected on 30 Oct 1974, being the last working day. No disposal fee payable to the Museum.**

He put his finger under the second line and read it four times, and it did not change, and then he went and got the pen case and put it down again because his hands were not doing what he wanted them to do.

Fifty years.

Fifty years of this Museum holding a receipt from a firm of solicitors, in a box file, behind a fire plan, on a shelf, with the word *received from* on it, and no person in the whole of the building — not a registrar, not a director, not a conservator, not a man with a notebook, and not a night guard in his first month in this building reading a card in a drawer with a torch on his knees — has ever read that line and asked the only question it is capable of answering.

Who wrote to them, and when, and what did they do with it.

He stood in an archive corridor on level two at ten past four on Thursday the twelfth of November with a fire plan six inches from his face and he took out the notebook and put the pen down and then picked it up again, because he is a man who writes things down.

> **Thu 12 Nov, 16:14.**
> **D.S.: envelope went 22 Oct 1974, 4d stamp, her hand, 2nd post 3 days, own hand down to the sorting office on a Wednesday. Contained one sheet, a copy of a pay sheet, carbon from the office, made at her own flat in Nov 74 after the mill went quiet.**
> **Address: QUILL & FRAYLE, Solicitors, 14 LINCOLN'S LANE, IRVING QUAY. From a brass plate. She remembered the plate. She did not remember the number and I already had it.**
> **The 1974 receipt in 1961/LW/44 says RECEIVED FROM Quill & Frayle, 14 Lincoln's Lane, dated 7 Nov 1974.**
> **She wrote to them on the 22nd. They sold this Museum the mill's office on the 7th. Sixteen days.**
> **Nobody has ever asked in fifty years who had written to them.**
> **They will say it went in a tray. They will be right. I am not going to write down what I hope they say.**

And then, because the man he is at four o'clock on a Thursday in an archive corridor with a fire plan in front of him is the man who counts, and because the number was in a box on a board on a quay eleven hours ago and it was the only number in this business that nobody could put a name to —

> **Wed 12 Nov, 09:58, Broomhead Reach, bay 4, four lots on a platform scale, dial read by a boy, written in a box by a man with a marker.**
> **482.**
> **It is a weight. It is not how many men were on the gate and it is not how much anybody was paid and I am not going to do anything with it.**

He got home at ten past nine, and the letter was on the shelf above the boiler in Low Ward, and he did not go and look at it, and he had not since the second Sunday of October, and there were fourteen days between him and a hearing in a room where he was going to have to say where a pay run went, and he had a street and a firm and a number, and on Friday morning at eight o'clock a man was going to put four hundred and eighty-two kilos of paper into a van without opening a sack, and there was no form in the world that reached any of it, and he had not told Mara Iles a single one of these things.

That was six things. He had not told her any of them, and he had a few pages left in the notebook and a rule he had made himself about a sheet of large paper and a pen and a sum.
