# Chapter 0031 — A Lamp in a Cupboard

The van came up the service lane at ten past seven on Tuesday the tenth of November and stopped the way vans stop against a wall, which is to say without needing to, and Elias Venn was on the loading-bay steps with his coat buttoned because the morning had that cold that gets into a coat before you have decided about it.

The driver got out, looked at the bay markings, and got back in again. There was a person in the passenger seat who got out instead, and she was about fifty, and she had a satchel, and she had a form on a clipboard, and she said, before Elias had got down the last three steps:

"Are you the collections clerk?"

"No."

"Thank you," she said, and wrote something on the clipboard, and handed it to him. "That's fine. You're the person who receives it, then, and that's the same box with a different question on it, and I'll need you to write your name against the line and the time and not to open anything."

The form was one sheet. It had a number in the top right in the shape the Museum gave to everything, and it had a line that said **CONDITION ON RETURN**, and a second line that said **SEAL**, and a third, further down, that said **DISPOSITION: re-plinth (Annexe D object, sealed) / stores / other, circle one**.

The seal was on the box. It was a paper seal over the closure, banded, printed with a store's name and a date and a five-figure number, and the number was the same one that had gone onto the Quay 3 four lines on the twenty-fourth of October, and it was intact, and it had been intact for seventeen days, and she made him look at it before she made him touch anything.

"That's a good seal," she said. "That is the only thing on this form I actually care about. The rest of it is a box with a lamp in it and I have done about two hundred of these this year."

He wrote his name and the time, 07:14, and the seal number, and he read the sheet first, all the way down, because he reads them.

Line four said: **Item re-plinthed on receipt, in accordance with the current certificate of condition (Annexe C).** Line five said: **Item stored in the event that the re-plinth is deferred.**

He got a biro out and put a stroke through line four, and then he put his initials beside the stroke, and then he wrote on the blank at the foot, because there was a blank at the foot: **RE-PLINTH DEFERRED. Item is a Schedule 12 carried object. It is not to be re-plinthed while a Registry determination is open. Stores. E. Venn (receiving), 07:16.**

She read it, and she did not argue with any part of it, and she said, "That's a good answer," which he had heard from a transfer clerk on a loading bay at six in the morning three weeks before, and which had felt then like a sentence about a crate and felt now like a sentence about his whole month.

"Stores," she said. "Which cupboard."

"That one."

It was cupboard 14, off the old frame store corridor on the ground floor, which is to say eight feet from the place where the cage had stood for a month and did not stand now. The key was on a board and the board had a number on it and he signed for the key and signed it back. She stayed in the bay with the engine running and did not come in, because she was not a Museum employee and had been told that the loading-bay office was not a place a member of the public could be.

The box went on the bottom shelf, on its side, on a piece of hardboard somebody had cut for the purpose. It was about the size of a bread bin. And on the top of it, in a hand that was not his, was **1961/LW/51**.

---

The Gallery of Unnamed Hands is barred to him. He had been in it twice since the twenty-sixth of October and both times by signing a visitors' book as a member of the public, and he was not going to do it a third time on a Tuesday morning for a lamp.

So he stood in the corridor outside it, from about twenty to eight, and looked through the open doorway at plinth 9.

Plinth 9 is a felt-topped plinth with no case. It has had no case since the twelfth of October, when the case went to a docket that did not travel. On the plinth there is a card in a plastic sleeve at the same slight angle the lamp's card used to be at, and the card says:

> **1961/LW/51 — FITTER'S LAMP. On loan from a bonded store, returned 10 November 2024. In stores pending a determination. See note on drawer 6B.**

And drawer 6B was open. It has been open since the middle of October. It is the third drawer down in the west unit of six, it runs on good runners, and the thing that is actually in it is a single sheet of carbon paper, thin, grey, folded once across, with eleven lines of typing on it and a ballpoint entry in the right-hand margin in a hand that slants the way a person's hand slants when they are writing standing up at a bench.

Elias Venn stood in a corridor and looked at it through a doorway from about eleven feet away and he could not read a word of it, and he had read it four times, on the sixteenth of October, with a torch, on his knees.

That was the price of the cupboard. He had spent four minutes working out, in the loading bay, at about half past seven, that the store was the correct decision, and he had been right, and being right had cost the case a thing it could not get back.

Because a store cupboard is a better place for a lamp than a public plinth. Nobody can lean on it. Nobody can put a plastic sleeve on the edge of it. It has a number on the shelf and a number on the key board and a signature in a book every time it moves, and in five years when the whole of this has been somebody else's problem there will be a shelf and a schedule and a person who knows the rule. Plinth 9 with a card that says *see note on drawer 6B* and an open drawer is not a store. It is an invitation with a hook in it. It is the only place in the world where a 1974 carbon receipt of eleven items is lying loose, and every person who walks into that room can see it, and one day somebody is going to walk in with a camera and a schedule and a reason.

So: the cupboard. Correct. Costly. Both.

He did the arithmetic, which he was not proud of, and which he could not stop doing, and which he wrote in the notebook afterwards in one line and did not act on.

*Plinth 9 with an open drawer is the reason Ivo's four exposures exist.*

Because in March, on a tripod, with a scale rule, for the insurance schedule, drawer 6B was open. That is the whole reason there are four photographs of a card in a police file. It was open then too. It was open the day before that. A drawer that is open is a drawer that anybody's condition survey will find, and the day a drawer is shut is the day a card cannot be seen, and the card in that drawer is the reason a room holds eight objects against a sheet dated 1988 that says nine, and the reason a woman of eighty-one said in a room in October that she had never had a letter.

He shut the cupboard. He turned the key. He signed the key back on the board, and the board has a column for the time, and the time was 07:41, and nobody would ever read that column.

---

Ivo Pell came in at eight and went up to the systems office with a mug, and Elias went with him, not because he was asked, and because the systems index is on a screen in a room anybody can walk into and read, and because it was Tuesday, and because the index is corrected on the ordinary Tuesday cycle and had been corrected on the ordinary Tuesday cycle twice already this month and both times it had cost somebody something.

The line was in already. Ivo had put it in at 07:52 without being asked, and the line was under *schedule of collections, location, Gallery of Unnamed Hands* and it said:

> **1961/LW/51 (fitter's lamp) — location amended 10 Nov 2024. Was: Gallery of Unnamed Hands, plinth 9. Now: stores, cupboard 14, ground floor, key board 14. Seal intact on receipt. Not on display. Not to be re-plinthed pending Registry determination.**

There was a reference field and a date field and an action field, and the action field said **NONE — RECORD CORRECTION**, and underneath the line there was a line in Ivo's own hand on a sticky strip stuck to the bezel of the monitor, which said:

*Third time I've done this. This is the first one that has cost anybody anything. Don't let anybody tell you it's a bin count.*

"Two hundred and forty-seven items in that index with a location in it," Ivo said, "and about nine hundred lines of it, and it's the only document in this building that anybody can walk in and read and find out what's in a room. That's not an accident. That's the only reason I'm allowed to touch it."

"Who else has read that line?"

"Nobody yet. It's seven minutes old." Ivo drank out of the mug. "It'll be read. Everything in that index gets read. It's on a screen in a room with a kettle in it."

Elias looked at the line for a while.

"Change the word *amended*," he said.

"It says amended. It's the right word."

"It's the wrong word for anybody who's a solicitor. *Amended* means it was wrong before and now it's right. It wasn't wrong. It was **true** on Monday and it's true today, and the lamp went somewhere on Monday, and the thing that happened is that a person moved it on purpose and wrote down why." He was already regretting it, and he said it anyway, because it was the kind of sentence that has to be said in a room where a mug is being drunk out of, and not in a corridor. "Put *relocated*. And put *by order of the receiving officer* so that there's a person in it. Not me. Whoever was on the desk."

"You were on the desk."

"I was on the steps at ten past seven in a coat, and I'm not a member of staff, and I have been on no rota since the eleventh of October, and if a lawyer asks who relocated a Schedule 12 object out of a public gallery and into a cupboard, the answer cannot be a suspended night guard." He stopped. "That's not me being careful. That's the sentence I'd have said in October and I want it said in a systems index before anybody from a firm walks in here."

Ivo changed the line. It took him about ninety seconds and he initialled the amendment with the date and the time, and the index keeps amendments, and the old wording is still on the file, which is the entire reason the file is worth anything.

"Three times now," he said. "The lamp, the cage, and this."

"And the first one cost a loading bay and the second one cost a corridor," Elias said. "You want to know what the third one costs, it's the drawer."

He said it, and Ivo looked at him, and neither of them said anything else about it, and the systems index went down the screen and got on with its ordinary Tuesday.

---

At about eleven the telephone in the loading-bay office rang, which is not a thing that happens, because it is not a line anybody uses, because the loading bay has no line and the office telephone is the one that came off the porters' board in 1991 and works about one time in three.

Elias let it ring four times. On the fifth it stopped.

It rang again at 11:06 and he picked it up.

"Broomhead Reach, bonded store." A woman's voice, unhurried, with a quay in the background of it somewhere and a gull on the recording. "Is that the Morrowgate? There's a consignment out of your collection sat in our bay four in bond since Friday the twenty-third, and I want to give you the dates on it because you're going to want them, and I have got them in front of me."

"Yes."

"Right. It's going to be broken bulk tomorrow, the eleventh, at twenty to ten, nine forty in the morning, and that's not a date we choose, that's the date the man who booked it chose, and I can't hold it for you because the booking's on the sheet and the sheet's got his initials on it." A pause, and a keyboard. "And then the balance of it — the loose paper, the books, the odd bit — goes to a paper merchant, and they've got a collection booked for Friday the thirteenth at eight, because that's when they come, and it's the same every week, and they were in on Friday last and they'll be in on Friday this, and I'm telling you now because the last time we had a collection nobody knew about it was a very expensive morning for a lot of people."

Elias had the notebook open. He had it open before the call came and he had a date at the top of a page and no lines under it.

"Break bulk, Wednesday the eleventh, nine forty. Paper merchant, Friday the thirteenth, eight. Both from you?"

"Both in the same bay, both off the same line on the sheet." She read something. "Site contents, general, as per schedule, consigned to a paper merchant, in bond. That's how it's booked. That's not a decision I made. That's a line a man in an office wrote in October and I inherited."

"Is the balance of the consignment a live claim?"

"I couldn't tell you that. I can tell you what's on the sheet. It says general, as per schedule, and there's no claim number against it and there's no hold against it, and if there were a hold on it I'd have a flag on my screen, and I don't have a flag on my screen." A keyboard again. "There's one thing in that bay that isn't general, if that's any use. There's a bound book in a polythene sleeve, and the sheet's got a pencil tick against it, and somebody in an office wrote *HOLD* on it in October in pencil, and I've left it where it is, because a pencil is not a hold and I'm not going to be the one who decides a pencil is a hold."

"Which page is the pencil on?"

"Page one, item four." The keyboard stopped. "You're the first person in a month who's asked me a question about a consignment other than *where is it*."

"I've been in the building six weeks," Elias said, "and I have got a notebook with a number of pages in it that nobody has ever asked me about, and the only thing anybody asks me about is where a thing is."

She laughed, which she had not been going to.

"Eleven forty-one," she said. "You've got that down?"

"I've got that down."

"Good. It's easier when somebody's got it down." And then, before she rang off, in the voice of a person adding a thing that is not on the sheet because she thinks somebody ought to know it: "For what it's worth, and it isn't anything, about half of these firms that ring me have never heard of the place the thing came from. That's not a criticism. It's how it works. Your collection's a number to us. It's a bay and a seal and a sheet, and when I break bulk on the eleventh I will read the seal out and a man will write it on a board, and he does that about forty times a day and he has never once opened one, and he is not going to open yours either, and if it is in that bay it is in that bay until somebody comes with paper that says otherwise."

"I understand."

"You sound like you don't," she said. "Sorry. It's not a job. You get to the end of a job."

He put the phone down and looked at what he had put in the notebook, and it was two lines and a time, and it was the first thing he had put in that notebook in three days that was not about him, and it was two lines long.

> **Tue 10 Nov, 11:41. Broomhead Reach, bonded store, q. 4 in bond since Fri 23 Oct.**
> **Wed 11 Nov, 09:40 — break bulk. Fri 13 Nov, 08:00 — paper merchant's collection. Both off one line on one sheet. Line reads: site contents, general, as per schedule.**
> **One item not general. Page 1, item 4, bound book in polythene, pencil tick, HOLD, October, pencil. A pencil is not a hold.**
> **Break bulk 11th. Merchant 13th. Nobody in this building can move either date. Not a claim. Not ours. A bay, a seal, a sheet, and a man with a board.**

Then he sat in the loading-bay office for a while with the phone off the hook, which it was, because he had not put it back, and did the thing he had started doing some time in October without noticing he was doing it, which was not counting objects any more.

He counted the rooms.

There were nine rooms he had been in this month that had other people in them: the loading bay, the loading-bay office, the security room, the front desk, the two queue rooms in the Sillick Lane branch library, the Hall of Former Faiths, the Gallery of Unnamed Hands, the systems office, and the front room at 12 Ferry Bank. Nine, and he knew all nine, and every one of them was quieter today than it had been on Monday, and there was no reason on earth for any of them to be quieter today, because it was a Tuesday, because it was half past eleven in the morning, and because a lamp had come back from a quay and gone into a cupboard and nothing else in this building had happened at all.

He noticed that he had done the counting, and he was embarrassed by the counting, and he did not stop it, and then he stopped it, on purpose, halfway through, which was new.

---

He did the dead letters after lunch. There were two hundred and seventy-six of them and it was a Tuesday, and a clerk's is a slow job, and nobody in this building can get at a man counting post that came back.

At about half past four he took the two carbons out. Not the buyer's; he has never seen the buyer's. His own, the seller's, the one in the drawer, and the Museum's own receipt of 7 November 1974, which is a different document about the same eleven items and has been in a Museum file for fifty years under a number nobody has ever read.

He did not compare them. He has never compared them. There is nothing to compare, because one of them is a receipt and the other is a carbon and both of them list the same eleven things, and he knows that, and it has been sitting in front of him for a month like a chair he has stopped seeing.

What he did instead was go and look at the eighteen-year-old instruction on the shelf in the security room, because it was the only other piece of paper in the building he was not allowed to have, and because a man at half past four on a Tuesday with nothing else to do is going to go and look at a thing.

It is a single sheet, a card, in a buff envelope, and it is headed:

> **QUIET WING — CARD ACCESS REORGANISATION. Instruction 06/QW/11. 11 October 2006.**

There is a name at the foot of it, typed, and the name is not on any list of people who have ever worked here. Not the payroll list, not the leaver list, not the old binders, not the Registrar's historic staff file, not the leavers' register in the post room, not the four C surnames in the level-two card index. Anja Ward has assumed for eighteen years that it is a contractor's signatory.

It has attachments. It has had attachments since 2006, and the attachments are in a second buff envelope stapned inside the first one, and the second envelope is marked **ATTACHMENTS — DO NOT SEPARATE**, and Elias Venn has never opened it, because opening it would put him in a room he is not permitted to be in, and because the twelve of October he worked out that the moment he asked Anja Ward about a name on an eighteen-year-old instruction, she would have to go and look, and then within a week somebody would know that a night guard had asked about the Quiet Wing instruction.

He looked at the second envelope for about a minute and a half.

He did not open it. It was half past four on a Tuesday, and there were two hundred and seventy-six dead letters on a desk behind him, and there was a merchant booked for eight o'clock on Friday, and there was a book in a polythene sleeve on page one, item four, with a pencil tick against it in a hand that had gone out of the building in October.

He went back and did the letters.

At eight o'clock he went up the hill. It was raining by Ferry Bank and not raining on the top of it, which is the way this city does it. He got the tram and sat on the wrong side of it with his notebook in his coat and the two lines in it, and he read them again, and then he read the second one, which was the one that would not let go:

> **Break bulk 11th. Merchant 13th. Nobody in this building can move either date.**

Then he did the thing he had started to do in the last week, which was to take a fact and turn it over and see what could be done with it, and there was nothing, and he had known there was nothing before he turned it over, and he turned it over anyway, because turning it over is what he is, and because it is a Tuesday, and because a man with two lines in a notebook and a clock in his head can at least work out how long he has.

Two days and a bit. He had until Friday morning at eight o'clock, on the far side of a bay, in a building he had never been inside, watched by nobody, to do anything at all about the paper in that bay, and there was no instrument in the world he had ever heard of that reached it.

Then he got off the tram and went and did the dead letters.

---

He was in the building until a quarter to eleven and the loading bay was empty and the cupboard key was back on the board with his time against it and the cage in bay 9 had a red disc on it and a padlock on it and had not been touched by anybody including him.

He had about a dozen pages left in the notebook and there is a shop on Temple Street that sells them and they are four pounds, and he thought about that on the walk up the hill, and the thought was: *the whole of this business is a document that is going to run out*, and *and on Friday morning at eight o'clock a man with a hand truck is going to put four hundredweight of a mill's office into a skip*, and *and I have got a pencil, a time and two lines, and no instrument at all.*

He was not frightened, and that was new, and he did not like it, because a man who is not frightened on a Tuesday is a man who has run out of things to be frightened about, and a man who has run out of things to be frightened about in the fourth week of November has had four weeks.

He got in at twenty to twelve and the letter was still on the shelf above the boiler in Low Ward and he did not go and look at it, and he had not gone and looked at it since the second Sunday of October, and there was a sentence he had said out loud in that room on the eighteenth of October, and it had not moved either.
