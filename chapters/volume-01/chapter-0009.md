# Chapter 0009 — The Log He Filed

Dael Ordway was standing at the service lane with a folder under his arm and his hat in his hand at ten past seven on Saturday morning, and the cleaners were coming in behind him, and he looked like a man who had been standing there since six.

"I signed in at half six," Dael said, answering a question Elias had not asked. "The systems print comes out at six and I wanted to read it before I did anything else."

"Morning."

"You were on the door."

"I was on the door."

"Right." Dael put the folder on the desk in the security room and squared it. "I'll say this once and then it's on paper, so don't make me say it twice. I am not accusing you of anything. I am signing a report, and the report is made of a maintenance history, and the maintenance history is true."

He turned it round so Elias could read it standing up. It was the morning systems print with a supervisor's summary typed across the top, and the summary was four lines long and every line of it was correct.

> 23:41 — door 7 controller offline, restricted access, Quiet Wing corridor. Fault (reader), not door event.
> 00:28 — controller answering, leaf re-armed, camera restored.
> Duration 47 min. Authorised window 23:40–00:30 (50 min), work order 4412-S, signed Director.
> Work order 4412-S **not closed**. No work recorded against the order.
> Login on order: PELL, I. (systems). System position of login during outage: not at frame.
>
> Supervisor summary: authorised outage of restricted reader exceeded no limit. The work order was not completed, and the technician's login was not at the frame at any point during the outage. The guard on duty is asked to confirm that access to the Quiet Wing corridor during the outage was not established.

Elias read it three times.

He read the first line and thought: that is exactly what happened, and I know it, and he has written it down.

He read the fourth line and thought: he has gone to the terminal and looked at the systems position of the login, and the systems do not know where anybody is, so the systems have told him PELL was not at the frame, and that is not a thing the systems can know. That is a thing a man has to go and find out, and he has written it down as a fact, and it is a lie of omission with a date on it.

He read the last line and thought: he is asking me to put my name at the end of it.

"Dael."

"44 in the service lane has been dropping for about two years, and the plant people have an open ticket that is a thousand days old, and the order that was authorised last night is not the first and it will not be the last, and the last one I have to shout about is the one that puts a *gap* in a restricted door on a building that has had one unexplained incident in eighteen years." He took his hat in his hands and turned it round. "Elias, do you know what an unexplained incident is, in the language of the insurers? It is not an incident. It is a hole. It is a place where a report should be and there isn't one. And a hole in a restricted door on a collection building is the exact shape of the thing that ends careers, and mine would go first because mine is the name on the sheet."

"How much of that is the insurers and how much is you?"

"All of it, and it's not a bad place to be." He was not angry. That was the difficulty. "I would be doing the same if it was the loading bay. If a shutter came down in the loading bay with a guard inside it, I would write the same four lines and I'd want the guard to sign it, and I'd hate myself a bit afterwards, and I'd do it again. That's the job. Somebody has to stand in front of the Board and say the building did what it said it did."

"And if somebody went down that corridor?"

"Then the sheet's got a hole in it and I have to explain a hole." Dael looked at him. "You were on the door. Was anybody in the corridor between twenty to twelve and twenty-eight past?"

The security room was very quiet. There was a folder on the desk. There was a book on the table by the tray in the next room with Elias Venn's name in it in two columns and a duty manager's initials beside the 02:41, a drawer away, and Dael Ordway, who signed out that book and read it every month, had not mentioned it, and neither had Elias.

Elias put his hand flat on the desk and thought about a card with a name under the ink on it, and a chair with no dust on the seat, and four photographs on a telephone, and a corridor that had been ninety feet long.

"No," he said. "Nobody was in the corridor."

Dael wrote *confirmed by guard on duty, E. Venn* and turned the folder round and gave him the pen.

Elias Venn read a false cause, and understood exactly which four words of it were false, and signed his name at the bottom of it in his own hand.

---

"You're not rostered till seven tonight."

"I know."

"Then go home. I'll take it from here." Dael put the folder under his arm. "Oh, and it's cancelled. The Saturday school group. Eleven o'clock, forty-one children, two teachers, and a Hill office that sends a girl to every single one of these. I'm pulling it."

"On what grounds?"

"On a maintenance notice, which is the truth, because the fault on forty-four is real and the fault on seven is real and I cannot open a Hall of Former Faiths to forty-one children while I have an unexplained outage on a restricted door in the same building and a re-inspection on plinth thirty-four at nine on Monday morning with one of our conservators opening it and a Registry observer sitting in the front row taking minutes." He was already writing the note. "Which means I ring the Hill office at nine and I tell them a mechanical fault and no danger to visitors, which is true, and I write a letter to the school, and I will personally answer two emails about whether Tuesday works instead. And on Monday a woman from the Accord Registry is going to open that case with a screwdriver and I am going to have to stand next to her while she does it and say the words *nothing has changed* out loud."

"That'll be honest."

"It will be a performance," Dael said, "and I shall give it well, and none of that is your business, and I am telling you it anyway because you are the only person in this building who reads anything." He looked at the folder. "Go home, Elias. You look like a man who's been in a war."

At the door he stopped.

"One more thing and then I'll let you go. The west riser is a known problem. It has been since February and it is on the list, and if it goes tonight then the sub-level gets wet and the facilities lads will pull the damp boxes out of B onto pallets in the bay, because that is what you do with wet cardboard at one in the morning. Don't go down there to help them. There is nothing down there you can help with and I want you nowhere near the lift on Saturday."

"Understood."

"And Elias." Dael put his hat on. "The plinth thirty-four card. There's no file for it and I know there's no file for it, because I looked for one in the March and the Registrar sent me a list of two thousand numbers with no names against them and told me the index was being rebuilt. In the meantime, if anybody asks you what's on thirty-four, the answer is *a bell from a mill*, and if anybody asks what the number means, the answer is *I don't know, nobody's told me*. That's not a lie. That's a man declining to guess in writing."

---

He stopped in the service lane first, on his way up, and put the telephone back in slot twelve and signed the book beside it. He wrote his name, and *back 08:20*, and nothing else, because there was no column for what it had been out for, and a half-entry in a book is a thing that stands up on its own and can be looked at by anybody who ever wants to.

Then he got as far as the third landing.

The stair landing between the second and third floors, by the pipework, with no window and no camera, was where he stopped at 09:31 on Saturday morning and took out his mother's number and stood in the cold and rang it.

The laundrette shuts at ten on a Saturday and it took her about forty seconds to answer.

"Already?"

"I'm on the stairs."

"You've been on the stairs since seven. I can hear the echo." A pause, and a shop being set up. "You ate?"

"Yes."

"That's not eating, that's answering." She let it go, because she always let it go after one go. "Your father's—"

"I know."

"He was going to send you something. For your birthday, and then it was your birthday the year after, and now it's a Tuesday in the autumn and you still haven't asked me for it."

Elias stood on a concrete landing two floors above an empty gallery and looked at the pipe going into the wall.

"I'm fine," he said.

"You are not, but that's all right." She was brisk. "Are your hours normal? You've been on nights a long while."

"They're normal."

"Because the girl at work said she saw you on Thursday morning in the street and you looked like you'd been up all night, and I said, he works nights, Mo, and she said, I know he works nights, I said it to him at half eight in the morning."

He closed his eyes. "There was a fault. It's been sorted."

"Elias." His mother's voice changed and went slower, which was worse than shouting. "Eighteen years I've had that letter. I have never once had to lie to you about it, because you have never once asked. And I have thought about that a great deal, and I have decided that is my business and not yours, and I am not going to bring it up when you're tired."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Ask me when you want it." A breath. "There's nothing in the world you can't tell me, love. Whatever it is, whatever it is about, whatever you've done or not done. I have never wanted a version of you that comes to me cleaned up."

"I know," Elias said.

And he stood on the landing and let it be the end of the call, and it was, and the three sentences he had said on it were all true and the one that had not been said had been sitting in the middle of them the whole time, and the line was true the way the maintenance record was true, and he had signed it, and his mother's voice went out of his ear and the stairwell was silent.

---

Ivo's workshop was a cupboard under the loading bay stairs with a bench in it, a vice, a pillar drill with a belt-driven motor, and a wall of small drawers that made the label room look like a library. Ivo was in there at ten past ten on a Saturday morning, on a Saturday he was not rostered for, on his own time, having come in on the Friday controller and been sent home at five minutes past midnight.

"You're not on today."

"I'm not on today." Ivo had a piece of painted sheet steel on the bench, about a metre square, and had already cut it and folded the corners over. "Cover. It goes over the case, it doesn't touch the plinth, and the clamps hold it to the case surround so if anybody comes in they can see at a glance what's happening and why. Do you know why that matters?"

"Because it's a Saturday and there's a school group."

"Because it's a Saturday and there's a school group," Ivo agreed. "Ivo Pell, junior guard, in the Hall, with a sheet of steel and a bag of ash, at half nine in the morning, in front of a woman from a donor's office. If I do it in a room with a door that shuts I have to explain it to one person. If I do it in the Hall I have to explain it to forty-one children and their teachers and the press in due course." He held up a clamp. "Bought these Thursday. Stole the two good ones out of the display stock, which I am not telling you about, which is the sort of thing that gets a person talked about in a canteen."

"Have you eaten?"

"I've had a thing that was a pastry." He put the clamp down. "Elias. Sit down. You look like the ground's been taken out from under you and I want to do this bit with you sitting down, because the bit I'm about to do is going to be about you and not about the cover."

Elias sat down on an upturned crate.

"Salt and ash," said Ivo. "One part salt, one part ash, and the ash has to be off something you burned yourself, and I did it in a tray on Thursday night in my own flat and I have not yet told anybody that, so put it in the notebook and not anywhere else." He had a jar of it on the bench, grey and lumpy. "You spread it on a card, flat, thin, and you write the question on the card. Not because a question on a card does anything. Because the card gives the— because it gives you somewhere to stop. It's not a shield. If somebody sold you one of these off the internet for a hundred quid it would be a piece of card with ash on it, and it would do exactly what this does, which is nothing, but it marks a place, and you have a place to stop, and a person with a place to stop can decide to stop."

"How long does it hold?"

"It doesn't hold. It shows." He tapped the jar. "It'll show a pull. Somewhere in the room, something will bend, and the ash goes first, and then you go. That's it. That's the whole invention. It's a line in the sand that you made yourself, in a building where nobody has drawn a line in the sand for you since 1961, and you take it away when you go, and you throw it away. It's rubbish. It's a thing to do so that stopping has a shape."

The cover took him most of an hour. They worked on it and did not talk about the twenty minutes.

At half eleven Elias got the notebook out and wrote on a page he tore off, five lines, and put it on the bench flat between the vice and the jar so they could both read it.

> ONE QUESTION. ONE ONLY. WRITTEN DOWN BEFORE.
> TIME LIMIT. WRITTEN DOWN BEFORE.
> THE COST. NAMED OUT LOUD BEFORE, TO A WITNESS.
> A WITNESS WHO CAN SAY THE WORD.
> THE WAY OUT, AND WE USE IT.

"Five lines," Ivo said. "That's it. That's a safety card, that. Nothing on it about what you're going to find. Nothing on it about the object. It's a card about you, and it is the most sensible piece of paper I have ever seen in this building." He looked at it a long moment. "Right. Stop word. You need a word. It can't be *stop* because half this building shouts *stop* at a school group. It can't be your name because if you go down and I have to say your name to a woman from a donor's office—"

"Fault."

"Fault?" Ivo turned it over. "Fault. Yeah. Fault. It's the word the building's got. The panel says it, the ticket says it, the engineers say it, it means *this has gone wrong and I am not the one who did it* and it means *you are leaving now* and it means *do not open this to ask me why*. And nobody in a school group is going to be frightened by the word fault, because the word fault is on the label of the broken lift in the north lobby and the children walk past it every week." He nodded slowly. "Yeah. Fault. If I say fault, you're out, you don't negotiate, you don't ask me a question first, you don't go back for the notebook. Out."

They made two anchor cards. It took twenty minutes and it was filthy work and they did it in the workshop sink, and Elias wrote a question on the first one in pencil and then rubbed it out, and then wrote it again, and it took him a moment to decide whether to write it down before he knew whether he was allowed to ask it, and he decided that the whole of the point of the line was that you write it before.

The clock on the workshop wall said twenty past twelve.

"I've got to be on at seven for the swap and I haven't slept," said Ivo. "And while you're standing there doing the thing with your face: what are you writing down."

Elias had not realised he was doing it. He had the notebook out and he was writing down the times, and the temperatures, and the state of the seal, and the door, and Ivo was watching him do it.

"Write it up twice," Ivo said. "One in the book and one in that."

"That's sensible—"

"No, listen." He turned round on the crate. "Elias. You'll write the nice one for the book, won't you. You'll write it up tidy, with the times in the right column, and you won't put anything in it that makes anybody ask you a question. And then you'll write the other one in the notebook with all the ugly bits in, the bit where it hurt, the bit where you lost track, the bit that if anybody read it you'd have to explain why you were in a corridor."

"That is the point of—"

"I know it's the point. You've told me the point about four times this week and I'm with you, I am, I've built you a cover out of a bin and I've built you a card out of my own fire, I'm not against you." Ivo's voice came up, and then went down again, because he was twenty-five. "The point is you keep the other one so it's *yours*. And mine doesn't go in a book, so when somebody comes and asks me what happened, and somebody will, Dael will, I don't have anything to be holding on to. I've got a bin and I've got a fault on a panel. You've got the truth. And that means that if it ever goes wrong — and it will go wrong, you know it will, I've watched you all week — you can decide later what the truth was. You can have a good week and a bad week, because there's a copy in a drawer. And I can't."

He took the notebook out of Elias's hands, held it up, and handed it straight back.

"So write it twice," he said, "and leave it in the book where the front desk can get at it, and don't come to me at two in the morning and say that you couldn't tell me a number, because I'll be the one who has to stand there and be the man who didn't ask."

Mara Iles was on the conservation floor at twenty to one on a Saturday afternoon because it was the only day in the week she had told her own staff she would be in, and because a registrar who is not there is a registrar who is not asked anything.

He showed her the five lines first, before he explained anything, because he had worked out on the tram that morning that this was the only version of the conversation in which she was doing her job.

She read it twice. "Who wrote this?"

"Both of us."

"You wrote the question on the bottom."

"The question is the point. It's the only line that's—"

"It's not one of your five lines. It's on your hand." She put the page down on the bench, squared to the edge, which he noticed. "Elias. I have been in this building five years and I have watched two directors put three objects into stores in five years and I have watched one of them come out of the store as a case in a gallery with a wrong date on it, and every single one of those decisions was made in a room with a door that shut. So I want to be extremely clear about what I am agreeing to and what I am not."

"Go on."

"You do this in the Hall, on a night with the building full, with the door open. Not in a store. Not in a treatment room at three in the morning with a blanket over your head. If you are going to stand in front of that case and put your hands on it, you do it where I can walk in and put my hand on your shoulder, and where there are two people between you and the next room, and where if you go down I can get four witnesses onto this floor inside ninety seconds. And the west range circuit is on a fault and I have written it up twice, and on Saturday the building's own air is on emergency, so if that circuit drops while you are doing it, I am not having you alone in this building with whatever it is. That's not a condition. That's the whole of what I am agreeing to." She stopped. "Two conditions. Do you want them or shall I keep going?"

"Give me the conditions."

"First. The result goes in the shared log, in my hand, exactly as it comes. Not summarised, not tidied, not 'a result'. Whatever comes out of the object, in the words it comes out in, I write it down and I sign it, and it goes in the file, and it stays in the file. That is the entire reason I exist in this building and I will not have somebody's private certainty living in a coat pocket because it was a bit odd at four in the morning." She held up one finger. "Second. You tell me everything, every time. Not the parts you've decided are relevant. Not the parts that would put you in a corridor. Every time. If I have to ask you what happened and you have to stop and think about how much of it I am allowed to know, then we stop, and I do not come back."

"Yes."

"You have not asked what the third thing is."

"What third thing?"

"There is no third thing," Mara said, "and that is the third thing. People who come in here with a locked door always have a third thing. I've had two of them. Both of them were men who were certain they were being careful." She put her hand flat on the page. "You're not one of those, as far as I can see, and that is not a compliment, because as far as I can see you are a man who has been quietly building a second set of books for a week and has not once asked me whether he is allowed to."

Elias did not answer that.

He had the notebook out. He wrote down the two conditions, and the date, and the time, and he wrote them in full and exactly as she had said them, and then he sat there with the pen resting on the paper and read back eighteen years of a man who had believed that if you wrote everything down properly and kept the copy in your own pocket, then nobody ever got hurt, and understood that he had just promised two women and a technician to do the opposite, out loud, in writing, to people who could not be trusted with it and would have to be trusted anyway.

"Saturday," he said. "Tonight. Before Monday, because on Monday morning a woman from the Registry opens that case with a screwdriver and I have no standing in the room at all."

"That is the first sensible thing anybody in this building has said about thirty-four all week." She took the page and folded it once and put it in her coat. "And the west range circuit is still on a fault, and the school group was cancelled at nine o'clock this morning, so if you were planning to be alone in this building tonight, this is the part where I find out."

"Saturday night is the long night," Elias said. "Cleaners from nine, conservation to eleven, facilities on for the sluice, the duty manager's on the freight lift. Ivo's coming in on a favour. It's the only night of the week I can do this with a door that anybody can open."

Mara Iles gave him a look that lasted exactly as long as it needed to.

"Ten minutes," she said. "Write that down as well, and not one minute more than ten, because I have read what a man in a bad night does to a clock and I am not doing it again in this building. And Elias—"

"Yes."

"You have understood what you have just promised me?"

Elias Venn looked at the bench, at the five lines, at the cover and the clamps and the jar of salt and ash, and at his own handwriting in a notebook that had more in it than it had had a week ago, and that the more was the price.

"Yes," he said. "I've understood it."

---

He went back on at eight and did the round. He did the Hall. He put the light on plinth thirty-four and stood in front of it and did not touch it and did not go near it, because he had made a promise on a landing in the middle of the morning about how it was to be done, and he was not going to start by cheating the first hour.

At 20:50 on Saturday night the building filled up around him, the way it always did on the one night of the week everybody was in it, and Elias went down to the workshop to get the cover, and Ivo was already there, and the clamps were in a bag, and the anchor card was in an envelope, and somewhere on the first floor a conservator was working late with the door open because it was the only evening she had.
