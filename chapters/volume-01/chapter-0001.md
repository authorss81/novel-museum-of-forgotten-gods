# Chapter 0001 — Night Round

The Hall of Former Faiths door had a habit, and Tuesday was the night it gave in to it.

Elias had learned to hear it coming. The latch pulled in, the bolt came home, and then nothing — no click, no second movement, just the door standing a half-inch proud of its frame while the building breathed its warm night breath around his ankles. He stood in the opening with his torch tucked under his arm and listened to a fire door try and fail to become a door.

Behind him the galleries ran away into the dark in their long rows of plinths. Ahead, the west range kept giving the boiler its bad recirculated breath. He did the thing he had done a hundred times, which was to stop treating a door like a mystery and start treating it like a mechanism.

The strike plate was the trouble. He crouched and put the flat of the screwdriver against it and levered, gently, and the plate came off far enough to show him the problem: two screws, one of them turned so far into soft old plaster that it had nothing to bite on, and the plate standing a half-millimetre proud of the frame so the bolt rode over it instead of into it. Underneath the plate the paint had gone to a crust, thirty years of institutional cream, and the bolt had a shadow of a groove in it that was not the shape of the frame.

He worked the paint out with the blade, corner by corner, and scraped the crust onto his thumbnail, and then he countersunk the loose screw a full turn and a half into fresh wood and set the plate back flush. He wiped the frame with the rag from his belt, because the rag was on his belt for exactly this, and pulled the door to.

It clicked. He pulled it again to be sure, and it clicked.

Nineteen fifty-five, a quarter of an hour after he had signed on. He wrote it in the security log as *FoF door, strike plate re-set, screw into sound timber* and added the time, because a fault with no time on it is a fault with no end on it.

---

He had come on at nineteen forty. In the security room the terminal was still humming through its night log, and the building felt around him the way it always did after seven: a working building, not a haunted one. Pipes ticking. The conservation floor's extraction fans going through their long complaint one floor up. Behind Civic Hill, a tram, going away.

The rota was on the board by the kettle, printed, laminated, gone slightly cloudy at the pins.

> NIGHT DUTY — 19:00 – 07:00
> VENN, E. — TUE to SAT
> PELL, I. — systems 07:00–15:00 TUE to SAT, junior guard TUE/WED
> SYSTEMS NIGHT: PELL, I. — THU
> Two-person rule, sub-level B freight lift, no exceptions.

Five nights on, two off, and a rota that stacked them badly if you were Ivo, who had a systems day under every one of his guard nights and a systems night at the end of the week, and who had started coming on shift with the specific quiet of a man who had been awake since Tuesday.

The rest of the house rules were on the same board in the same printing, and one of them had a tray under it. Every personal phone in the building went into a grey steel cupboard at the service entrance, unlocked, in numbered slots, and went out again into a paper book with a column for a name and two for times. He had been signing that book two years and had never once read the line printed across the head of it, and the line was about photographs, and the tray had two hundred and six slots in it, and there was never any reason to look at which one was yours.

The notice board above it had three things on it. A fire drill rota nobody had read since the spring. A reminder about the donors' gallery photography ban. And a River Works maintenance notice, printed on their letterhead, dated Monday, which somebody had pinned at an angle.

Elias read it standing up.

> MORROWGATE DISTRICT WORKS — RIVER WARD
> NOTICE 3/BB-11
> Third ward-bell, South Sluice station: bell re-cracked 21:40 Monday. Bell struck on site; note off-normal; not cleared for service. District works attending. Registry countersigned. Bell to remain in position pending assessment.

Below it, in biro, somebody had written *bell 3, still ringing* and initialled it. The initials were a shop supervisor's shorthand, a single flat loop. Elias had seen that initial a hundred times on work orders.

Four hundred metres and a river away, a civic bell with a crack in it was striking a note that was not its note, and the building had a ready-made explanation for any other noise in the week. He read that the way he read everything on that board, as one more piece of the Museum's ordinary burden: somebody upstream had a problem, and the paperwork had come down the hill to tell us about it, and now if anybody on this floor heard a strange noise they would have somewhere to put it.

He was aware, distantly, that this was the kind of thinking that made people comfortable and wrong. He let it go by. It was 19:44 and he had a building to walk.

The systems queue on the terminal had picked up the River Works notice overnight and filed it in the Museum's own log, which was how everything about this building worked: somebody else noticed something, and the Museum's terminal made a note of it, and the note arrived at twenty to five in the morning on a machine in an empty room. That was 04:12 Tuesday. There was a line on it and nobody had read it but him.

Below that, the maintenance request queue. Six open items, all of them somebody else's problem in a good way: two lamps on the west range, a leak under the basin in the paper room that was being mopped rather than fixed, a request for a spare bulb the size of a fist. He read them, the way he read them every night, in case one of them had quietly become the sort of thing that would be a problem by six in the morning.

Then he took his torch out of its drawer — the switch needed a second press, always had, since the spring — and a flask of tea, and a rag, and went out to do the floor.

---

Ivo was at the base of the stairs, doing the ground floor with a torch and a phone in his teeth, and he had a way of walking that made no sound at all, which was the most irritating thing about him.

"Forty-four open," Elias said.

"Forty-four." Ivo took the phone out of his mouth. "Door?"

"FoF. Strike plate." He held up the screwdriver. "It was riding."

"You fixed it with a screwdriver."

"It's a strike plate."

"It's a — Elias, we have a *maintenance department*, they have a thing, it's a whole—"

"You've got a systems night this week somewhere."

"Thursday," Ivo said. "Systems day Wednesday, systems night Thursday, systems day Friday, then I'm back on the floor Friday night and the door 7 module goes in—" He stopped, rubbed the back of his neck, and gave up on the sentence, which was a mercy. "You'll do the Hall, I'll do the public side and the perimeter. Don't take the stairs too fast, the third one's off."

"Understood."

"And Elias." Ivo was already walking. "If the ward-bell notice is still on that board, take it down."

"Why?"

"Because in three weeks somebody's going to hear something and point at that piece of paper and then you and me will spend a fortnight explaining that a bell in a sluice has nothing to do with anything in this building." He put the phone back in his teeth. "Pigeonhole is how buildings get closed."

Elias thought about that on and off for the rest of the night.

---

The Hall of Former Faiths was the room the public actually came for, and on a school-morning week it was the reason the Museum had a roof. Thirty-one plinths down the centre, glass cases, low warm light set into the coving, and a long printed card at the door with the house rule in a plain sans serif:

> **THE HALL OF FORMER FAITHS — DO NOT TOUCH EXHIBITS.**
> Conservation of these objects is not possible if they are handled. Please use your eyes.
> Thank you for protecting what cannot be replaced.

He liked the card. It was honest about the thing it was about.

The middle cases held the small saints of a working city: a threshold stone with a worn hollow for a foot, a ferryman's tally board, a shrine lamp that had not been lit in sixty years and was not supposed to be, a case of trade tools with their owner's initials burnt into the handles. Every label in the room was careful. Every label in the room was also, in the way Elias had learned to notice over two years and never been able to explain to anyone, a sentence that had been *arranged*. Not wrong. Arranged. A name with the trouble taken out of it.

Plinth thirty-four was a small bronze bell under a low case, about the size of a man's fist, worn pale on the shoulder where a thumb would go. The casting seam ran up one side and had been left proud. The lip was hollowed in a curve, not evenly — deeper on the left, which meant a hand had held it there for years. The clapper was original and undamaged. There was a staple through the crack at the shoulder, bright and modern against the old metal, a repair done by somebody who had wanted the bell to keep working rather than to be right.

The label said:

> **TALLY BELL**
> Cast 1902. Bronze.
> Recovered on the closure of a textile works, Low Ward. Used to call the shift to work.
> Acq. 4471 / B.

*Municipal Works, unremarkable industrial object, retained for completeness of the Low Ward series.*

Elias read it the way he read every label, which was twice. The first time for the words. The second time for how the words were standing.

He put his torch on the case seal. Intact. He checked the plinth, the case, the run of the case's own shadow on the floor, the two sensors under the case lip, the little green telltale at the base. All correct. He moved on down the row, and at the end of the row he stood for a moment and looked back at thirty-four, because it was a nice object and he liked it, and there was no reason to like it, and he did anyway.

---

The rest of the round was the rest of the round. He checked the loading bay, where the ground-floor dock off the freight elevator held a row of overnight returns and one delivery of framed prints still wrapped. He checked the Gallery of Unnamed Hands, which was darker and colder and which he had never once had a reason to enter. He checked the conservation floor, walking fast and holding his breath out of politeness, because the extraction fans in there moved so much air that you could not hear a radio from the doorway.

At the foot of the maintenance stair, the floor had a stain the shape of the Low Ward water table, which came up under the sub-level in a wet week and left a mark by the stair each autumn until the heating dried it. He had noted it on every previous night with the same note, and it was the same stain, and he noted it again, and there was something he found restful about a fault that was simply a fault.

The loading bay desk phone rang at midnight and he took it in the security room.

"Security." He said the word the way it was supposed to be said, and then a voice on the other end said it was a fault, and Elias said, "Which fault," and the voice said, "No — the fault, the reported fault, the one that's been reported," in a tone of great patience, and Elias wrote *caller, no substance* and put the receiver down and felt briefly, honestly proud of the building and everybody in it.

At 02:44 he came back up through the west range with the Hall in front of him and the whole museum behind him, dark, and the tram had stopped running, and there was nothing at all to do for another half hour.

At 02:52 the bell on plinth thirty-four gave one note.

---

He did not run. He wants to say that afterwards, and it is true: he did not run, because there was nothing to run toward. A noise in a closed building is a noise. It is the oldest thing he knew about his job, and the whole of the craft is refusing to turn a sound into an event before you have seen the event.

So he came down the row at his ordinary pace, and he did not turn the torch on, because a torch would have spoiled his night vision, and the hall's own cove lighting was more than enough to see by.

The note had come out of the metal, not out of a strike. That was the thing his ears told him before anything else, and it was the thing he wrote down later: no clapper movement, no acceleration, no decay. The sound had simply been inside the bell, the way a tapped rim is inside a rim.

He stood in front of the case. He checked the seal, and the plinth, and the floor around the plinth, and the window, which was shuttered from inside like all the public-gallery windows after twenty-two hundred. He put his hand on the case, flat, the way you check a case for movement. It was as cold as the rest of the room.

He looked at the door. The door he had fixed. He looked at the run of the door's shadow on the floor, which was the only thing in the room that had moved since he came in.

Then he went back to the security room, and he opened the building log, and he read the column heads — *time / location / observation / action* — and he typed in what he had actually seen.

> 02:52 — Hall of Former Faiths, plinth 34. Single bell note from the object in the case, no strike, no visible movement, case seal intact, no entry to room. Ambient 15°C. Logged as building noise observation. No fault found.

He read it twice. It was a complete entry. It was accurate in every particular. It gave Dael exactly what Dael would want and asked for nothing, and it had a time on it, and it would end the question the way a question should end in a building with a Board and an insurer and a school group booked for Saturday morning.

He signed it E. Venn and closed the book.

Then he sat for a while with the tea going cold beside the keyboard, and the building ticked and complained around him, and the unease came up through the floor of him the way the damp came up under the sub-level: not because anything was wrong but because something was not yet known to be right. It was a small unease, entirely reasonable. A building makes noises. He had a theory, and the theory was Monday's notice on the board, and the theory was good, and he let it stand because a good theory is worth more than a bad question at four in the morning.

Somewhere under it, the way the stain sits under the stairs whether or not you look at it, was the fact that a bell had rung in a hall where no one was working, and no bell in this building rings because a bell is there. He put a name on the feeling the way you put a name on a smell. Building noise. Settling. He'd check the door again next week, and the plate would be fine, and he would feel ordinary.

He thought about the letter.

He thought about it the way he thought about it most nights, which was as a shape rather than a fact: his mother's white envelope on the shelf above the boiler at the laundrette, with his father's name on it in his mother's hand and the paper gone soft along the fold from being taken out and put back so many times the fold had learned to hold itself. Eighteen years. He had never seen inside it. She had never offered and he had never asked, and the not-asking had become a shape in his life that he was careful not to look at directly, the way you are careful not to put your hand into a machine you have not switched off.

He took the flask of tea, and drank it cold, and did not do anything about it.

At 03:10 he walked the Hall one more time because the log said he had, and the bell on plinth thirty-four was dark and still and green at the base where the sensor told it, and the label said *TALLY BELL, cast 1902, unremarkable*, and he wrote the time at the top of the next page and closed the book.

Forty-one children and two teachers were booked into that room at eleven on Saturday morning, and there were three more nights of this rota to get through before then, and the sound was in the log now, in the Museum's own hand, with a time on it, and a sound with a time on it does not stay a sound. It becomes a record. And records are what the building keeps.

Elias turned the terminal off at ten past three, with the best part of four hours of the shift still in front of him, and sat out the rest of it as quietly as a man can sit out the best part of four hours.
