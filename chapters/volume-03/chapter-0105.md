# Chapter 0105 — A Reach, A Rope, And A Number

**On Wednesday the ninth of June 2025 a man of thirty-two, a man of twenty-five and a man of about fifty-five drove nine miles upriver to a lock house at Braeswick Lower Lock, and a man of about sixty-one took a coil of rope off a nail and put it on a table in a working room, and it was the first time in twenty-seven weeks that anything in this case had been described by the person who used it.**

**It is about eleven metres of tarred manila, the colour of a wet brick, with a splice at about four metres, a lead line, a canvas tag with a parish name and a year on it, and knots at intervals that are not at intervals. It is not read in this chapter. It is not carded, and it is not accessioned, and it is not going into a case, and no chapter is permitted to card it. The one thing this chapter does is a piece of arithmetic, and the arithmetic is that the knots are not random, and the man of fifty-five who did it is right that the two numbers on his sheet do not agree, and he will say which of them he trusts and why, and he will not say the sentence, and nobody in that room is going to make him.**

---

## And it is a nine-mile drive and there is no reason on earth for any of the three of them to be there, and two of them give a reason and the third gives none

Gwilym Petherick was fifty-five or fifty-six depending on which of his two birthday forms you believed and had been a river engineer for thirty-one years and had a van, a folding rule, a staff gauge about four feet long that lived in the back of the van, and a printed table in a plastic wallet that had been laminated in about 2016 and had two columns of numbers on it.

"**I am going up there on Wednesday because the reach board at Braeswick wants re-hanging and I am the only person in this county who is paid to care about it, and I have been there in March and I did not like the state of the plate.**"

"**And I am going because the loading bay invert at the Museum is about nine inches below the drawing,**" said Ivo Pell, "**and it is not nine inches below, it is about fourteen, and the drawing is from 1974, and if there is a flood in this city that goes anywhere near Civic Hill the water comes in the bay and not down the gutter, and I would like to know how a bay drains before anybody tells the Board it does.**"

"**And I have got no reason at all,**" said Elias Venn, **and did not give one, and Ivo Pell looked at him for about two seconds on the ring road and then said, **"**That's the first honest thing anybody has said to me since March and I am not going to ask you the rest of it.**"

---

## And the reach board is not a mystery and it is a wall with a scale painted on it, and it is at Braeswick because that is where it has been

It was outside the lock house, on the river face, above the door, and it was about a metre and a half of painted board in a frame: a white ground, black graduations, a figure in black and white at every half metre, and at the top a line and the words **REACH BOARD — BRAESWICK LOWER** and under that, in a different paint, older, **1911**.

"**It is not a gauge,**" said Emrys Tasker. He was about sixty-one and had been at that lock house since 1988 and before that at the one above it, and he had a kettle on and did not offer anybody anything until he had said what he came out to say. "**A gauge is a thing the water comes up to. That is a ruler. You read it with your eyes and you write it on a pad and you take the pad away, and if you lose the pad the year is gone. There is a gauge on the pier about two hundred yards down and it is the water authority's and it is the one you want if you want a number anybody else will take.**"

"**Then why hang this one.**"

"**Because it is here, and it has been here since 1911, and because you cannot telephone 1911.**" He took his hand off the frame. "**And because in about 1991 they put a plastic one on the pier and took the money for it and it is still going wrong, and I have never once seen anybody from that office come and look at this one.**"

---

## And the rope is the tie-line, and a tie-line is the rope that the board hangs from, and it comes out of a dry store in a sack and it goes on a table

It came out of a sack on a shelf in a dry store behind the lock house, and it came out in one movement the way a thing comes out that has been taken out about nine times, and it was laid out on the table in a shape that was not a coil.

"**Eleven metres,**" said Petherick. "**Give or take. Manilla, about three-quarters of an inch, tarred when it was new. The splice is at about four metres and the splice is a proper one — somebody shortened it and somebody else did the work and did it right. There is a lead line at the head of it, which is for sounding, and it is still on, which means somebody wants to use this again.**"

"**I do,**" said Tasker.

"**And the tag.**"

Tasker turned the head of the rope over and there was a canvas tag, a strip about four inches by two, machine-stitched at one end, and the stitching was rusted and the canvas was stiff, and on it in stencil, half of it worn away:

> **BRAESWICK**
> **1968**

"**That is the parish,**" said Tasker, "**and 1968 is either the year it was made or the year the board was hung, and I have never known which, and I asked the man from the water authority about eleven years ago and he said it did not matter and he was not entirely right.**"

"**It matters enormously,**" said Petherick. "**The board is 1911. If 1968 is the board then the rope is thirty-nine years younger than the thing it hangs. If 1968 is the rope then somebody made a tie-line in 1968 for a board that was fifty-seven years old, which is ordinary, and if 1968 is the board then somebody rehung a 1911 board in 1968 and that is a record in somebody's day book about a hundred miles away.**"

"**Which is it.**"

"**I do not know. I am telling you that I do not know, and that the two of us have now agreed on the fact and disagreed on the reason, and that this is what happens to a manila tag in fifty years.**"

---

## And there are eleven knots, and they are not at intervals, and a man of thirty-two counted them twice and was embarrassed both times and said so out loud

They were in the tie-line between the splice and the head, and the spacing between them was not a spacing, and he counted them and counted them again and got eleven both times and then apologised to the room for counting.

"**That is not a confession,**" said Tasker. "**I have counted them about four hundred times. It is the only interesting thing on this riverbank.**"

"**Who tied them.**"

"**I did.**"

Nobody had asked that and it came out flat, in about two seconds, with no weight on it at all, and it was the plainest thing anybody had said in three days.

"**Every season at the start of it,**" said Emrys Tasker. "**I get a ladder out and I put the board back up and I look at where the water got to, and I tie a knot in the line to mark it, and I take the line down and I put it in the sack and it goes in the sack until the next season. That is all it is. It is a mark. You are not going to get anything else out of me and I have not got anything else and I have never told anybody anything else about it, because there is nothing else.**"

"**And how do you know where the water got to.**"

"**By the board. The board is a ruler and I read it with my eyes and I have read it every season since 1988 and I write the number on a pad in a drawer in that house and the pad is a pad and it is not a record and it is not kept.**" He put two fingers on the table an inch from the rope. "**And then I do not have to remember the number. That is the whole point of tying a knot. You are not writing it down. You are putting it somewhere you can get at with a cold hand and no light.**"

---

## And the chapter's only piece of real work is that a man of fifty-five laid a rope against a wall and showed that nine numbers were a series and not a set

He did it on the outside table because it was the only flat thing, and it took about an hour, and nobody helped him and nobody talked much.

He laid the rope out straight along the wall under the board with the knots up, and he read each knot against the painted graduations with his folding rule, and he wrote on a sheet of A4 in biro: a number, in centimetres, above the sill of the door.

> **Knot 1 — 44 cm · Knot 2 — 96 · Knot 3 — 71 · Knot 4 — 121 · Knot 5 — 58 · Knot 6 — 133 · Knot 7 — 89 · Knot 8 — 105 · Knot 9 — 36 · Knot 10 — 60 · Knot 11 — 112**

"**There are nine of those that are marks,**" he said, "**and there are two that are not, and I have known which two since about 1994 and they are the whipping and the seizing on the third and the eighth, and I would not have known if I had not been looking for it, and the man who tied them did not know either.**"

"**And the nine.**"

"**Sort them.**"

He sorted them: 36, 44, 58, 60, 71, 89, 96, 105, 112, 121, 133 — eleven numbers, nine of which are marks.

"**They are not random,**" he said. "**I have laid this rope out on three other walls since 1998 and I have read the same nine numbers every time to within a centimetre, and here is the test and it is the only test there is.**"

"**Which is.**"

"**Whether the numbers are the numbers the reach has seen.**" He put a finger on the board. "**That is a flood level. It is on the plate. It is not in my table, it is on a board that has been outside since 1911 with a line painted at the top of it. I can compare a knot with a line on that board the way you would compare a ruler with a ruler. And every one of those nine is within a centimetre or two of a line on that board, and there is no reason at all why nine knots in a rope should land on nine painted graduations, and there is one very good reason why they should, and I am not going to say the good reason out loud in a lock yard because I do not like sentences like that.**"

"**Say it in your own words.**"

"**I have said it in my own words.**"

He wrote one more line under the eleven numbers and underlined it.

> **These are heights. They are not levels.**

"**A height is a fact about a rope,**" he said. "**It is how far a piece of cord is above a doorstep. A level is a fact about the river. To go from one to the other you have to know what the doorstep is, in the system the river is measured in, and that is a datum, and a datum is a number that somebody agreed on, and if you do not know which number was agreed on then the nine numbers on that sheet are a list of how far nine knots are from a door and nothing else at all. Not wrong. Useless. A different thing.**"

---

## And the table in his wallet has two columns, and the two columns do not agree, and the difference is not a mistake and is not going to be fixed

He took the plastic wallet out and put the laminated sheet on the table next to the rope. It was headed in a small printed hand, and the print was about 2016, and there were two tables on it and a note at the foot.

> **BRAESWICK REACH — REDUCTION TO DATUM**
> **Table A: heights above Newlyn.** Table B: heights above gauge zero, Braeswick pier gauge.
> **NOTE: the relationship between Table A and Table B at this reach is not constant. Do not convert one to the other by adding or subtracting a fixed figure. See note 4.**

"**Read note four,**" said Petherick.

> **Note 4. The offset between a datum on the sea and a zero on a river gauge changes as the bed between them changes. Over the period 1998 to 2016 the offset at this reach moved by about 140 millimetres. Figures printed before 1998 should not be converted using figures printed after it.**

"**So they do not agree.**"

"**They do not agree and they have never agreed and the reason they do not agree is a hundred and forty millimetres of riverbed that has moved since 1998, and neither of those two numbers is anybody's fault and neither of them is going to be repaired.**" He squared the sheet. "**And here is what I am not going to do, and I have thought about it for two days and I have decided to say it out loud in a lock yard instead of not saying it at all.**"

"**Go on.**"

"**I have got a table that converts a height at this lock to a level at this reach. There is no other one in this county. I made it for myself out of about nine years of gauge readings, and it is good, and it is about two pages, and if I give it to anybody it becomes a standard, and then in about four years somebody in an office will use it on a reach four miles away where the bed is different, and they will not know that, and they will print a number.**"

"**And if you do not give it to anybody.**"

"**Then the only numbers in this country for the height of the water at Braeswick are on a board, and a gauge, and a laminated sheet in my own wallet.**" He picked it up. "**Which of the two do I trust. You have asked me the right question for once so I am going to give you the answer and then I am going to stop.**"

"**I did not ask.**"

"**You did not ask. I am going to tell you anyway, because you have spent a morning not asking me anything, and there is a limit to how much of that a man should be allowed to get away with.**"

He put a finger on Table B.

"**I trust B. I trust the gauge zero, because the knots were tied by a man reading a gauge, and he did not know what Newlyn was and neither did I when I was his age, and a knot has no opinion about the sea. Every one of those nine was put in by somebody who had a board in front of him and a number in his head and a piece of cord, and what he was marking was a level and the closest thing he had to a level was a painted line.**"

"**And the other one.**"

"**And the other one is the one that is right,**" said Gwilym Petherick. "**Because it is the one with a definition and a chain of custody and about ninety years of practice behind it, and because if anybody ever writes any of this down in a document that anybody else will act on, it has to be in Newlyn and not in mine. So I trust B and I would have to use A, and the difference between the two is between a hundred and thirty and about two hundred and seventy millimetres depending on the year, and I have just told you that on a wet table in a lock yard and you may do what you like with it.**"

He put the sheet back in the plastic wallet and the plastic wallet in his inside pocket and buttoned the pocket.

"**Nobody wrote that down,**" said Ivo Pell.

"**No.**

"**I am not going to write it down either,**" said Elias Venn, "**and I want to say why, and it is that this is a working place and the man has just told us the two things he knows and one of them is that the other one is the one that is right, and if I write that down then in about four years there is a piece of paper in this city that says a river engineer said the knots were levels, and he did not say the knots were levels, and he will deny it, and he will be right to.**"

"**You are learning,**" said Petherick, **and did not say it kindly, and got into the van.**

## And the fifth time in twenty-seven weeks that a question was available in a room, nobody asked it, and the reason is that it is a worse question in a working place than it is in a corridor

It was there. He was aware of it in the way he was aware of a door. The knots were on a rope, and the board was on a wall, and the board had been on that wall since 1911, and the rope was the board's tie-line, and the two of them were about four inches apart and had been for about fifty years, and there were eleven numbers on a piece of A4 that came from a painted line.

The woman of about fifty had asked it in a corridor in April, because she had to wind an alarm off at six. A man of about sixty-five had asked it in a hall in May and had been told no four times.

He did not ask it, and he said why, out loud, to a man of twenty-five in a yard, in about nine seconds, which was more than he had said about it in twenty-seven weeks.

"**It is a worse question in a working place. A man who hangs a board every spring and ties a knot in a line is not doing it for a reason, and if I go up there and ask him what the pattern is, then for the rest of his life he has a thing he has to have an opinion about that he did not have before, and the only answers available to either of us are bad ones.**"

"**That is a good reason.**"

"**It is the only reason I have got, and it is the fifth time, and I have not said it out loud before, and I said it to you and not to him, which is cowardice, and I would rather it was in the record that I noticed.**"

## And on the Thursday afternoon at a wharf four miles downriver, a man of twenty-five put a camera away, and the reason is a condition he asked for himself on the first of December 2024

The tide line on the wharf wall was about two feet below the coping stones and it was not a mark anybody had made. It was forty years of the same line, a dark band with a lime line on it, and where it met the door of a bonded store there was a sill about nine inches above the quay and a clean rectangular wet stain around it.

"**There is your drainage invert,**" said Petherick to Ivo Pell. "**And there is your sill, and if the water comes over the coping there the store fills, because the door is inward and the floor is a foot below the sill.**"

"**Right.**" Ivo Pell had the camera up and then he had it down. "**No. I am not doing it.**"

"**It is four exposures and about ninety seconds.**"

"**I know exactly how long it is and that is not the problem.**" He turned the camera over. "**On the first of December I asked the Director for a condition and I got it, and the condition is that every photographic exposure of collections material by a member of staff is countersigned at the point of making. Not afterwards. At the point. And a wall and a sill on a wharf is not collections material, and I have known that for six months, and I would like somebody with a duty to say it in a room so that I can stop having this conversation with myself at half past three in the afternoon on a working wharf.**"

"**So telephone her.**"

He telephoned her. It took about four minutes and he did not put it on speaker and Elias Venn stood about eleven feet away and could hear almost none of it, which was correct.

"**She says no, it is not collections material and it never has been and nothing in that building can make it collections material by photographing it,**" said Ivo Pell. "**And she says take it.**"

"**Then what is the problem.**"

"**Then the problem is me, because take it means countersign it, and countersign it means somebody else has to be standing here at the point of making it, and there is one member of staff on this wharf and it is me.**" He put the camera in his bag. "**And she has said she will countersign four exposures on Wednesday morning, and that I may take them on Wednesday afternoon, and that if it needs doing before Wednesday I am to telephone her and she will come and stand on a wet wall herself, and that she will not enjoy it.**"

"**Which Wednesday.**"

"**The twenty-third.**"

**Neither of them said anything else about it, and the tide line went on being exactly where it had been for forty years, and a man of twenty-five put a camera in a bag on the tenth of June 2025 and took it out again on the twenty-third, and on the twenty-third there was about four feet of water inside a bonded store and the tide line on the outside of that wall was not a line any more but a stain with a top edge to it about a foot higher, and he photographed the stain, and it is in a file on the fourth floor of a building on a hill, and he is the only person in this case who knows what the picture he did not take on the tenth of June would have shown.**

## And the rope went into a dry store at South Sluice, on a hook, above the pump floor, and it is not going anywhere

Emrys Tasker had taken it off the nail and Petherick had put it in the back of a van and Ivo Pell had made a noise about the van's floor being wet, and it went about nine miles back downriver and into a store at a flood-control station on the bank, up eleven steps, above a pump floor that was four inches higher than the floor outside, and onto a hook that is for hose, and it is there now.

"**It is a working thing in a working store,**" said Petherick, "**and it is not going on a schedule and it is not going in a case, and there is nobody in this city who is going to be able to produce it in four years unless somebody in this building remembers that I put it on a hook.**"

"**Whose store is it.**"

"**The city flood office's, technically, and in practice it is a man called Ferris who has been on that bank for nineteen years and who does not want anything of any kind put on that wall and I have put it on a hose hook and apologised.**"

And in the same room, in a cage on the wall above a whiteboard, there was the third ward-bell, which had been re-cracked in the autumn and sounded a note about a quarter of a tone flat, and which a man rang on the twentieth of December and again in April because it is rung when the reach is at a warning level, and which somebody who was not a keeper struck at about ten past five on the Wednesday, because it is a bell and you ring bells.

Nobody in that room listened to it for anything but that.
