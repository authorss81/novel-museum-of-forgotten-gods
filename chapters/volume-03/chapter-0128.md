# Chapter 0128 — A Door Key On A Fob

**On Thursday the second of September, at about eleven at night, in a room above a shop in Fisher Lane, a man of thirty-two with no post, no employer and no standing read a door key on a fob out of a grey box of twenty-two things that nobody owns, and what came back was certain, and warm, and about the wrong person, and he believed it, and on the Friday the third, at about twenty past eleven in the morning, in a hall at the bottom of Ander's Row with about forty people in it, he said a living woman's name into a list of the drowned.**

**This is the first time in his life that his power has injured a living person. It is not supernatural and it is not a curse and it is not the object and it is not a god. It is a man believing a feeling, and the reason he believed it is that there was nothing in the world he could have used to disbelieve it with, and the reason there was nothing in the world he could have used is that there is no form for whether there was a second person and no form for the number of persons in a premises on the date of the event, and that line is in about nine hundred files, and it has been in a room, and it is on a sheet of ten items in a drawer, and it is in four words on a sheet of A4 with an empty name line on it, and neither he nor anybody else can put a box round it.**

## And he did not touch anything for about nine minutes, and the reason he did not is a method, and the method is the manuscript's method, and it has six steps and one of them is a witness and he has no witness

The room above the shop in Fisher Lane was let by the hour and it was empty and it had a grey box in the middle of the floor and a table with two chairs and a fan and the window open onto a lane where a bus stopped at about half past eleven and went away.

There were twenty-two things in the box. He had counted them on the Saturday and counted them again on the Wednesday and they were twenty-two both times, and there was no list, and there was no way of counting them that anybody else could check.

He did the first thing first, which is that he wrote down what he was doing and the time, on the page at the back, in the middle, where the date goes. He did not write the time on the front of the page, where a fact about a person goes, because a fact about a person is what he has a rule about and this was not yet that, and he wanted to be able to say afterwards that he had not known yet.

Then he put the fan on, because the room was warm. Then he stood with his back to the open window, because a reader wants the door behind him and not a window, and that is the second of the six steps and he had it from a man of fifty-five in a lock yard in June and not from a book. Then he took his shoes off, which he had never done and did not know why he did, and which he wrote down as *shoes off, do not know why.*

Then he looked at the twenty-two things for about nine minutes without touching any of them, and he has written down that he counted nine minutes by the bus, and that the bus came at about eleven and a half past.

A brass doorstop. A spectacle case with one lens in it. Four hair slides on a card. A child's shoe, size about two, wet through and dried badly. A tea towel with a corner torn off. A purse with about two pounds in it. A tin of screws. A man's belt. A dog collar. A plaster. A photograph in a frame with the frame's back off and no photograph in it. A set of keys on a ring with no fob — eleven keys and a plastic ring.

And a door key on a fob. Silver, worn bright at the shoulders, on a moulded plastic fob with no number and no initial and no tag, and the ring was a split ring about two inches across and it was closed, and there was nothing written on anything in it.

The fob is not a record. That is not an opinion. It has no maker that anybody can give, no date that anybody can give, no material that anybody has written down, no provenance, and no place, and there is no line on a Museum card for an object that was put in a cardboard box in a branch library by a woman who was told on a Saturday that she could hand it in, and the registrar who took the box said so out loud in a corridor with four other people in it, and the label-room man would agree with every word of it and has a sentence of his own about a rope from the twenty-fifth of February that nobody has improved on since.

**An object that is not a record cannot be given to a person, and therefore cannot be denied to one, and a man with no post is the only person in this city who may hold it without it becoming a case.**

That is the sentence. It is the thing he went there for. He did not say it out loud, and he has written in the book that he did not say it out loud, and the reason he did not say it out loud is that the room was empty and there was nobody to say it to, and a man alone in a room with a box of things that nobody owns is the most dangerous possible position for a sentence that is a reason.

He picked it up at about nine minutes and put his hand round the fob and closed his hand on it.

**Who is in this house.**

It came in about four seconds and it did not come in gradually, which is not how it works and is what he was frightened of.

A hall. Not a room: a hall, with a stair going up out of the frame on the left and a door on the right that is the front door and a floor that is hard and has been mopped a great many times.

A torch on the stairs, and somebody on the stairs with a torch, and a beam on the wall going up.

And a woman's hand, on the other side, holding a key up at about the height of a shoulder, and the hand is not frightened. That is the first thing and it is not a fact; it is a quality in the hand, the way the thumb is on the side of the ring instead of across it, the way a hand holds a key it has held before and does not need to look at.

And a voice, and three things said, and they are the clearest thing that has ever come out of an object in this city and he will hear them for the rest of his life:

***I'm Marguerite. Go on, then.***

And the door going. And rain on stone. And somebody out.

And then — and this is the part he did not see coming and the part that took eleven seconds off him and put a nosebleed on the counter — **the key being turned from the inside.** Somebody coming back in out of the rain and shutting the door and turning the key, and then the bolt, and a hand flat on the inside of a door, and the last thing in it is not a feeling at all, it is a piece of housekeeping: a woman checking that a door is locked, and then going away from it.

And the whole of it is about eleven seconds long and it is the warmest thing that has ever happened to him.

He stood in a room above a shop in Fisher Lane with his hand shut on a key and a nosebleed going down his chin onto the table and no memory gone, no minute lost, nothing taken out of him at all, and a woman's first name in his mouth.

That is the cost. That is all of it. It is not expensive and it is not small and it is not going to hurt him in a month.

## And he did not write it down, and the reason he did not write it down is new, and the reason he did not write it down is that a book has one reader and a room has forty, and that is the first thing in thirty-seven weeks he has got right for the wrong reason

He said it out loud at about eleven twenty on the Thursday night to an empty room, once, to find out whether his mouth would do it.

It did.

He stood there for a while and then he sat down and put his head in his hands, and there is a rule in the front of his book and the third line of it is *do not write it down*, and he has broken it twice in thirty-seven weeks, once in a room with nine people in it in May and once in July for a man of fifty-eight who had not asked to be caught.

He did not break it on the second of September and the reason was new and he wrote the reason down, because the reason was the rule.

**A book has one reader. A room has forty. If I write it down then it is mine, and I am a man with no post who is owed nothing and is wrong about a box in a form. If I say it in a room then it is theirs, and it can be checked, and it can be stopped, and it can be corrected, and there is a woman of twenty-eight with four words on a sheet of A4 in a folder on a second shelf who has been sitting on a reading since March 2016 and who will tell me in about nine seconds that I have done a stupid thing, and I would rather have that in front of forty people than in a book at half past eleven at night.**

Then he said the sentence he had not come to say, four times, into the fan, to nobody.

**There is a house here.** He got to *house* and stopped, and the stopping was not because the sentence was too big but because a house is a building, and there is a building in this case with four hundred and six lines in a council spreadsheet and a level on it and about two hundred and thirty tenancies behind four hundred metres of it, and the sentence he has been carrying since December is about a house and not about a building, and if he says the second half then he is talking about a person, and there is no version of the second half that is not about a person, and a person who has not asked to be held is not a collection, and he has said that sentence in a book four times and has never said it to a living soul.

He did not say it. He has said it out loud three times now in five months and has not said it to anybody once, and the reason the third one was the closest is that there was nobody there at all, and he has noticed that the sentence is at its worst when there is no witness, which is the exact opposite of what he would have expected, and he has written that down too, because it is true, and because he does not know yet what to do with it.
