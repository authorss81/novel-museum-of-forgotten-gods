# Chapter 0123 — Nineteen O'Clock, In A Room, With The Windows Open

**On Wednesday the eighteenth and Thursday the nineteenth of August 2025 a bag came out of a cupboard in a vestry nine miles upriver and a thing that had been written down on Wednesday the seventeenth of July was done on Thursday the nineteenth at ten in the morning in a registrar's office on Civic Hill, in a room, with two witnesses, and the finding was a null, and the null was written in the words *no person is named in this minute* and dated and signed. Nobody was named. The hand was not identified. The note in the schedule of collections was not read aloud and is not part of the finding.**

**A registrar of sixty-eight took the minute for the second time in thirteen days, having taken one by telephone on the sixth of August, and she has kept a minute book for forty-one years and had never minuted a meeting she was not at. On Thursday evening, in a room with the windows open, she was asked to put the two reasons side by side and she said the first one out loud and then said that the second one was better, and nobody in the room disagreed, and the second one is at the end of this and it is the reason this chapter is the shape it is.**

## And on the Wednesday morning a bag came out of a cupboard in a vestry, and there was a hardback of interments on a shelf above a radiator with a dehumidifier under it, and for about four minutes nobody knew a man of thirty-two was in the room

The bag had a piece of paper in it and the piece of paper was the sheet of the seventeenth of July and the sheet had been folded twice and then folded again and it had been folded and unfolded a great many times in seven weeks by a woman who was sixty-eight and who had no reason to fold it at all except that her hands do that when a thing is nearly a minute.

> **TO BE READ, in a room, on Thursday 19 August 2025, at ten o'clock in the morning, in the registrar's office, Civic Hill.**
> **1. The hand on the index card of 1902. 2. The two entries in the day book for the week of the casting. 3. The note at the entry for plinth thirty-four, which is already public, which names no person, and which is not read aloud and is not part of the finding.**
> **To be present: M. Iles, Registrar, and such other persons as are told they are required to attend. Two witnesses. A note will be kept. If there is nothing on the card, that is the finding, and it will be written in those words, and dated, and signed.**
> **The book is not to be read in a corridor.**
> **— A. Shand, registrar of burials, Braeswick, 17 July 2025.**

Averil Shand had gone to unlock the vestry and had gone out again to move her car, and the door had been left on the latch because she had a bad knee and had told him in June that she had a bad knee and he had written it down and had not offered.

The vestry was about eleven feet by nine and it had a window with wired glass and a radiator under a shelf and a dehumidifier on the floor with a tank that had been emptied on Monday because it had been full on Saturday.

The book was four inches thick and it was on the shelf and it was not in a box and it was not wrapped and it was not behind glass, and the spine said nothing, and there was a linen tape round the front edge and a pencil in a loop.

Nobody in that parish knew what it was except her. That is a fact about a parish and not about a museum, and he had written it down, and the two of them were the only people on earth who knew it and one of them had got out of of a chair at about four times the speed of a person with a bad knee.

He did not touch it.

He read it.

There is no word for it in this case and there has never been one, and the words that exist in this business are *resonant* and *carrying* and *answered*, and all three of those are about a thing doing something to a person, and what happened in a vestry at about eleven twenty-five on Wednesday the eighteenth of August was a man standing a hand's width from four inches of paper reading a hundred and forty years of a river valley, and what came back was **a great many people, all at once, in no order, and none of them wanting anything from him.**

He had expected one. He had wanted one. He had a name in his head for the thing he wanted and it was a woman in a kitchen and it was not in the book.

What he got was a Tuesday in March and a man of about sixty putting a spade in, and then a Thursday in 1929 and a child on a rope, and then eleven people standing in a doorway in sleet in 1968, and then a smell of tar off a boat, and then nothing, and then — because the book is four inches thick and there is a hundred and forty years in it and the room is eleven feet by nine and he is one man — **a great many more, and a kind of tiredness that is not his, in a hand he does not have, and the word that is closest to it is *done*.**

It lasted about four minutes and there was no cost he could name afterwards, and there was a moment about two minutes in where he put his hand flat on the shelf and stopped it, and he did not do it with a word because he has no word, and he did it by deciding that he had read enough, and that decision took him about nine seconds and it is the first time in thirty-six weeks that he has been able to stop a reading at a moment of his own choosing.

Mara Iles found him at about half past eleven with his hand on the shelf and the bag in the other one.

"**Did you read it.**"

"**Yes.**"

"**How long.**"

"**About four minutes.**"

"**What did you get.**"

He thought about it for a while, on the steps, in the sun.

"**I got everybody,**" he said. "**And I got them in no order and I could not tell you one thing about anybody. And I could tell you that somebody in this parish was very tired in about 1931 and it was not a feeling I went in looking for, and I have got no way of knowing whether that is true or whether I have made it up, and I know which of those two I would rather.**"

"**So you do not know if it was accurate.**"

"**No.**"

"**Then write that down.**"

"**I am not writing it down.**"

"**Elias.**"

"**I have got a rule about it,**" he said, "**and the rule is that a fact a person gives me in a street is theirs until it is in a file, and a file is the only place in this city where a person is safe, and I do not write it down. And I have broken it twice this year and I am not going to break it a third time for something I cannot check, because the day I put a feeling in a book with my name at the top of it, that is a fact about me and not about a parish, and I have spent thirty-five weeks learning the difference and I am not going to unlearn it on a Wednesday morning in a vestry.**"

Mara Iles did not argue with it, which was new, and she said one sentence on the steps.

"**Then tell me the reason and put nothing else on the page, and if somebody asks me in nine months why I have a blank in a file, I will be able to say a man read a book and could not tell whether he was right, and that is a true sentence and it is better than a wrong one.**"

## And on the Thursday morning at ten o'clock the book that is not read in a corridor was read in a room, and there were five people in it and two of them were witnesses and one of them had not been told he was required to attend and was not asked to leave

The registrar's office on Civic Hill had been Mara Iles's for five years and it had a window that opened and a table that was too small and a chair for anybody who wanted one and a line of telephone that had been disconnected in about 2019 and had not been reconnected, and that is the first thing she said.

"**The telephone does not work and I have not had it reconnected, and the reason it does not work is that a thing that happens in this room is a thing that happens in this room.**"

The two witnesses were **Fenn**, of the label room, who had a surname on a badge and had done the job a very long time, and **Emrys Tasker**, who had come down nine miles from Braeswick Lower Lock on the first bus, and who had known the registrar of burials of that parish for forty years and had never once asked her the question.

Elias Venn was there because a registrar of twenty-eight had said on the Wednesday afternoon that a man with no post could not be a witness to anything and could not be in the room, and had then said, in the same breath, that he would be told he was required to attend, and had written that on the minute in advance, and had not been laughed out of doing it.

"**It is on the sheet before we start,**" said Mara Iles. "**E. Venn was told he is required to attend. He has no standing and no box and I have put him in a minute in a room in a museum, which is a thing I have not done before and I am going to do it and nobody is to argue with me about it, because the last time this case went looking for a hand, it was two men with a torch on a wet quay at four in the morning and one of them wrote down a number that everybody believed for four months.**"

"**What is on the card.**"

> **MUSEUM OF QUIET SAINTS. Series card file, 1902. Card ref. 1902/HF/3.**
> **Obverse:** typed. `PLINTH 34. BELL. FOUNDRY, 1902. CASTING WEEK COMMENCING 11 OCTOBER 1902.` Under it, in the same typed hand, a number in the form the Museum uses, with a series year and two letters and a number.
> **Reverse:** ink. A line, about forty millimetres, gone over twice. Four characters survive at the start of the line. Below the line, a second line, shorter, also gone over, and below that, in the same ink and possibly the same sitting, a figure: two letters, no year, and a number.

Nobody in the room could read the four characters from where they were sitting, and the card was put under a lamp, and then it was not read aloud, and it was read.

The reading of the card took about four minutes. Two of those were Fenn, who read handwriting for a living and had said in a conservation office in February that he would rather not, and one of whom was Tasker, who read the knots in a line the same way.

"**There is no name on that card that I can read and I am not going to guess one,**" said Fenn, "**and the reason is not that it is illegible. It is legible. It is a name in a hand that was writing fast and then stopped and then went over it.**"

"**How fast.**"

"**Fast in the way people write when they are copying something off another piece of paper,**" said Fenn, "**and stopped in the way people stop when they have looked up and there is somebody in the room.**"

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

"**Say the next sentence,**" said Elias Venn, "**and say it as a guess and not as a finding, because I will not have it in a minute as a finding.**"

"**All right.**" Fenn put the card down. "**A maker's name goes on the face of a card, because a maker's name is what you are buying. If it goes on the back, then somebody did not want it bought, and then went over it twice, and it did not take, and then left the card in the file.**"

"**Is that a guess.**"

"**That is a guess and I know it is a guess and I have known it since the second of February and I have not said it to anybody because it is mine and I have a job.**"

"**Write it down and mark it a guess.**"

"**I would rather it was on the page than in my head,**" said Fenn. "**Yes. Write it down.**"

And the day book, and the two entries, and the thing that nobody expected, which was that the day book had a witness column and that it was empty in both lines.

> **FOUNDRY DAY BOOK — week of 11 October 1902.**
> **14 Oct. — Pattern, third bell. Out 06:20. In 19:10. Witness: —**
> **18 Oct. — Pattern, third bell. Out 06:10. In —**

"**A pattern is the thing you cast from,**" said Emrys Tasker. "**It is not the bell. It is the shape, and it is kept in a box and it is brought out and it is put in the sand and it is packed round and it is two days' work for one man and a boy, and if it is brought out at a weekend then the weekend was a weekend somebody worked.**"

"**And the first one came back at ten past seven in the evening.**"

"**And the second one did not.**" Tasker looked at it for a while. "**And there is no in on it, and there is no in on it because a day book is a day book, and a man writes the in when the pattern comes back off the bench, and if it did not come back off the bench then the man does not write anything, he goes and looks.**"

"**For how long.**"

"**For ever, if nobody ever went back and looked,**" said Emrys Tasker. "**Which is the ordinary thing. That is what a day book is. It is a record of what came back and no record at all of what did not, and a hundred and twenty-three years is a long time for a box to sit in a foundry and for nobody to write one line on a page.**"

## And the finding was a null, and the null was a finding, and at about eleven fifteen a registrar of sixty-eight explained in a room why she had taken a minute by telephone eight days before, and then explained it better

"**I am going to say the six things,**" said Mara Iles, "**and I have written them and I am going to read them out and then four people are going to try to improve on them and I am going to let them, because I wrote them on the Wednesday and I have read them about nine times since and there may be something in them and I am the registrar of this building and I am twenty-eight and I have been doing it for five years.**"

> **1. The hand on the reverse of the index card of 1902 is not the hand of the typing and is not attributed to any person.**
> **2. Four characters of a name survive. They are not read aloud in this minute and are not reproduced in it.**
> **3. The figure below them is two letters, no year, and a number. A Museum accession reference carries a series year. This does not. It is not a Museum accession reference and it is not treated as one.**
> **4. The two entries in the day book for the week of the casting are present. One is complete. One has no return entered. The day book has a column headed *Witness* and the column is empty in both lines.**
> **5. No comparison of the hand on this card with the hand on the card at plinth thirty-four has been made, and none can be made in this room. The only images of the plinth card are four photographs taken by an officer of another body in June and marked not for reproduction. The comparison is not made. It is not declined. It is not available.**
> **6. NULL. No person is named in this minute and none is identified. The attendance of anybody in this room is not evidence of anything and is minuted as attendance and as nothing else.**
> **7. The note at the entry for plinth thirty-four in the schedule of collections is public, names no person, and is not part of this finding and was not read aloud and nobody in this room has it in front of them.**
> **M. Iles, registrar, 19 August 2025.**

"**Say the reason for six,**" said Averil Shand.

"**Because a registrar of twenty-eight was told in June by a registrar of sixty-eight that a thing is read in a room, on a day that is written down, by people who are all in the room, with a note kept by somebody with a duty, and a null written in those words.**"

"**That is the procedure. I want the other one.**"

"**I gave you a reason on the telephone on the sixth of August and I wrote it into item four of that minute and it is on a page upriver and you wrote it and it is the reason you gave then and I am not going to take it back.**" Shand put her hands flat on the table, which she did. "**I gave it because I have kept a minute book for forty-one years and I had never minuted a meeting I was not at, and because a registrar of burials is asked for a list eleven times in forty-one years and I was not going to be asked for the whole of a meeting and get nothing, which is what happens every time a thing is not written down.**"

"**That is a good reason.**"

"**It is a good reason and it is the wrong one,**" said Averil Shand, "**and I have had a week and a day to sit with the fact that I gave a telephone on a Friday night and got a thing I did not expect, and I am going to say the other one, and I want it in the minute in this room and not only in a minute book upriver that nobody will ever read.**"

"**Go on.**"

"**The first reason was about me,**" she said. "**The first reason was: I am not going to be asked for a whole of a meeting and get nothing, and that is a reason about a woman of sixty-eight and what she is owed. It is true and I have no argument with it and it is not why I did it.**"

Somebody's chair went over about four inches and was brought back.

"**The second reason is that a minute is not a thing you do for the person who asked you,**" said Averil Shand. "**A minute is a thing you do so that a person who was not in the room, and will not be born for another fifty years, can be told what was decided and on what day and by whom, and why it was decided that way and not another way. And the reason I know that is because I have been the person who was not in the room.**"

She looked at the man of thirty-two at the end of the table.

"**In 1986 a woman forty miles away came and asked me for a list and I gave her one, because she asked properly and I had no ground to refuse her and I have never had one since, and it was in date order, and she worked from the front, and she did not get to the eleventh of March 1949, and she told the family he was not there. And I found it on the fourteenth of June 1990 and I sent it and the family have had a different story since and the story is theirs and it is not mine and I have never once been asked about it and I have never once been asked whether I would do it again.**" She squared the sheet in front of her. "**I was the person who was not in the room. There was a thing that happened and it was true and I was not in the room and there was nothing anywhere that said what had happened, and there is a family forty miles away that is still living on what a woman told them in 1986 and the only reason anybody knows there is a second version is that I wrote it down in a minute book that nobody has ever asked to see, in a parish, on the fourteenth of June 1990.**"

Nobody in the room said anything.

"**So that is the second reason, and it is better than the first, and the first one is not wrong, and I am going to keep the first one in the minute as well, because the person who does this next time in this parish is about thirty and she is going to want a reason about herself and she should have one.**"

## And at nineteen hundred on the Thursday, in a room with the windows open, because the day had been hot, the minute was read out four times, and on the fourth reading a woman of twenty-eight said the sentence that made it a document, and a man with no post nearly said the other sentence and did not
