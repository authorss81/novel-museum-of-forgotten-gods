# Chapter 0129 — The Woman Carried Out

**On Friday the third of September, from about half past eleven in the morning until about ten past ten on Saturday the fourth, a name that was not hers was in a column on a provisional schedule in a Methodist hall at the bottom of Ander's Row, and the woman it belonged to was alive, at home, forty-nine years in the same house, and had opened a door on the night of the eighteenth of June for a woman she did not know and had been waiting nine weeks for anybody to ask her about it. The woman it was given to was in the hall for two hours and nobody asked her name. She is about forty-three. She is not named here and she is not named anywhere, and that is not a rule, it is what has happened.**

**The misidentification landed at about twenty past eleven on the Friday and it was not supernatural and it was not a curse and it was not an object and it was not a woman being unlucky. It was a warm accurate feeling about a woman letting somebody out of a door, joined to a name, on an evening when a key was last turned from the inside. And the registrar of twenty-eight had to decide what to do with a document that is wrong and a person who is not a document, and she decided on Friday afternoon in about eleven seconds, and the thing she decided was to use the only instrument this case has ever had, and to point it at the man.**

## And at about half past twelve on the Friday a duty officer with nineteen years in post telephoned a house in Ander's Row and a woman of sixty-nine answered it, and said that she had not been carried out, and that she had let somebody in, and that nobody had asked her

"**It is Delyth Morgan, district register, Northglass. I am ringing about a schedule we worked yesterday. I am going to read you a line and I am going to tell you what I think about it before you answer, because I have a name on a column that I entered myself and I have not liked it since I wrote it.**"

"**Go on.**"

"**The line reads *fourteen Ashgill Row, Bankfoot, ground floor, one person carried out at about two in the morning by a boat from the low bank*, and the name we have entered against it is yours, and your address is twenty-seven Ander's Row, and I want to tell you before you say anything that the name was given to me in a hall yesterday morning by a man who said it came out of an object and that he had not checked it, and that I entered it anyway, and that I have been ringing every number on that schedule since about half past twelve yesterday and this is the first one where the person answered.**"

There was a pause on the line of about four seconds.

"**I have not been carried out,**" said Marguerite Sowle.

"**No,**" said Delyth Morgan. "**I did not think for one second that you had. I am going to read you the rest of it and I am going to apologise before I do.**"

"**I was not carried out,**" said Marguerite Sowle, "**and I have not been in a boat in my life, and I have been at number twenty-seven for forty-nine years, and I was at home.**"

"**Then I have put a name on a line that is not yours, and I entered it in a room with about forty people in it, and the line is a public working document and there is a process for taking a name off it and the process takes until Tuesday, and I am going to tell you what the process is and then I am going to tell you the other thing, and the other thing is the reason I rang you myself instead of writing.**"

"**Go on.**"

"**A name that has been on a provisional schedule and then taken off it stays on it. It is struck through and it is not erased and it is not destroyed, because a schedule of people provisionally recorded is a record of what people said, and the thing that gets struck out is somebody's statement, not our certainty. So by Tuesday morning there will be a line in that hall with your name on it and a line through your name, and both of them will be readable by anybody who asks to read the schedule, and anybody may ask.**"

Marguerite Sowle did not say anything for about eleven seconds.

"**Right,**" she said. "**And what is the other thing.**"

"**The other thing is that the line is now completable,**" said Delyth Morgan. "**And I have been doing this for nineteen years and I will tell you exactly what that means, because I would rather you had it from me than from a letter. A provisional entry with no name on it cannot be closed. A provisional entry with a name on it can be sent forward. And an entry that is sent forward is the only thing in this flood that anybody is paid money for, and it is paid to a policyholder at an address on a date of loss, seven hundred and fifty pounds, once, per household, per event.**"

"**So somebody gets seven hundred and fifty pounds.**"

"**Somebody gets seven hundred and fifty pounds,**" said Delyth Morgan, "**and that somebody will be a person at an address, and it will not be you, and it will be the person at fourteen Ashgill Row, and somebody is going to write to that person this week asking them for a date.**"

"**And they will not be able to give her one.**"

"**They will not be able to give her one,**" said Delyth Morgan, "**and that is not my department and I have no power over it and I have never had any power over it.**"

There was a pause again and it was longer.

"**Miss Sowle,**" said Delyth Morgan. "**There is a second thing and I am going to say it and you are going to think it is strange. On the night of the eighteenth of June, did you open your door?**"

"**I did.**"

"**To whom.**"

"**To a woman I had never seen in my life,**" said Marguerite Sowle. "**She came up the path at about half past twelve in the morning with a torch in her teeth and a rope over her shoulder and she was going down toward the water and she knocked on my door and I opened it and she said *is there anybody in that row* and I said *there is a woman and a girl* and she said *go on then* and she went out into it, and I stood in my hall with the door open for about four hours and then I shut it and locked it and I have not been able to say anything about that to anybody since the nineteenth of June because there has been nobody to say it to.**"

"**Miss Sowle.**"

"**I am not being clever,**" said Marguerite Sowle. "**I have been forty-nine years in that house and I have never once in my life been asked anything. I have not been asked by the council, I have not been asked by anybody, and I am aware that I am sixty-nine and I am aware of what that sounds like at this precise moment and I do not care.**"

## And the woman of about forty-three was in the hall for two hours and nobody asked her name, and at about twenty past one in the afternoon she put her hand flat on the table and asked whether it was a good thing to be on it, and she was told that it was a working document, and she said right

She had come in at about ten past eleven and she had been at the back, and there were about forty people in the hall and about six of them from the district register, and nobody made anybody sign anything and nobody asked anybody anything except a duty officer asking whether anybody recognised an address.

At about twenty past one, when the June entries were finished and the room was emptying, she came up to the table and put her hand flat on it, which is a thing that about four people in this case do and she had not been taught it from anybody.

"**Is it a good thing to be on it,**" she said.

Delyth Morgan said, "**I am sorry, I did not catch who I was talking to.**"

"**It does not matter,**" said the woman. "**Fourteen Ashgill Row. I am in the ground floor.**"

"**Then I would like your name for the—**"

"**No,**" said the woman, and she said it pleasantly, and about forty people had heard the question and four of them were children and nobody in that hall had heard a name being asked for in nine weeks in a public room and now they had.

"**No,**" said the woman of about forty-three. "**I did not come for that. I came because a woman came round with a leaflet in the last week of August and it said anybody could come, and I have been in this ground floor since the fourteenth of June and there has been nobody in it at all except the man about the joists.**"

"**You are on the schedule,**" said Delyth Morgan. "**You are the person the schedule is about. There is an entry and the entry says a person was carried out at about two in the morning by a boat and the entry has your address and it does not have your name on it, and I am going to be honest with you, because you have been honest with me: it had a name on it this morning that was not yours and I took it out at about twenty past twelve and it is struck through and it is not erased and by Tuesday there will be a line through a name on a public document, and I am sorry for that more than I am sorry for anything else I have done in nineteen years.**"

"**Who was it.**"

"**A woman of sixty-nine at number twenty-seven in the same row as you are in.**"

"**No,**" said the woman of about forty-three. "**No, that is not right, and I am telling you it is not right, and I am not going to say anything else about it, and I have got a reason and the reason is that I have not been asked anything since June and I am not starting on a Saturday afternoon in a hall.**"

"**That is completely yours,**" said Delyth Morgan, and she had her hand out of her pocket before she said it and she put it back.

"**Right,**" said the woman of about forty-three, and she stayed for another half an hour, and she did not tell anybody her name, and nobody asked her again, and about seven o'clock that evening somebody would say, in a room above a shop in Fisher Lane, that this was the single worst afternoon of a case that had had about ten of them.

## And on the Saturday morning at ten past ten a name was struck through in a hall and not erased, and the woman it belonged to asked whether she could have the letter back, and she was told that she could not, and she kept it, and it is on her table

"**I would like to ask you something and I have got about nine seconds to do it in before somebody needs me,**" said Marguerite Sowle, on the telephone, at about half past eleven on the Saturday.

"**Ask it.**"

"**I had a letter on Friday afternoon. Somebody from an office on the north side knocked at about two o'clock and gave me a card and said it was about a name and that there had been a mistake and that they were sorry, and the card is in my hand and I have read it about nine times.**"

"**What is on it.**"

"**My name and my address and the fourteenth of Ashgill Row and the words *provisional record* and a date, and underneath it, printed, in capitals, *SCHEDULED FOR DETERMINATION*, and underneath *no action is required from you* in italics.**" She read the italics out loud. "**And I want to know whether the no action is required is true. Because I have been at home since the nineteenth of June and I have not had a letter in nine weeks and I have had a card with my address on it in a fortnight, and I am sixty-nine, and I would like to know whether I am to expect somebody.**"

"**I do not know,**" said Delyth Morgan.

"**That is the honest one,**" said Marguerite Sowle. "**I will tell you what I want and then you can tell me what you can. I want to give the card back.**"

"**You cannot give it back. Once a name has been on a schedule it is a record of what somebody said and it is held by us, and a card in your hand does not change whose record it is, and if you give it back it will be a record of you having been told and we will not be able to prove that, and I would rather have it in your hand and on our file.**"

"**Then I am not giving it back,**" said Marguerite Sowle, "**and I would like it said on the telephone that I asked and I was told no, and that is the first time in about forty-nine years anybody in this city has asked me for something and said no to me, and I have found that I am not as upset about it as I would have thought.**"

Nobody asked her about the door, because a duty officer of forty-seven had spent about a quarter of an hour on the telephone on the Friday explaining that she had no power over anything but a column, and the column had been dealt with.
