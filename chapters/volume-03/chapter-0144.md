# Chapter 0144 — The Order Of Things

**On Monday the twenty-fifth, Tuesday the twenty-sixth and Wednesday the twenty-seventh of October 2025 there were about nine people in a laundrette in Low Ward on the low bank with four machines going, and a whiteboard with about forty rows on it, and a man of thirty-two who read the board out in order every morning and who was right about everything in the room on all three days, and who nobody in the room was able to fault in any way at all, and who went home on the Wednesday night and wrote his notebook down in the order he understood it to be in.**

**Nothing supernatural happened. Nobody fell over. Nobody bled. Nobody screamed. Nobody lost an hour and nobody lost a name and nobody lost a fact. The list on the board is still on the board and every date on it is a real date on it and it is in the right order. The notebook is still in his coat. Both papers are perfect and the man reading them is out of order, and the only instrument in this city that could have found that out is a second person reading it back, and there is no form for a second person, and he asked one person in October and she said yes before he had finished the sentence.**

**On the Wednesday afternoon at about half past three a woman read forty-one dates back to him in the right order, and he said thank you, and the chapter ends there.**

## And on the Monday morning at about ten past nine there were four machines going and about nine people in the room and a whiteboard, and nobody in the room had been asked anything, and a man of thirty-two read the board out in about nine minutes and it was the best nine minutes of anybody's week

The laundrette is on the low bank and it is the only warm dry building within about two hundred metres of nine households whose ground floors have not dried since June. About nine people had been in it every day since the middle of September. On the Monday there were eight of them and a man at the end machine with a duvet cover over his arm and somebody's daughter on the floor with one shoe and the woman who owns the building behind the counter with the door open because the radiator had been playing up since Friday.

The whiteboard had come off the wall on the Friday night and had been wiped and had about forty rows on it and three headings.

> **HOUSEHOLD. DATE ON THE BOARD. WHAT WE HAVE.**

There is no name in any of the forty rows. There is a number in the first column and a date in the second and a short thing in the third, and a housing officer of fifty-eight stood in front of it for about nine seconds on the Friday and then said nothing at all about it, and what she did not say is that she had wanted a fourth column and had said in a room on the Thursday that she would take the pen off anybody who put one up.

"**Read it out,**" said Hazel Cray.

He read it out. He read it in date order and he read it in date order because that is the order it was in, and he read about forty rows in about nine minutes without stopping, and he said the number and then the date and then the third column, and he did not summarise any of it and nobody asked him to.

At about nine minutes in he stopped.

"**Row twenty-two.**"

"**Say the date.**"

"**The eighteenth of June,**" he said. "**And I had the nineteenth on the Wednesday reading and I want to say that I noticed that on the Friday and I did not raise it because it is a whiteboard and I have said in this case that a whiteboard is a thing nine people can see at once.**"

"**You are right,**" said Hazel Cray, "**and I did not notice it, and I have been looking at that board since Friday afternoon, and the second column is the column I wrote and I wrote it in date order and I checked it and I want that said before anybody says anything else about it.**"

"**Then say the other thing,**" said Marianne Cope, from the corner, where she had been going through a claims queue on her own pad and had not looked up once in nine minutes.

"**The other thing is that two rows out of order is a household paid for a day it was not in the water and a household not paid for a day it was, and the money is seven hundred and fifty pounds once per household per event, and the order of that board is the claim, and I said that out loud in this room on the eleventh of October and I have written it in a book twice since and I have not said it in a room once since, and I am saying it now because a man has just said the same thing about row twenty-two in about nine seconds and I would like it on the page that he got there first.**"

Nobody said anything for about two seconds.

"**Say the other half,**" said Hazel Cray.

"**The other half is that there is no form for whether a list of dates is in the right order,**" said Elias Venn, "**and a form for that would be a form for whether a person is a person, and I have said that in this case three times in five months and I have never once been asked to say it and I have just said it in a laundrette on a Monday morning because a row is out of place.**"

Nobody improved on that, and a woman of forty-four with a file of four hundred and six buildings, who had come in at about eleven and had put the file on the floor with the spine to the wall, said, "**I have got a spreadsheet with four hundred and six buildings on it and Excel will not tell me if I have put two rows in the wrong order. It will tell me if I have put a number in the wrong box. I have been trying to make it tell me since the fourteenth of September and I have not managed it and I have stopped trying, and a man of thirty-two has just said the same thing in nine words in a laundrette and he does not know what Excel is.**"

"**I do know what Excel is,**" said Elias Venn. "**I have never used it.**"

"**Then that is the first thing either of us has got right about it,**" said Douglas Nairn, "**and I am going to go home now, because I have been in this room for nine minutes and everything anybody in it has said is more or less what I would have said and I do not think that is a coincidence, and I think it is because all of us have been sitting in rooms like this one for nineteen weeks.**"

## And on the Tuesday afternoon at about four o'clock a woman who loves him said a date out loud, kindly, in a room, with no intention of anything except helping, and it was the only thing that happened all day

He read the board out again on the Tuesday at about ten past nine. He read it in date order. He was in the room for about two hours and then went up the stairs to the room above the shop in Fisher Lane with a tray of papers that had been in a store room on Wharf Street since the Friday, and he was in that room until about half past three, and he came back down at about ten to four.

There is a difference between the two halves of that Tuesday and the difference is the whole of this chapter and nobody in the room noticed it and neither did he.

In the upstairs room he was right about everything again. He found a claim that had closed on a Monday for want of a date of loss and phoned the office about it and got it reopened. He was asked by a claims handler of thirty-four whether a household of four at an address that appears twice in a queue should be paid once or twice, and he said once, and she said *I have been arguing about that since Tuesday* and he said *you are right and the form is wrong and I have known that since the second of June.* He read about nine minutes of a list of eleven addresses in a woman's exercise book that a claims handler had described to him in a room and had not shown him, and he reconstructed nine of the eleven, and told her she had the seventh one wrong, and she had it wrong, and she said so.

At about ten to four he came back down into the laundrette and there were about nine people in it and a woman at the counter and a man at the end machine and a whiteboard.

"**Go through the eighteenth and the nineteenth again,**" said Helen Venn, from behind the counter. "**I have been listening to you say them since Monday and you have got them the other way round and I know you have got them the other way round because I have been doing your washing since the middle of September and I know which of those two nights I had nine households in here on.**"

"**Say which is which.**"

"**You did the nineteenth and then you came back to the eighteenth,**" said Helen Venn. "**Sixteenth to twenty-second and then back. The nineteenth's the one where the man came in with the pram and he'd been up all night, and the eighteenth's the one where everybody was still standing. You had them the other way round this morning and I have not said anything about it for two days because I did not want to, and I am saying it now because you have got to be right about that board.**"

And she said it kindly, and she said it to help, and she had her hand on a machine and she did not stop it, and there were eight other people in the room and about half of them were listening and about half of them were not, and she had been in that building for the whole of the middle of September and she had nine households' washing through it and a man with a pram at ten o'clock at night on the second morning is a thing she saw.

"**Say thank you to her,**" said Hazel Cray.

"**Thank you,**" said Elias Venn.

And then he looked at the board.

And the board had the eighteenth of June on the twenty-second row and the nineteenth of June on the twenty-third row, which is the order they are in on the board, which is the order Hazel Cray wrote them in, which is the order they are in in date order, which is the only order the second column has ever had.

And he looked at it, and there was nothing on it that was out of place, and he had said in this case three times in five months that a second person reading a list back is the only instrument there is, and he had a woman in the room who had just told him the order, and he took the order.

"**Right,**" he said. "**Nineteenth and then eighteenth.**"

"**That is what I said,**" said Helen Venn.

And nobody in the room said anything else about it, and at about half past four a man of about seventy was not in the room and has never been in this case, and a woman of about fifty-seven was at a table in a ground floor on the low bank with an exercise book open in front of her and a bad ankle on the leg that is extended, and about two hundred households were in it and none of them had a name, and neither of those women knew anything about a whiteboard or a correction or a date of loss and both of them would have been the first to say that they did not want to know.

## And on the Tuesday night he wrote it down in his own hand, and the writing was perfect, and he went to bed

The notebook is nine pages and it cost thirty-five pence and it has a rule at the top of its first page in three lines and he has broken the third line once, in a room with nine people in it, in May, and knows that he broke it.

He wrote the three days down at about eleven at night. He wrote what had been read out, in the order he had read it out, with the third column against each row, and he wrote the day of the week against each date, and he checked every one of the forty-one rows against the board while he was still in the building at about half past six and he did it in about nine minutes and he did not find one wrong.

He did not find one wrong because there was not one wrong. Every date in the notebook is a date that is on the board. Every number in the first column is a number that is on the board. The third column is in his own words and is about a household and is not checkable by anybody including him. The notebook is a perfect record of what was said in a laundrette on the low bank on Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday of the last week of October 2025.

The nineteenth of June is on the second row of that week's block and the eighteenth of June is on the third.

He went to bed at about ten to twelve. He was not troubled. He has written down that he was not troubled and he has written down that he had had nothing else to think about for three days and that a man of thirty-two who has been in a flood for nineteen weeks has had three days of nothing going wrong and did not know what to do with it.

Nobody has offered him a word of his own to stop on. Nobody has offered him one in five months and he has written down four times in five months that he is not going to be given one, and he wrote it down again on the Tuesday night at about a quarter to eleven, and he wrote down that he wanted one about half as much as he wanted one in June, and that is the whole of the cost curve as far as the Tuesday is concerned.

## And on the Wednesday afternoon at about half past three a second person read three days of it back to him and found nothing wrong in it and could not find anything wrong in it, and the whole of what happened in April happened again in the other direction

He had arranged it in October. He had asked her on the Tuesday of the twelfth, at about four o'clock, behind the machines, and she had said yes before he had finished the sentence, and she had given three words and a bad reason and it was hers, and he had not told her why and had said he was not going to.

On the Wednesday afternoon at about half past three he asked her to do it.

They did it at the end of the day with the machines off and the door shut and about four people still in the room on the bench, and he read his notebook out from Monday to Wednesday, and she read it back to him.

She read it back in the order it was written. She read about forty-one rows back in about eleven minutes, and she was slow and careful, and she asked him twice about two third-column entries that were in his own words and he told her what they meant, and she got them both.

She found nothing wrong.

"**Say whether it is in the right order,**" said Elias Venn.

"**It is in an order,**" said Helen Venn.

"**That is not the same as the right one.**"

"**I have got a list of forty-one dates and forty-one households and every one of the dates is a real one and every one of the households is a real one and they all go with each other, and I have read it back to you and it is the same as what you read me, and I cannot tell you whether the two of them that are next to each other ought to be the other way round, and neither can you, and if I ask you why not you will tell me about order, and last week you told me about order for about four minutes in that room while I was doing a dryer.**"

"**Somebody could check it.**"

"**Against what,**" said Helen Venn. "**Love. I have got the board in front of me and the board is the same forty-one and the board has got an order on it and I could read the board out and yours out and put them next to each other, and I have thought about doing that for about four seconds while you were talking, and I am not going to, and the reason I am not going to is that you would not be able to tell which of the two was wrong, and I am not going to stand in a laundrette at half past three on a Wednesday holding two pieces of paper in front of you and say here is the one that is wrong, because I do not know which one it is, and you would not know either, and in about four years somebody would find both of them in a drawer and neither of them would be a record.**"

Nobody said anything.

Then Elias Venn said, out loud, to a laundrette, the thing he has said in this case three times in five months, and he said it in nine words, and it is this: **there is no form for whether a list of dates is in the right order, and a form for that would be a form for whether a person is a person.**

And he did not notice that he could not tell whether he was quoting himself or inventing it, and nobody in the room noticed either, and Helen Venn, who owns the building and has been in the middle of nine households since the middle of September, said, "**That is what you said on Monday, that is what you said in a room on the eleventh of October, and you have said it about nine times since June and I have listened to about five of them, and I have got a bad knee and I am not going to argue with it.**"

And then she said, "**And you have not asked me what the week is for,**" and he said, "**No,**" and she said, "**No,**" and neither of them said anything else about it.

## And at about half past three on the Wednesday afternoon a woman read forty-one dates back to him in the right order, and he said thank you

He asked her to read the board.

He had wanted the notebook read back and the notebook had been read back and it was perfect, and then he stood up and went to the board and put his hand flat on the clean part of it, below the last row, and asked her to read it.

She read it. She started at the top of the second column and went down it, one row at a time, slowly, and she did not stop and she did not summarise and she did not ask him about a single third-column entry because she was reading the dates.

The eighteenth of June is on the twenty-second row. The nineteenth of June is on the twenty-third row.

She read all forty-one and put the pen down on the ledge under the board and looked at the middle distance at about the machines for a second, and then she said the last two of them again, on her own, in order, the way you would say a thing to be sure of it.

"**Eighteenth. Nineteenth,**" said Helen Venn.

"**Thank you,**" said Elias Venn.
