# Chapter 0102 — The Box On Page One

**On Thursday the third of June 2025 a man of thirty-two sat in a chair that was not his in a flood claims office on the second floor of a building on Wharf Street and was told, by a woman of about thirty-four with a duty and a nine-year-old clean copy of a two-page instruction in a plastic wallet, that about four hundred adjusters in this city have been carrying that instruction since about 2011 and that not one of them has read page one.**

**And the reason he was in the room is that a woman of sixty-one who lives over a shop in Fisher Lane has had a claim since December that has not been settled, and that on Tuesday she had received a second letter about it from a different department of the same office, and she did not know it was the same water, and neither did anybody else in her building, and the letter has a form in it with a box on page one and the box is the whole of this chapter and the whole of the next nine weeks.**

---

## And the office is the one everybody in this city sends a photograph of a kitchen to, and nobody in it has ever sent one of a form

Marianne Cope had been a flood claims handler for nine years and had the instruction in a plastic wallet because the original had been folded in half four times in a decade and had gone soft at the creases, and she kept a clean copy because she was the person whose job it was to know what was on the two pages, and she said that in the first nine seconds, before anybody had asked her anything, and she said it as a fact and not as a complaint.

"**You have a photograph of somebody's kitchen,**" she said. "**I know you have, because that is what comes in, eleven or twelve a day in June and about four a day in February. Kitchen, hallway, the corner of a bedroom where the boxes were.**"

"**I have not got anything.**"

"**No. And I am going to be straight with you about why that matters to you and it is not a favour. You are not the policyholder, and you are not the claimant, and you are not an adjuster, and you do not work for the company that wrote the policy, and there is no return in this office that a man in a coat can send in. That is not me being unkind. That is the single most useful thing I can tell you this morning, because you have been walking into rooms for about six months and being told that you may look at a piece of paper, and I would like you to know how little of that is available to you by right.**"

He had come with nothing. He had a notebook in his coat and the notebook was not evidence of anything and there was no form anywhere in this city on which a man in a coat is a party.

"**Then I am asking as a person and not as anything,**" he said.

"**You can ask me as a person. I will answer what I am allowed to answer and I will tell you which parts those are, and I will do it in about nine seconds, and then you are going to be disappointed and you are not going to be able to do one thing about it, and I have had that conversation about four hundred times.**"

---

## And the instruction is two pages, and page one is boxes, and page two is the schedule, and the copy in the plastic wallet is the cleanest one in the country

She put it on the desk face down, which he noticed, and then turned it over, which she also noticed him notice.

> **FLOOD CLAIMS — INSTRUCTION TO HANDLING STAFF AND ADJUSTERS**
> **Issued 2011. Two pages. Page 1 of 2.**
> **This instruction does not replace the policy. Where this instruction and the policy differ, the policy governs. Completed claim forms are not to be held pending further instruction.**
>
> **1. CLAIM NOTIFICATION AND OPENING**
> **2. CLAIMANT DETAILS (name of claimant, address of claimant)**
> **3. POLICY AND PERIOD OF COVER**
> **4. **DATE OF LOSS** (mandatory — see Part 5, section D)**
> **5. BASIS OF SETTLEMENT**
> **6. EXCESS**
> **7. CONTENTS — itemised schedule, or election under section 5**
> **8. PAYMENT**
> **9. CLOSURE**

"**Four hundred and eleven adjusters, contractors and loss adjusters' clerks in this city were issued that between 2011 and last November,**" said Marianne Cope. "**I know the number because I send the wallets out. I am the person who sends the wallets out. There are eleven of us in this office and I am the one who has the current print and the archive of the previous four.**"

"**And how many have read page one.**"

"**I do not know. I have asked four.**"

"**You have asked four and you do not know.**"

"**I have asked four and two of them told me the truth and two of them told me they had read it and one of those two was telling the truth about something else, and I have stopped asking, and I have started doing something else instead, which is I have started a note at the back of my own copy, in pencil, that says which box the adjuster read out of the form to me on the telephone. There are thirty-one lines in it. It is not a record. It is in pencil in the back of a plastic wallet and if I leave this firm it goes in a bin.**"

---

## And the box is in the position where a name is in a postman's form, and it is mandatory, and it has a cross-reference to a page nobody opens

Item four is two-thirds of the way down a column of nine, between the policy and the basis of settlement, and it is in brackets and it is mandatory and it points forward to a page.

"**It is the fourth box down,**" said Elias Venn, "**and it is the only one of the nine with anything in it that is not a name, a number, or a date you would put in anyway. The other eight I would have filled in without reading the sheet.**"

"**Then you have understood the instruction and I have not got to do the rest of it.**" She turned the page over. "**Page two. Section five is the basis of settlement and it gives two. One is an itemised schedule, where you prove what each thing was worth and you are paid up to the sum insured. The other is an excess settlement, where we pay you the excess and you give up the itemised claim and nobody establishes what was in the house.**"

"**Which one do people take.**"

"**Excess. Almost all of them, because almost none of them can prove what a wedding present was worth in 2011.**"

"**And how much is an excess.**"

"**A flat sum. Per event. On a schedule.**" She had not said the number and he did not ask for it. "**And here is the part I am going to read you and then I am going to stop reading, because it is printed and I have read it and I am not going to stand here and be the person who says it in her own words four months before the argument has been had with the people who have to have it.**"

> **PART 5. EXCESS SETTLEMENT — SCHEDULE AND ATTACHMENT**
> **A.** An excess is payable **once in respect of each household** in respect of each event.
> **B.** The household for the purposes of A is the policyholder **at an address on the date of loss**.
> **C.** An excess is attached to an event by reference to a date and not otherwise.
> **D.** **Where the date of loss is not evidenced, the settlement cannot be attached and the claim is referred.**

"**That is the whole of it,**" said Marianne Cope. "**Three letters, a sentence, and a cross-reference from page one to page two. It has been in that form since 2011 and it was drafted by somebody who was extremely good at their job, and I have never in nine years known an adjuster to read section B, and I have read it about four hundred times because I am the one who sends the wallets out.**"

He looked at it for a while.

"**What is the box for.**"

"**No.**"

"**You have just read me the sentence that says what it is for.**"

"**No, and I am going to tell you the reason and then you are going to be annoyed with me, and the reason is the truest thing anybody in this office will say to you this year.**" She put her hand flat on the plastic wallet. "**The form answers what the field is. You are asking what it is for. And nobody knows. I have asked two people who used to be senior to me and both of them are gone, and one of them told me it was for the audit trail and the other one told me it was for the statistics, and I have been doing this for nine years and I am telling you that they were guessing, and they were guessing in a room where I was junior, which is how you guess.**"

"**That is the second time in six months somebody has said a version of that sentence to me.**"

"**Then somebody in your case has been in a room like this one, and I hope it was somebody competent, and I am sorry for whoever it was.**"

---

## And about nine hundred claims came in after the water in December, and they are all sitting in a box, and she put the number on the table and would not put a person near it

"**Nine hundred and four,**" said Marianne Cope, "**opened between the ninth and the nineteenth of December. That is the count of claims and it is exact and it is a count of claims, not of households and not of people, and I have been asked twice this month to turn it into one of the other two and both times I said no and both times I was right.**"

"**How many are waiting on the box.**"

"**I am not going to give you that number either and I want you to understand that it is a professional judgement and not modesty. If I tell you nine hundred out of nine hundred, then every person who reads what you write will believe there is one thing wrong with nine hundred claims, and there is not. There are nine hundred claims and there are about nine hundred different reasons and one of the reasons is a box on page one and about forty of them are a box on page one.**"

"**Forty.**"

"**Forty is my number and my number is pencil in the back of a plastic wallet.**"

---

## And the reason there is a second letter in the world at all is that the first one was never completed, and the woman who got the second letter has been in a flood once

The letter came on Thursday the third of June to a first-floor flat above a shop in Fisher Lane, and it is four paragraphs, and it is signed by a department that is not the department that sent the first letter, and it says the word *enclosed* once.

Kathleen Orrey was sixty-one and had run a stationery and newsagent's for nineteen years and had a claim in since December for the water that came into the ground floor and up to the second stair in the second week of December, and it had not been settled, and she had been sent a form, and she had sent it back, and the form had come back.

"**I have done everything you have asked me to do twice,**" she said, **on the telephone, to Elias Venn, on Wednesday the fourth, at about eleven in the morning, and she was not complaining, which was the thing he noticed.** "**I filled it in. I sent it back with photographs of the shop. They sent it back and they said it was incomplete. So I filled it in again and I sent it back again, and this time somebody rang me and was very nice about it and said it had gone to a different team because of the deadline.**"

"**And you thought the second letter was about June.**"

"**I thought it was about the water that is coming, because the first page of it has a period on it and the period is in June, and I have not had June yet.**" She was quiet for a second. "**I have had December, though. I have had December three times.**"

"**Mrs Orrey, can I ask you what is in the box on page one of the first form. The fourth box down.**"

"**The date it happened, love. I wrote the ninth. It was the ninth. I have a photograph of my own front step with the date on the camera on it and I sent that as well.**"

"**And what came back.**"

"**They said it could not be accepted from the photographs and that it had to be evidenced by a document, and I have been to three shops and I have looked at my bank and my rent book and I have found a letter, I have found a letter, and I have sent it, and I have had nothing back and now I have had another letter about June.**" She said the last part in a voice that had gone up half a tone and came back down. "**I am not being difficult. I want to say that first. I have never been difficult about a piece of paper in my life. Somebody has to ask and I answer.**"

"**I know. That is why I am asking you and not somebody with a stamp.**"

"**Then ask.**"

"**What did you put in the box, in the end, the second time.**"

There was a pause of about four seconds.

"**I left it out,**" said Kathleen Orrey. "**Because after the photograph they told me it had to be a document, and I sent the letter, and then they told me the letter was not the right kind of letter, and I have not got the right kind of letter, and I did not want to write something in a box that was not true, so I left it out and I sent it anyway.**"

---

## And the first form came back on the fourth of June with four lines on it, and the fourth line is a cross-reference, and the person who typed it has not read the page it points to either

He had the scan on his phone because she had photographed it in the shop and sent it to him at about eleven twenty and she had asked him twice whether it was all right to send it to a stranger, and he had told her twice that it was, and it was, because it was her own claim and her own document.

> **Your claim reference: DL/24/10207**
> **We have received your claim form dated 12 December 2024 and your supporting correspondence.**
> **Item 4 of the form (DATE OF LOSS) is blank.**
> **Item 4 is mandatory. It is cross-referenced at Part 5, section D of the instruction to adjusters, a copy of which we enclose.**
> **Unless we receive the item, this claim cannot be assessed and no payment can be made on it. If you believe the item has been completed, please contact the handling team quoting the reference above. Your claim will remain open for thirty days from the date of this letter and will then be closed, and may be reopened on production of the item.**

"**They have sent her the instruction,**" he said. "**They have sent her the two pages, because the form points at page two, and page one is in the same wallet.**"

"**They send it to everybody,**" said Marianne Cope, on the telephone at about ten past four on the fourth of June, when he rang her back and asked her one question and got the same answer she had given him twice. "**It is in the standard enclosure list. I have never known a claimant to read it and I have sent it to about two hundred and fifty people since December, and I have asked four of them what section D says and three of them told me it was about the audit.**"

---

## And in a stairwell on the fourth floor of a building on a hill a registrar of twenty-eight had read page one, and had done it on the Wednesday, in about an hour, and had not been asked to

She had not come to the office and she was never going to come to the office, and she was in a stairwell because her own office was on the fourth floor and the stairwell was where people at that building talk.

"**You rang me on Wednesday morning in one sentence and I have not asked you why and I am not going to.**"

"**I did not expect you to look at it.**"

"**I looked at it on Wednesday night in about an hour and I have done nothing else since, and I am going to tell you the two things I found and then I am going to go and do something else, because I have nine hundred of your floods in front of me and about forty of them are not a Museum problem and I have to get through them.**"

"**Go on.**"

"**One. It is good.**" She said it flatly. "**It is good work. Whoever drafted it in 2011 understood the problem completely and wrote it in the shortest possible words and put a cross-reference where a cross-reference belongs, and section B is one line and it is the best sentence anybody in this case has read in twenty-six weeks. I am not being generous. I have been reading bad drafting in a professional capacity for five years and I know what good drafting looks like and that is it.**"

"**And two.**"

"**And two, there is nothing on either page that asks how many people were in the house.**" She did not raise her voice and she did not slow down. "**Not on page one and not on page two. There is a box for a date, a box for a policy, a box for a name, and nothing whatever for whether there was a person in a building on a night. And I have looked, and I have read it twice, and I want that on the record somewhere that is not a public document, because I am a registrar and that is the first time in five years I have read a professional instrument and found a hole in it that is not a matter of taste.**"

"**That is a different thing from the box.**"

"**It is a completely different thing and it is on the same two pages, and I have not worked out yet whether that is because nobody minded or because somebody did and thought it was somebody else's page.**"

"**Can you do anything with it.**"

"**I can do one thing and I am about to do it and I am telling you that I have done it.**" A door opened somewhere below them. "**I have written to the instruction's issuing office. I have asked them for a copy of the consultation that produced section B, or failing that for the drafting history, or failing that for the name of the person who signed it off. I have put my job title on the letter and I have not put your name anywhere on it, and it is dated today, and it will be answered in about four weeks because that is how long they take, and I am telling you the number of weeks now so that you cannot go and count.**"

"**Thank you.**"

"**Do not do that. There is nothing on that letter and it is not a favour and I have not done it for you.**" She started down. "**And I have read page one, and I know what is on it, and I am not going to tell you what I think it is for either, because the person who owns that is a woman on the second floor of a building on Wharf Street who has done nothing wrong and has been doing it for nine years, and if I say it first she will have spent the rest of the afternoon being told by a registrar from a hill that her own instruction does not do what she thought it did, and I have not got the standing and neither have you.**"

---

> **Fri 4 Jun, 23:30. Twenty-five weeks and five days since the sixth of December. The practice is twenty-one weeks and four days old. The bell has said nothing for a hundred and seventy-three days and the panel on its case is a hundred and eighty-nine days old.**
> **A flood claim is settled against a date of loss. A date of loss is a box on page one of a two-page instruction that has been in a plastic wallet in about four hundred places in this city since 2011. I have now read both pages, both of them on the Thursday, from a clean copy, in a chair that was not mine. I have four lines of it in this book and not the whole of it, and I am not going to write the rest of it out here, because I wrote down nine numbers in a room in May and one of them was wrong, and the rule at the top of this first page is do not write it down, and I have broken that rule once already in public and I am not going to break it again for a thing I can go and look at in an office on Wharf Street whenever the office is open.**
> **Here is what I am allowed to say, and I have checked it against what a woman of thirty-four said to me twice in one day and I am not improving on her.** Page one is boxes. Page two is a schedule and four letters. The fourth box down is a date and it is mandatory and it points forward. And the last of the four letters says that where the date is not evidenced, the settlement cannot be attached and the claim is referred. That is all it says. That is the entire mechanism and it is printed on a sheet of paper and it has been there since 2011.
> **And a woman of thirty-four with nine years in that office said the thing about the wrong question, and she said it before I got there, and I have now heard it three times in six months and the third time was in a room with a plastic wallet on the table. The form answers what the field is. You are asking what it is for. Nobody knows.**
> **And she is right about the box. I can see that she is right about the box from where I sat, and she said she could not do one single thing about it, and I believe her, and I have believed her twice, and the second time she read me a printed sentence and then would not say it in her own words and told me why, and the reason was that she has not got the standing and neither have I, and I have written that down because the first time somebody has told me the correct reason for a refusal in twenty-five weeks, and the correct reason is always worse than the rude one.**
> **And a woman of sixty-one over a shop in Fisher Lane has had a claim since December that has not been settled, and she has done everything this office has asked her to do twice, and on the third of June she got a second letter about a flood that has not happened yet, and the first page of it has June on it, and she has not had June, she has had December three times. And she filled a box in once with a date that was right, and it was refused because it was in a photograph, and then she filled it in with a document, and the document was not the right kind of document, and in the end she left it out rather than write something in it that was not true, and sent it anyway. That is the whole of the course of events and it is eleven weeks long and it has not cost her one pound and it has cost her everything a pound is.**
> **There is no box on either page for how many people were in the house. A registrar of twenty-eight read both pages on Wednesday night in about an hour and found that, and wrote to the office that issues the instruction today, and put no name of mine anywhere on it, and told me in a stairwell that it is not a favour and there is nothing on the letter. I asked her whether she could do anything with the box and she said yes and she told me the number of weeks before I could go and count, which is a thing nobody has ever done for me and which I am going to think about for a while.**
> **The bill goes out. The name comes back.**
> **What a date of loss is for. Asked in an office on Wharf Street on Thursday and refused twice by somebody competent, and the refusal is the same sentence a records officer refused me in a label room in April, and I have now been told the wrong question three times by three people with duties and I am beginning to think the wrong question is the only question anybody in this city can currently ask.**
> **What a legal neighbour is. And what a claim is, which is a piece of paper with a box in it, and a household that cannot fill the box in is not a household that has lost something, it is a household that has never been one, as far as a schedule on page two is concerned.**
> **And who keeps it. Nobody. A woman of thirty-four has forty in pencil in the back of a plastic wallet and it goes in a bin when she leaves. A registrar of twenty-eight has put four weeks on the clock out loud so that nobody can count. A woman of sixty-one has a claim reference and a box she will not fill in with something untrue, and a second letter about June, and she is owed about nine hundred and four claims' worth of attention and she has got one telephone call a fortnight from a man with no post.**
